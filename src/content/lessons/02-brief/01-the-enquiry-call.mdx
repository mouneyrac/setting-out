A landscaper's phone rings. Ten minutes later they either book a site visit or
they do not, and the caller usually has no idea a decision was made.

That call is not small talk. A site visit, a measure-up, a concept sketch and a
priced quote is somewhere between half a day and two days of unpaid work. No
sensible contractor spends that on every enquiry, so they spend ten minutes
working out which enquiries are real. Across the Perth trade you will hear
figures like half of all enquiries never becoming a quote. That is not rudeness.
It is arithmetic.

You are your own client, so nobody is going to ring you and ask these questions.
You have to ask them of yourself, out loud, and write the answers down. It is the
cheapest hour of the entire project.

## The ten questions, and what each one is really testing

| # | The question | What it is actually testing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | What's the address? | Suburb tells them soil, likely limestone depth, block era, council, travel time and tip runs |
| 2 | What's the access like? | Side gate width, verge, slope. This is the number one hidden cost multiplier |
| 3 | What's prompting this now? | The real driver behind the job, which is rarely the thing first mentioned |
| 4 | What's there now, and what's staying? | Demolition, disposal and the risk of what is under the existing paving |
| 5 | What's the budget range? | Whether the job is possible at all |
| 6 | When do you want it finished? | Whether the timeline is physically and seasonally achievable |
| 7 | Who else is part of the decision? | Whether the site visit will need to be repeated |
| 8 | Have you had other quotes? | Where you are in the process, and what you have already been told |
| 9 | Do you have a design, or do you need one? | Whether this is a design job, a build job, or both |
| 10 | Is anything holding it up? | Approvals, neighbours, fences, trees, a pool, a pending sale |

### Why access is question two and not question nine

On a 1990s Perth survey-strata block the side access is often about 900 mm
between the wall and the fence, with a meter box and a downpipe in it. That one
measurement decides whether limestone blocks arrive by bobcat or by wheelbarrow,
and whether excavated sand leaves the same way. The difference is not 10 per
cent. It can double the labour on the earthworks and demolition line.

A pro asks early because it changes the quote more than the paving choice does.
Go and measure yours now: narrowest point, gate width, and the height of
anything overhead.

### Why "what's prompting this now" beats "what do you want"

Ask someone what they want and you get a materials list — decking, a firepit,
maybe some artificial turf. Ask what prompted the call and you get the actual
brief: the dog has destroyed the lawn, the neighbours built a two-storey and the
back yard lost its privacy, a toddler is about to start walking near an
unfenced drop, or the reticulation has failed and the whole garden is dying in
February.

The second answer tells you what "finished" has to achieve. The first tells you
what someone saw on Instagram. [Needs, wants and the five-year
view](/curriculum/brief/needs-wants-and-the-five-year-view/) pulls this apart
properly.

### Why the budget question is asked plainly

Amateur operators dance around money and then waste two days quoting a job that
was never affordable. Professionals ask directly, usually with a range attached:
"Most backyards like yours land between X and Y — does that sound like the right
neighbourhood?"

If the caller will not give a range, the professional has learnt something
either way. Either the number is embarrassingly low, or the caller genuinely has
no idea what landscaping costs, which is fixable, or they are price-shopping and
intend to use your quote to beat someone else's down. [The money
conversation](/curriculum/brief/the-money-conversation/) gives you the Perth
bands so you can answer this one honestly to yourself.

## What a professional does with the answers

They are making four judgements, in this order.

1. **Is it real?** A caller with an address, a driver, a rough number and a
   timeline is a project. A caller with none of those is browsing.
2. **Is it possible?** Budget against scope, timeline against season and
   approvals.
3. **Is it ours?** A firm that builds limestone retaining and paving does not
   want a native-planting-and-retic job, and will say so.
4. **Is it worth the unpaid hours?** Quoting is a real cost, carried as
   overhead, which means the quotes a firm loses are paid for by the clients it
   wins.

Note what is missing: nowhere in there is "how nice does this person sound".

## The warning signs that make a pro walk away

- **Budget refusal.** "Just give me a ballpark and we'll see." Nothing gets
  quoted off that.
- **One decider on the phone, two in the house.** The site visit will be run
  twice and the design will be reopened after the second one.
- **"We've already had three quotes."** Quote four is being used as leverage.
- **A timeline that ignores the calendar.** Someone ringing in mid-November
  wanting turf, paving and planting finished before Christmas.
- **Scope that grows during the call.** It started as a courtyard and by minute
  eight there is a pool.
- **An unresolved dispute.** A shared retaining wall, a dividing fence the
  neighbour has not agreed to, or an existing structure with no approval. See
  [dividing fences](/curriculum/approvals/dividing-fences/) and [retaining wall
  approvals](/curriculum/approvals/retaining-walls-approval/).

When you catch yourself producing one of these answers about your own project,
that is the signal — not to abandon it, but to fix that specific thing before
going further.

## The WA-specific parts of this call

**The $20,000 line.** Landscaping is explicitly named as "home building work" in
WA. Once your project crosses into permitted building work valued at $20,000 or
more, you are generally in registered-builder territory, with home indemnity
insurance attached. A Perth landscaper qualifying an enquiry is quietly working
out which side of that line the job sits on, because it changes who can contract
for it. [DIY vs subcontract](/curriculum/costing/diy-vs-subcontract/) is where
that decision gets made.

There is a recent and important exception. Since **1 July 2026**, constructing a
**Class 10a building valued under $50,000 no longer requires a registered
building contractor** in Western Australia — and Class 10a covers a great deal of
what a landscaping project actually builds: patios, pergolas, carports, sheds and
similar non-habitable structures. That is a genuine change, so do not rely on
what a tradesperson told you two years ago.

Be careful not to over-read it, though. Three separate questions are involved and
this exemption only answers the first:

| Question | What the exemption changes |
| --- | --- |
| Must a *registered building contractor* do the work? | Not for Class 10a under $50,000, from 1 July 2026 |
| Do you still need a *building permit*? | Unchanged — ask your council |
| Is *home indemnity insurance* still triggered? | Unchanged — it turns on permitted work over $20,000 |

Confirm all three with
[Building and Energy](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-or-renovating-your-home)
and your own council before you assume a structure is outside the system.
[Structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/)
works through it properly.

**The calendar.** Perth has a real build season. The winter sprinkler switch-off
runs 1 June to 31 August for scheme and bore water alike, and establishing new
lawn or garden in that window needs an approved exemption. Laying turf in
January is a watering problem; laying it in July is a permission problem. A
contractor asking "when do you want it finished" is asking a harder question
here than in Melbourne. [Bores, the roster and the winter
ban](/curriculum/approvals/bores-roster-and-the-winter-ban/) has the detail.

**What is under the existing garden.** On a renovation, the enquiry call cannot
tell anyone whether there is limestone at 400 mm, coffee rock, or a decade of
buried builder's rubble under the lawn. A good contractor says so on the phone
and prices the excavation as a provisional item. Your equivalent is [Perth
soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) and [auditing an existing
garden](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/).

## Now run the call on yourself

Sit down with the ten questions and answer them in writing, in your workbook,
the way you would answer a stranger on the phone. Short answers. No hedging on
the money one.

Then read your own answers back as if you were the contractor deciding whether
to take the job, and mark the ones that would have made you hesitate.

The next lesson, [the consultation visit](/curriculum/brief/the-consultation-visit/),
takes those answers onto the block and turns them into observations — the
structured ninety-minute walk-through that a professional runs before they will
put a single number on paper.
