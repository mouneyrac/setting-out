People do not ask for what they need. They ask for what they have seen — an
outdoor kitchen, a fire pit, a strip of artificial turf, a water feature. Those
are answers, and they arrive before anyone has asked the question.

Your job at this point in the brief is to run that in reverse. Take every item
on the wish list, find the function underneath it, and write the function down.
That is the requirement. The item is just one of several ways of delivering it,
and it is usually not the cheapest one.

Then you do the second half, which almost no homeowner does unprompted: you ask
what the property has to do in five years, not what it has to look like on
handover day.

## What a professional is doing at this stage

A designer taking a brief is building a list they can trade against a budget.
They already know that a chunk of the wish list is not going to survive the
quote, and they want those cuts to happen now, on paper, while they are cheap.
Cutting something in week three of the build costs a variation, a rescheduled
subbie and usually some already-poured concrete.

There is a second reason, and it is the one that separates competent designers
from order-takers. If you build exactly what the client asked for, they can
still hate it. That happens when the thing they actually needed was never
articulated — shade on the western wall of the living room, a path a wheelie bin
rolls down, somewhere to stand in February that is not 40°C. The wish list was
made of features. The disappointment is about function.

So the professional writes down functions, ranks them, and only then decides
what objects deliver them.

## Tracing wants back to needs

Work through your list one item at a time and ask what it is for.

| What is on the list | The function underneath | What that opens up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Built-in outdoor kitchen | Cook and eat outside without walking in and out | Shade, a power point, a tap and a flat 3 x 3 m surface. The joinery is optional. |
| New lawn across the whole yard | Somewhere soft the kids and the dog can run | A smaller lawn in the one spot that gets morning sun, and mulch or paving elsewhere |
| Big paved entertaining area | Twelve people seated at Christmas, twice a year | Check whether a 4 x 5 m paved area plus overflow onto lawn does the same job |
| Instant screening hedge | Not being overlooked from the neighbour's upstairs window | A 2.4 m screen at one specific point beats 18 m of hedge you have to clip |

The point is not to talk yourself out of nice things. It is that once the
function is written down, you can compare three ways of achieving it and pick
the one that fits the budget you set in
[the money conversation](/curriculum/brief/the-money-conversation/).

## The must-have test

Use the test the [MoSCoW method](https://www.agilebusiness.org/resource/what-is-moscow-prioritization/)
uses: what happens if this is not delivered? If the honest answer is "the garden
still works, it is just less good", it is not a must-have. Must-have is reserved
for things where there is no point doing the project without them, plus anything
legal or safety-related.

Borrow the effort split as well: no more than about 60% of effort in
must-haves, with roughly 20% held as could-haves forming the contingency pool.
Perth renovations turn up buried builder's rubble, a slab under the lawn, a
limestone wall with no footing, and 25-year-old poly retic that shatters when
you touch it. If your must-have list consumes the whole budget, the first
surprise takes something out of it.

## The five-year view

Write down who lives here in five years and what the block has to do for them.
Be specific — ages, dogs, cars, work-from-home, who mows.

- **Kids.** Today's toddler is on a bike; today's twelve-year-old has a licence
  and friends who park out the front. Play space converts to something else, so
  do not build it in permanently.
- **A pool, even a maybe.** If a pool is more than about a one-in-three
  possibility, set the barrier line now. Anything holding more than 30 cm of
  water needs a compliant barrier isolating it from the street, the neighbours
  and every habitable building, and retrofitting that line through paving and
  planting you have already paid for is where budgets die. See
  [pools and pool fencing](/curriculum/approvals/pools-and-pool-fencing/).
- **Dogs.** A dog decides your turf species, your edging detail, your gate
  hardware and whether surface drip irrigation is viable at all.
- **Ageing, yours or a parent's.** Levels are the least reversible thing in a
  garden. A continuous step-free run from the car to the door, and a main path
  wide enough for two people or a wheelbarrow — call it 1.2 m — costs almost
  nothing to design in now and a fortune to add later. Single steps are the
  worst detail in domestic landscaping; either make it a proper flight or make
  it a ramp.
- **Resale.** [REIWA ranks landscaping below](https://reiwa.com.au/the-wa-market/resources/articles/will-a-garden-add-re-sale-value-to-your-home/)
  location, home age, liveable rooms and build quality. Design for yourself, but
  do not build something a buyer will price as a liability — a large thirsty
  lawn, or 30 m of hedge that needs clipping four times a year.
  [Landscaping and property value](/curriculum/review/landscaping-and-property-value/)
  goes further into this.
- **The climate.** This is the WA-specific one. Rainfall in the south-west is
  down 15–20% since the mid-1970s, and that has cut streamflow into Perth's dams
  by about 80% — from a pre-1970s average of 420 billion litres a year to 35
  billion litres in 2025. Sprinklers are off entirely from 1 June to 31 August,
  and outside that window you get one run before 9 am or after 6 pm on your
  rostered days. Design a garden that lives on that, or less.

## Rank your decisions by how hard they are to reverse

This is the ranking that should drive how much time you spend arguing.

| Decision | Reversibility | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levels, retaining, drainage | Effectively permanent | Everything else sits on it. Getting a fall wrong puts water under the house. |
| Pool and its barrier line | Effectively permanent | Constrains gates, paving, planting and sightlines across half the block |
| Tree positions | Five to fifteen years | Water Corporation wants 2.7 m from the boundary and 0.5 m from its assets before you even discuss aesthetics |
| Paving and concrete footprint | Expensive | Demolition, spoil removal to a Perth tip, new base, new edge |
| Buried services — retic mainline, power, water points | Expensive if under paving | Cheap now, jackhammer later |
| Structures, pergolas, screens | Moderate | Footings are the sticking point |
| Plants in beds | Cheap | The forgiving part of the garden. Change your mind freely. |
| Mulch, pots, furniture, light fittings | Trivial | Do not spend brief-stage energy here |

Spend your design effort in proportion to permanence. Argue about the levels.
Do not argue about the shrubs.

## You are renovating, not starting from sand

An existing garden is a bank of assets you have already paid for, and the
cheapest way to fund a want is to keep something that already works. A mature
shade tree in the right place is worth more than anything you can buy. A sound
limestone wall, a paved area that is level and drains, a functioning mainline —
all of it counts.

The trap is the opposite error: keeping something because it exists. A path in
the wrong place, kept out of thrift, compromises the layout for twenty years.
The test is the same as everywhere else here — does it serve a function you have
written down? If not, its only value is the demolition cost you avoid, and that
is usually a small number.

You will get the evidence for these calls in
[auditing an existing garden](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/)
and [tree assessment](/curriculum/site-analysis/tree-assessment/). For now,
mark items as keep, probably-keep or unknown, and move on.
