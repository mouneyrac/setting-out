Everything in the last four lessons was collection. This one is the write-up.

A design brief is a written statement of what the garden has to do. It is not a
design, and the fastest way to write a bad one is to start describing solutions
— "a raised limestone garden bed along the western fence" instead of "screening
from the neighbour's second storey, and something that survives afternoon sun on
that side". The first sentence has decided the answer before anyone has stood on
the block in February. The second states the problem and leaves the answer open.

You are your own client here, which makes this harder, not easier. A paid
designer would push back on half of what you say. Nobody is going to do that for
you, so the document has to do it instead.

## Why a professional insists on this before drawing

Three reasons, all of them commercial.

**It makes quotes comparable.** Consumer Protection WA's advice is to get at
least three written quotes and to be clear about exactly what you want done. If
three landscapers price three different interpretations of a vague conversation,
you are not comparing prices, you are comparing guesses. The brief is what makes
them price the same job.

**It is the reference point for every variation.** Once work starts, "can we
just..." is the most expensive phrase on site. A pro measures every change
against the signed brief and prices it as a variation. Without the document,
scope creep is invisible until the invoice arrives. [Contracts and
variations](/curriculum/costing/contracts-and-variations/) covers the mechanics.

**It survives you changing your mind.** In month five, tired and over budget,
you will want to delete the thing that mattered most. The brief written in month
one, when you were thinking clearly, is the only argument against that.

## A structure you can copy

Ten sections. Write them in this order — the later ones depend on the earlier
ones. Aim for two to four pages. If it runs longer than five you are designing,
not briefing.

### 1. The property and what is already there

Address, block size, orientation, and — because you are renovating rather than
starting on bare sand — a short inventory of what stays, what goes, and what is
undecided. Two lines each on the existing lawn, the retic, the trees, the paving,
the fences and the shed. The [existing garden
audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/) fills this in
properly later; for now, record your intent.

### 2. Users and uses

Everyone who uses the space and what they do in it. Not "family of four" — how
old the kids are now, whether the dog digs, who does the maintenance, whether
anyone has mobility limits, and where you actually sit in January versus July.

### 3. Must-haves, with a reason attached

Each must-have gets a sentence saying what it is for. "Shade over the western end
of the alfresco, because it is unusable after 3pm from November to March" is
briefable. "Pergola" is not. This is the output of [needs, wants and the
five-year view](/curriculum/brief/needs-wants-and-the-five-year-view/).

### 4. Explicit exclusions

The section most people skip and the one that saves the most money. State plainly
what you are not doing: no pool, no water feature, lawn only in the rear
north-east corner, no synthetic turf, verge out of scope this stage. Every
exclusion you write down is an argument you do not have to have later.

### 5. Budget and staging

A total number, a contingency percentage, and — if the money will not cover
everything — which stage each element sits in and what must be built first so
later stages do not require demolition. Use your figures from [the money
conversation](/curriculum/brief/the-money-conversation/), and note the deposit
caps: 10 per cent for most trades, 6.5 per cent for home building work contracts
between $7,500 and $500,000.

### 6. Maintenance you will genuinely sustain

Write hours per month, not adjectives. "Low maintenance" means nothing; "two
hours a fortnight, no hedge trimming, no seasonal replanting" is a design
constraint. Be honest — this section is the one most likely to be a lie, and the
garden will find you out in about eighteen months.

### 7. Style and materials direction

Three or four reference images and a sentence about what you like in each.
Materials you will accept and materials you will not. Water Corporation's four
free Perth garden plans are useful here purely as vocabulary — being able to say
"coastal, not cottage" saves a lot of talking.

### 8. Known constraints

Services, easements, sewer, retaining, dividing fences, overlooking neighbours,
strata or covenant conditions, and council rules. Note the ones you have
confirmed and the ones you still need to check; Phase 3 and Phase 4 resolve them.

### 9. Programme

When you want it usable, and any fixed dates. Note that Perth's planting window
is autumn to early winter, so a build finishing in December means either a summer
of hand-watering or a planting delay of five months.

### 10. What failure looks like

Three specific outcomes that would make you regret the project. These are the
questions you will answer in [reviewing against the
brief](/curriculum/review/reviewing-against-the-brief/), so write them so they
can be answered yes or no.

## The WA lines a generic template will not prompt you for

| Write this into your brief | Because |
| --- | --- |
| Maximum lawn area, in square metres | Perth allows sprinklers on two rostered days a week, before 9am or after 6pm, with no sprinklers at all from 1 June to 31 August. Lawn area is a water and maintenance decision, not a look. |
| Shade as a functional requirement, by location and time of day | Afternoon western sun is the single biggest determinant of whether Perth outdoor space gets used. Brief it as performance, not as a structure. |
| A soil improvement allowance | On Swan Coastal Plain sand this is a real line item, and it is invisible in the finished garden — which is exactly why it gets cut first. |
| Scheme water, bore, or both | Changes irrigation design, running cost and plant palette. Decide before, not during. |
| Verge in or out of scope | Council rules differ. In Stirling, plants and mulch need no approval but paving, synthetic turf and in-situ concrete need an application approved first. |
| Which zones may be watered and which must survive on rainfall | This becomes your [hydrozoning](/curriculum/concept-design/hydrozoning/) plan in Phase 5. |

## Test it before you rely on it

Read it aloud. Anything you cannot say without hedging is not decided yet — mark
it open rather than leaving a vague sentence that looks decided.

Then apply the yes/no test to every line: in two years, could someone standing in
the garden say whether this was achieved? "A relaxing space" fails. "Seating for
six in shade at 4pm in January" passes.

Then hand it to someone who knows how you live. They will find the thing you have
been avoiding. That is the point.

## What happens to this document next

Phase 3 takes your brief onto the site and finds out which parts of it the ground
will actually allow. The [constraints and opportunities
plan](/curriculum/site-analysis/constraints-and-opportunities-plan/) is where the
brief meets levels, soil, services and shade — and where some of it gets rewritten.

Keep it in your workbook as a living document, dated. When you change it, change
it deliberately and note why.
