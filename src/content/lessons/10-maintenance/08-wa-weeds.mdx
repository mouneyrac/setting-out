Weeding is not one job. It is four or five different jobs that look alike, and
doing the wrong one at the wrong time of year is how a Perth garden ends up
with more weeds than it started with. Pull a soursob in October and you have
just planted a dozen bulbils. Slash a grass while it is seeding and you have
sown the bed.

On a renovation you also inherit somebody else's seed bank and somebody else's
lawn. The lawn is the bigger problem: kikuyu and couch do not respect a garden
edge, and almost every retrofitted bed in Perth loses to them unless the
barrier is built deliberately.

## What a professional does at this stage

A maintenance contractor does not "weed the garden". They identify each
recurring species, classify it by life cycle, and attack each one in its own
vulnerable window. That is the whole difference. The categories are:

| Category | Perth examples | Vulnerable window | What actually works |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Winter annuals** | Capeweed, flatweed, wintergrass, annual veldgrass | Seedling stage, weeks after the autumn break | Hand-weed or treat at three-to-five leaves; you are exhausting a seed bank |
| **Geophytes** (bulbs, corms, tubers) | Freesia, harlequin flower, gladiolus, watsonia, black flag, cape tulip, soursob, arum lily | Early in the growing season, before storage organs renew | Dig out early and completely; late removal spreads cormels and bulbils |
| **Running warm-season grasses** | Kikuyu, couch | Late spring and summer, in active growth | Barrier plus repeated systemic treatment; nothing single-shot works |
| **Woody escapees** | Japanese pepper, Victorian tea tree, lantana, morning glory | Any time, before fruit ripens | Cut and treat the stump immediately; expect suckering |

The second thing they do is check the legal list, because a few of these are
not merely unwanted.

## Declared pests: the ones you are legally required to control

Under the Biosecurity and Agriculture Management Act 2007 some plants are
**declared pests**, and
[DPIRD states plainly](https://www.dpird.wa.gov.au/businesses/pests-weeds-and-diseases/plant/declared-plants/)
that owners and occupiers are obliged to control them. The categories are C1
exclusion, C2 eradication and C3 management, and — this catches people — the
category can differ between local government areas.

Check on the
[Western Australian Organism List](https://www.dpird.wa.gov.au/online-tools/western-australian-organism-list/),
by name and for *your* LGA. Do not rely on a printed list; declarations change.

Two that turn up in suburban Perth gardens:

- **Arum lily** (*Zantedeschia aethiopica*). Planted as an ornamental, escaped
  everywhere. FloraBase records it as a
  [declared pest under s22(2) of the BAM Act 2007, banned from sale since 2006](https://florabase.dbca.wa.gov.au/browse/profile/1049),
  and present across the Swan Coastal Plain. **All parts are toxic** — gloves,
  and keep children and dogs off the cut material. The trap is that it sheds
  tiny daughter tubers whenever you hand-pull it, right through the growing
  season, so a rough dig-out reliably makes the patch bigger. Work the June to
  September window and expect two or three seasons.
- **Boneseed**. DPIRD confirms it is
  [a declared pest and a Weed of National Significance](https://www.dpird.wa.gov.au/businesses/pests-weeds-and-diseases/plant/declared-plants/boneseed/),
  with several infestations around Perth under active eradication. Report it
  rather than quietly removing it — you may be inside a mapped program.

Plenty of genuinely destructive Perth weeds are *not* declared — gazania,
freesia, black flag, morning glory, veldt daisy. No legal duty, same removal
verdict, and no WA ban on selling most of them. The responsibility is yours.

## Why kikuyu and couch get into your beds

Because they attack on two fronts. Both are **rhizomatous and stoloniferous**
at once: stolons run along the surface rooting at every node, rhizomes do the
same underground. A standard 75 to 100 mm plastic or steel garden edge stops
neither — the stolon walks over the top and the rhizome goes under.

They are also warm-season **C4** grasses, most actively growing in late spring
and summer. That is exactly when a systemic treatment translocates properly,
and exactly when most people have stopped going outside.

Three parts, and you need all three.

1. **Kill what is already in the bed first.** Building a barrier around
   established kikuyu just gives it a private enclosure. Rhizomatous grasses
   need **multiple treatments within a single season** — a few surviving
   rhizomes re-establish the whole mat.
2. **Build a physical barrier.** At Blue Gum Lake in the City of Melville,
   after repeated spraying failed to stop couch creeping in from adjoining
   turf, a crew trenched in a **one-metre-wide conveyor belt buried
   vertically** — twelve people, four hours, 100 metres, in sand. Your version
   is a root barrier trenched in vertically, deeper than any off-the-shelf
   garden edge. No Australian standard sets a depth, so dig at your own lawn
   edge, see how deep the rhizomes run, and go well below it.
3. **Give the stolon nothing to root into at the surface.** A mow strip, paved
   band or concrete kerb flush with the turf forces the runner across bare hard
   surface where you can see it and cut it. Combine both — the edge detail is in
   [mulch, edging and finishing](/curriculum/build/mulch-edging-and-finishing/).

**Solarisation** is the non-chemical option for a badly infested bed, and Perth
summers make it viable. Lay heavy-duty clear or black plastic over moist soil,
edges firmly held down, four to twelve weeks, December to February. The trapped
heat kills plants, seed and pathogens. Clear gets hotter; black also blocks
photosynthesis. Expect to spot-treat survivors.

## Dealing with weeds before they seed

This is the habit the whole lesson rests on. Every hour spent before seed set
saves several later — the City of Stirling notes a lupin seed bank in the soil
lasts **30 to 40 years**. The rules that are not obvious:

- **Slash annual grasses after the flower head emerges but before the seed is
  dry.** Too early and they flower again. Bag the heads; seed keeps ripening
  after cutting.
- **Remove geophytes early in the growing season.** Later, contractile roots
  drag the renewed corm deeper, and stem cormels and bulbils form and detach at
  a touch.
- **Do not hand-pull oxalis or soursob after bulbil formation.** You are
  propagating it. Same for gladiolus and cape tulip, which shed masses of tiny
  cormels that float in water and stick to boots.
- **Do not just pick the flowers off.** Work in south-west WA found that
  removing pink gladiolus flowers as they opened produced a *larger* replacement
  corm the following year.
- **Weed the clean areas first, then the infested ones**, cleaning boots and
  tools between. You are the main dispersal vector on your own block.

Work when the soil is moist — after rain, or the morning after a watering day.
In sand a moist pull gets the root; a dry pull snaps it off and it reshoots.
Through summer, weed early, carry water, and do not clear heavy growth alone in
February.

## If you use herbicide

Anything perennial needs a **translocated** (systemic) herbicide that reaches
the roots. Contact herbicides only burn what they touch and are for annual
seedlings. Three legal points, none optional. The
[WA Department of Health states that it is illegal to apply pesticides in a
manner not indicated on the label](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/Articles/U_Z/Using-pesticides-safely) —
read the whole label, not just the rate. Wear the protective equipment the label
specifies and do not spray on windy days. And if you **pay** someone to spray,
they must be a licensed pest management technician working for a business
registered with the Department of Health under the
[Health (Pesticide) Regulations 2011](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/Health-for/Licensing-and-industry/Pesticides).
Ask for the licence.

Two Perth cautions. On free-draining sand, residual herbicides leach out of the
root zone faster than in loam — less reliable, more likely to reach groundwater.
And near a soak, drain, wetland or the river most products are not registered
for use at all. Check the label before, not after.

## Disposal

Weeds go in the **lime-green FOGO bin**, where commercial composting
temperatures destroy seed that a backyard heap will not. Bag ripe seed heads
and fruit first.

Never dump garden refuse in bushland or on a verge. Old dumps are a documented
source of bulb-weed invasion into Perth bushland — clearing one at Shenton
Bushland took five working days and 30 cubic metres of material.
