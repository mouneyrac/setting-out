Autumn does not arrive in Perth in March. It arrives when the nights turn
dewy and the flying ants come out, which is usually April and sometimes late
May. Spring does not start in September; the wattles are nearly finished by
then and the real explosion of flower comes in October. A calendar built on
four European seasons will have you feeding in the wrong month and planting
in the worst possible one.

The Noongar people of the south-west run a six-season calendar, and it maps
onto this climate because it was built from it. As a maintenance framework it
is simply more accurate: it splits our long summer in two, treats autumn as a
working season rather than a transition, and separates the first cold rains
from the flowering spring that follows. This lesson uses it, then bolts on the
thing the calendar cannot tell you — the fixed legal dates that govern when
you are allowed to water.

## Whose knowledge this is

The six-season calendar is Noongar knowledge, held by Noongar people. Perth
sits on Whadjuk boodja, one of the language groups of the wider Noongar
nation. It is not folklore and it is not yours or mine to reinvent — use it as
it is given, and attribute it.

One thing follows practically: the seasons are read from what is happening,
not from a date. The [Bureau of Meteorology's Nyoongar
calendar](https://www.bom.gov.au/resources/indigenous-weather-knowledge/indigenous-seasonal-calendars/nyoongar-calendar)
says so explicitly — the seasons can be long or short and are indicated by
change around you rather than by dates. The month ranges below are the
Bureau's approximation, published so people can get their bearings. Spelling
varies too, because it is an oral language; Noongar, Nyoongar and Nyungar are
all in use.

## What a professional does at this stage

A maintenance contractor does not price a visit. They price a year, and they
build it the same way every time: write in the immovable dates first — set by
law, by the weather, or by a supplier's stock cycle — then slot the flexible
work into the gaps left over. Nobody competent starts with "the garden needs
feeding" and then discovers it is July.

For Perth the immovable set is short and worth learning by heart:

| Fixed constraint | The dates | What it costs you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sprinkler Switch-off | 1 June – 31 August, every year | $100 fine, bore users included |
| Watering roster | Two rostered days, once, before 9am or after 6pm | $100 on-the-spot fine |
| New lawn or garden exemption | 42 days if planted 1 Oct – 31 Mar; 35 days if planted 1 Apr – 30 Sep | Up to $500 if you breach the conditions |
| Fertiliser window | September, October, November, March, April | Nutrient leaches straight through sand |
| Firebreak and fuel load notice | Set by your local government | Council infringement, and a real fire risk |

The roster is allocated by street number, so
[look yours up](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/check-your-watering-days)
rather than assuming it matches your neighbour's. Bore users get no relief:
same two days, same hours, same winter shutdown — see
[bores, the roster and the winter ban](/curriculum/approvals/bores-roster-and-the-winter-ban/).

In a renovated garden the calendar runs on two layers. Established plants that
came through the works follow the seasons below. Anything planted in the last
two years is still on the establishment schedule from
[the first twelve weeks](/curriculum/maintenance/the-first-twelve-weeks/), which
overrides them. Mark those beds on the plan so you do not accidentally wean
them a year early.

## The six seasons as a work calendar

### Birak — first summer, December to January

Rains ease, easterlies in the morning, sea breeze in the afternoon. Nothing
gets planted and nothing gets fed.

- **Water** — controller at 90% in December, 100% in January. On sand, split
  long stations into two shorter cycles so water soaks instead of running.
- **Feed** — no. Summer feeding on sand feeds the groundwater.
- **Prune** — deadhead only. Never hard-prune into a heatwave; you expose bark
  that has never seen direct sun and it will scald.
- **Mulch** — should already be at full depth. Check it is off stems and trunks.
- **Check** — heat stress twice a week on anything planted in the last two
  years, dry patches that indicate water-repellent sand, and your fuel loads
  against your council's firebreak notice.

Working alone in 40 degrees is genuinely dangerous. Start at first light and
leave the heavy work for Djeran.

### Bunuru — second summer, February to March

The hottest, driest stretch, and when a garden shows you every mistake in its
irrigation design.

- **Water** — 100% in February, 80% in March. A patch that stays dry while
  everything around it is wet is hydrophobic sand, not a broken sprinkler;
  hand-apply a wetting agent and water it in.
- **Feed** — hold until the heat breaks. March is the first legitimate month.
- **Plant** — no. Nothing planted in Bunuru survives without babying.
- **Check** — pots and raised beds daily, sunburn on exposed trunks, and the
  uniformity of your worst station.

### Djeran — autumn, April to May

Cool dewy mornings, southerlies, flying ants, red-flowering gums. The most
productive working season of the Perth year, and the one most people waste.

- **Water** — 60% in April; manual only in May, if at all.
- **Feed** — yes. April is one of the two good windows. Slow release, at label
  rate, and low-phosphorus for anything in the Proteaceae.
- **Prune** — yes. Structural pruning, cutting back spent summer perennials,
  shaping before winter growth.
- **Plant** — **this is the window.** A plant that goes in during Djeran gets
  Makuru and Djilba rain to build roots before it ever meets a Perth summer.
  Nothing you do in spring buys that.
- **Mulch** — top up to 50–75 mm before the rain, and apply a wetting agent at
  the start of winter so the first rain penetrates rather than sheets off.
- **Check** — gutters, soakwell inlets, drainage falls and retaining wall
  weepholes, all before the first real downpour.

### Makuru — winter, June to July

Coldest and wettest. Ravens pair up, black swans arrive on the lakes. **The
sprinklers are off, by law, from 1 June to 31 August.**

- **Water** — nothing automatic. Rainwater-tank-fed systems are exempt; a newly
  planted garden needs an applied-for exemption, lodged the day it goes in.
- **Feed** — no. Fertiliser applied before heavy rain on sand ends up in the
  Swan and the Canning.
- **Prune** — the dormant window. Deciduous fruit trees, roses in July,
  structural work on anything leafless.
- **Plant** — good. Bare-rooted fruit trees and roses land in nurseries in
  July, and it is the only sensible window for relocating an established plant.
- **Check** — weeds germinating now, killed before they set seed; the
  controller's backup battery; and service the retic while it is not running.

### Djilba — first spring, August to September

Cold clear days alternating with warm wet ones. Wattles start the flowering
run. Magpies swoop.

- **Water** — still off until 31 August, then manual only in September. Resist
  turning the controller back on out of habit on 1 September; most Perth
  gardens do, and most do not need it.
- **Feed** — yes. September is the primary feeding month of the year.
- **Prune** — cut back winter-flowering shrubs as they finish.
- **Check** — **run your full retic audit now.** You may test your own system
  only on rostered days, before 9am or after 6pm, two minutes per station; a
  Waterwise Garden Irrigator can test any time. Before summer, not during it —
  that is
  [watering and the retic audit](/curriculum/maintenance/watering-and-retic-audit/).

### Kambarang — second spring, October to November

Balgas and kangaroo paws flowering, and the warming trend begins. October is
the month you are most likely to meet a snake — look before you reach into
long grass.

- **Water** — 60% in October, 80% in November.
- **Feed** — still in the window. Last feed before the shutdown.
- **Prune** — spring-flowering natives immediately after flowering, not later.
- **Mulch** — the deadline. Full depth, wetting agent applied, before Birak.
- **Check** — shade up, pots relocated, and your council's firebreak and fuel
  load requirements.

## The controller adjustment nobody makes

Six changes a year to one dial, using Water Corporation's published figures:

| Months | Seasonal adjustment |
| --- | --- |
| January – February | 100% |
| March | 80% |
| April | 60% |
| May | Manual only, if needed |
| June – August | Sprinkler Switch-off |
| September | Manual only, if needed |
| October | 60% |
| November | 80% |
| December | 90% |

The percentage works off your base run time, so a 10 minute station at 80%
runs for 8 minutes. Set the base correctly first — that depends on sprinkler
type, and it is the next lesson.

## Making it a calendar you will actually use

Phone reminders beat a laminated chart on the shed wall, because the shed
chart gets read once. Set six recurring events, one per season, each carrying
its four or five tasks, plus two hard alerts: **25 May** to wind the controller
down and finish the pre-winter work, and **28 August** to book the retic audit.
Put a copy in your maintenance manual from
[the handover phase](/curriculum/handover/the-maintenance-manual/) so it stays
with the garden if the house changes hands.

The next lesson takes the watering column out of this calendar and turns it
into a working system: seasonal run times by sprinkler type, and the annual
station-by-station audit that finds the blocked nozzle quietly killing one
corner of the garden.
