Perth sand does not hold fertiliser. On a deep grey Bassendean sand the phosphorus
retention is effectively nil, which means soluble phosphorus applied to the surface
moves down past the root zone with the next decent watering and keeps going until it
reaches the watertable. From there it travels sideways into a drain, a wetland, and
eventually the Swan or the Canning. [River Guardians](https://www.riverguardians.com/projects/phosphorous-awareness/)
puts the current load at roughly 251 tonnes of nitrogen and 26 tonnes of phosphorus a
year, against a target that requires cutting phosphorus by 46%. Fertiliser use on home
lawns and gardens is one of the sources that project sets out to reduce.

So the Perth rule is the opposite of the instinct. Less product, applied more often,
timed to when the plant is actually growing, and never in the days before heavy rain.

The second half of this lesson is about killing plants rather than rivers. A large part
of the WA palette — the Proteaceae: banksia, grevillea, hakea, dryandra, isopogon,
adenanthos — evolved on some of the most phosphorus-impoverished soils on earth. Feed
them like a rose and they poison themselves, slowly, and you will blame the weather.

## What a professional does at this stage

A maintenance contractor worth paying does four things a homeowner almost never does.

**They test before they feed.** A soil test for Colwell phosphorus and Phosphorus
Retention Index tells you whether phosphorus is needed at all. The
[Fertilise Wise BMP recommendations](https://www.sercul.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Nutrient-Recommendations-2025.pdf)
give the rule flat: a PRI of zero or negative means do not apply phosphorus. On a
low-PRI sand with a Colwell reading above about 7 ppm, more phosphorus is money thrown
at the groundwater. Twenty-five years of blood and bone in an established Perth bed
usually means you are already there.

**They buy nutrient, not bags.** A 25 kg bag at 10% nitrogen delivers 2.5 kg of
nitrogen. The same money in a 15% product delivers 3.75 kg. Rates in this lesson are
grams of product per square metre for a stated analysis — read the label, do the
arithmetic, and stop measuring in handfuls.

**They feed by zone, not by garden.** The feeding map is the same as your
[hydrozone map](/curriculum/concept-design/hydrozoning/): lawn, exotic beds, native beds,
and vegetables all get different products on different months.

**They put it in the calendar.** Fertilising that happens when someone remembers happens
in the wrong month. It belongs in [the Perth year](/curriculum/maintenance/the-perth-year/).

## Why your soil group changes the answer

Fertilise Wise splits the metro area into five soil groups. Map them onto the dune
systems from [Perth soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/).

| Fertilise Wise group | Roughly | pH | Phosphorus retention | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Central Coastal Plain | Bassendean grey sand | 4.0–6.0 | Extremely low | Everything leaches. Lowest rates, highest frequency, controlled release only |
| Western Coastal Plain | Spearwood / Karrakatta, sand over limestone | Neutral surface | Moderate | Watch iron, manganese, zinc and boron — alkalinity locks them up |
| Coastal | Quindalup, limestone beach sand | 8.5–9.5 | Moderate | Strongly alkaline, alkaline bore water, trace element deficiencies are the norm |
| Eastern Coastal Plain | Along the Swan and Canning, at the scarp base | Acid–neutral | Low to high | Varies within one block; test rather than assume |
| Scarp | Foothills and Darling Range | 5.0–7.0 | High | Nutrient loss is by erosion, not leaching. Different problem entirely |

## The law already narrowed your options

Most Perth gardeners do not know that WA regulates what can be sold in a fertiliser bag.
Since 1 January 2013, under the
[Environmental Protection (Packaged Fertiliser) Regulations 2010](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/law_s43219.html):

| Product | Maximum phosphorus |
| --- | --- |
| Garden fertiliser | 2% by weight |
| Lawn fertiliser | 1% by weight |
| All purpose fertiliser | 1% by weight |

Plus: where a product contains nitrogen and more than 0.5% phosphorus, the N:P ratio
must sit between 5:1 and 20:1, and the phosphorus percentage must be printed on the
label. It applies to containers up to 50 L or 50 kg. Penalty $5,000 per offence.

Three things follow. First, you can trust the label to state the phosphorus number —
read it. Second, a 2% phosphorus "garden" fertiliser is legal and is still far too much
for a sensitive banksia. Third, and most important, **raw animal manure, unprocessed
vegetable matter and mulch are not fertiliser under these regulations and carry no cap
at all.** The over-phosphorus problems on Perth blocks almost always come from a trailer
of chook manure, not a bag.

## The phosphorus trap

Phosphorus-sensitive natives are non-mycorrhizal and use cluster roots to scavenge
phosphate from soil that has almost none. The cost of being that efficient is that they
have very little ability to switch uptake off when phosphorus is abundant
([Lambers et al. 2013](https://academic.oup.com/conphys/article/1/1/cot010/361663)).
They keep absorbing, phosphate accumulates in the leaves, and the plant poisons itself.

| Group | Typical WA examples | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proteaceae | Banksia, Grevillea, Hakea, Dryandra, Isopogon, Petrophile, Adenanthos, Lambertia | Extremely sensitive. Low-phosphorus native product only, or nothing |
| Some Fabaceae | Bossiaea, several Acacia | Sensitive. Treat as above |
| Myrtaceae | Eucalyptus, Corymbia, Melaleuca, Callistemon, Chamelaucium, Kunzea | Tolerant of low rates, but they do not need much |
| Everything else | Lawn, roses, citrus, vegetables, most exotics | Normal feeding, at Perth rates and Perth timing |

Symptoms of phosphorus toxicity are slow and easily misread: yellowing then blackened,
scorched margins on the **older** leaves first, spreading inward, followed by tip
dieback weeks or months after the feed. People blame heat or drought and water more,
which makes it worse.

There is no cure. You cannot strip phosphorus out of a bed. This is a prevention-only
problem, which is exactly why it belongs in a maintenance lesson rather than a rescue
one.

Practical rules:

- Around Proteaceae, use only a controlled-release product formulated for natives and
  labelled low in phosphorus — the concentration nurseries use for sensitive species is
  around 1.3% phosphorus, and less is fine. That is
  [what Kings Park tells you to do, species by species](https://www.bgpa.wa.gov.au/grow-it-at-home).
- Better still, feed the soil, not the plant. Coarse mulch and a light compost topping
  will keep an established banksia healthy indefinitely.
- Keep blood and bone, poultry manure, mushroom compost and "all-purpose" pellets out of
  native beds entirely, and think about where the retic overspray from the lawn zone lands.
- Do not plant Proteaceae straight into an old rose bed, an old vegetable patch, or the
  strip where the chook pen was. That soil is loaded, and the plant will fail in year two.
- Phosphite, the dieback treatment, is not phosphate. It is a different compound and does
  not cause phosphorus toxicity — see the
  [UWA SPICE fact sheet](https://www.uwa.edu.au/study/-/media/Faculties/Science/Docs/Supersensitive-plants.pdf).

## Soluble, slow release, controlled release

| Type | How it releases | On Perth sand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soluble / liquid | Immediately plant-available | Mostly past the root zone within one or two waterings. Useful only as a targeted, small, in-season correction |
| Slow release (organic, pelletised) | Microbial breakdown, driven by moisture and warmth | Reasonable. Release accelerates in summer when the soil is hot and wet |
| Controlled release (polymer coated) | Diffusion through a coating, governed by temperature | The default choice. Rated in months, so one application covers a growth period |

One Perth caveat on controlled release: the month ratings on the bag are set at around
21°C. A February soil under full sun runs far hotter, so a "six month" prill can be spent
in three. Assume the shorter figure and plan the next feed accordingly.

## Rates and timing

[Fertilise Wise](https://www.sercul.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/FertiliseWise-BrochureALL.pdf)
publishes maximum rates for lawn: **25 g per square metre** of a complete
fertiliser (roughly 10–12% N, 0–1% P, 6–10% K), or **12 g per square metre** of a
nitrogen-only product above 20% N. As a sanity check, an adult hand holds about 40 to 50
grams — so a handful covers two to four square metres, not one plant.

The published calendar is two applications in spring and two in early autumn —
September, October, November, March and April — and none in summer or winter. Summer
fertilising drives growth that then demands more water; winter fertilising is washed
into stormwater drains and groundwater before the grass can use it. Couch and buffalo may
be dormant in winter anyway.

The [Water Corporation](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/lawn-care/how-to-keep-your-lawn-healthy-during-winter)
and [Be Groundwater Wise](https://begroundwaterwise.wa.gov.au/waterwise-gardens-lawns-and-verges/)
allow more — every two months through the warm months and once or twice in winter with a
potassium-and-iron product. Both positions are defensible; they are optimising for
different things. Resolve it like this: default to the spring-and-autumn calendar, and if
you feed outside it, use a genuine controlled release at half rate and never within 48
hours of forecast heavy rain.

Two more things that matter more than the product:

- **Apply a wetting agent when you fertilise.** On water-repellent sand, fertiliser
  washed off dry patches goes straight to the drain. See
  [turning Perth sand into soil](/curriculum/build/turning-perth-sand-into-soil/).
- **Water it in, lightly, straight away** — but remember the
  [winter sprinkler switch-off](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/winter-sprinkler-switch-off)
  runs 1 June to 31 August and applies to bores as well as scheme water, with a $100
  fine. If you cannot water it in, do not apply it.

## Handling

This is low-risk work, but not no-risk. Wear gloves and eye protection with granular
products, do not spread in wind, and sweep spilled granules off paving and driveways back
onto the bed rather than hosing them — hosed granules go directly into the stormwater
system. Blood and bone attracts dogs, and a dog that digs up and gorges on it can end up
at the vet. Store bags sealed, dry and off the ground; high-nitrogen products are
oxidisers and do not belong next to fuel or oily rags in the shed.

## What this changes downstream

Your fertilising programme is now three lines per zone in the maintenance calendar:
product, rate in grams per square metre, and months. Record the soil test result beside
them so that in three years you can test again and see whether phosphorus is still
unnecessary — it usually is. Lawn gets its own detailed treatment in
[lawn care through the Perth year](/curriculum/maintenance/lawn-care-through-the-perth-year/),
and if your beds are dry in patches despite the retic running, that is a
[watering and retic audit](/curriculum/maintenance/watering-and-retic-audit/) problem,
not a feeding one.

The next lesson is pruning, which is the other half of the same decision: you prune to
set up the flush of growth, and you feed to support it. Getting the order and the month
right is most of the result.
