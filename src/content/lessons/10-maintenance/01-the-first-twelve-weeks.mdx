The build is finished and the risk window has just opened. Everything you spent on
plants, turf, soil and irrigation is now sitting in sand that holds almost nothing,
attached to root systems that are still entirely inside the pots they arrived in.

Establishment watering is a different job from ongoing watering. Ongoing watering
keeps a spread root system topped up. Establishment watering keeps a root ball wet
long enough for roots to leave it — which in Perth sand means frequent, shallow and
targeted, then progressively less frequent and deeper, on a schedule that steps down
over about six weeks. Run an established garden's programme on a new one and you lose
plants in a fortnight. Run an establishment programme forever and you rot them.

If you are reading this in August, note that you are inside the Sprinkler Switch-off.
Sprinklers are banned across Perth, Mandurah and parts of the South West from
[1 June to 31 August every year](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/winter-sprinkler-switch-off),
bore users included, with a $100 fine. An exemption is the only lawful way to run
sprinklers on a new garden in that window.

## What a professional does at this stage

They price a twelve-week establishment period into the job and they show up for it.
Typically a visit at week 1, week 2, week 4, week 8 and week 12 — not to admire the
work, but to catch the three failures that kill new gardens: a station that is not
actually running, a root ball that has gone hydrophobic inside a wet bed, and mulch
that has crept up against a stem.

Three habits are worth copying.

**They set the controller and write it down.** The programme goes into the
[maintenance manual](/curriculum/handover/the-maintenance-manual/) with station
numbers, run times in minutes, and the date each step-down happens. A schedule that
lives only in someone's head does not survive a holiday.

**They diarise the exemption expiry.** The day the exemption ends is the most
dangerous day in the garden's life, and it arrives with no warning from anyone.

**They replace failures at week 8, not month 14.** A replacement planted in October
is fighting for its life. The same plant put in during the establishment period is
just another plant on the schedule.

## The clock started on installation day

[Apply for the watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
on the day the garden went in — the period runs from installation, not approval.
Breaching its conditions risks a $500 fine, and inspections are random.

| Installed | Exemption | Days 1–10 | Days 11–20 | Days 21–28 | Days 29–end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Oct – 31 Mar | 42 days | 3.5 mm, 3× daily | 5 mm, 2× daily | 10 mm daily | 10 mm every second day |
| 1 Apr – 30 Sep | 35 days | 4 mm, 2× daily (to day 14) | 7.5 mm daily (days 15–28) | — | 7.5 mm every second day |

Summer times are before 9am, noon and 3pm; autumn and winter are before 9am and 2pm.
If your water comes from a bore, Water Corporation will not process this — ring the
[Department of Water and Environmental Regulation](https://begroundwaterwise.wa.gov.au/garden-bore-regulation/)
on 1800 508 885.

### Turning millimetres into minutes

The schedule is in millimetres. Your controller is in minutes. Use the
[published application rates](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/how-long-should-i-run-my-sprinklers-for)
to convert, per station, and write the answer on the controller door.

| Emitter | Rate | 3.5 mm | 10 mm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pop-up spray, micro spray | 35–45 mm/h | ~5 min | 13–16 min |
| Gear drive rotary | 10–20 mm/h | ~13 min | 30–40 min |
| Rotary | 10–15 mm/h | ~17 min | 40–60 min |
| Dripline | 15–20 mm/h | ~12 min | 30–40 min |

Then verify it once with catch cups, because a table describes a system at correct
pressure and yours may not be — see
[irrigation scheduling and uniformity](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/).
On sand over a hard layer, or on any slope, split the 10 mm into two shorter cycles
about an hour apart rather than one long run.

## Weeks 6 to 12: the cliff

The exemption ends and the garden drops from daily watering to
[two rostered days a week](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/why-you-need-to-adjust-your-irrigation),
once each day, before 9am or after 6pm. That is roughly an 80% cut in frequency,
delivered to plants that are nowhere near self-sufficient. This is where the plant
budget actually dies — not in week 2, when everyone is paying attention.

Two things carry you across it.

**Seasonal adjustment.** Set the controller's percentage to the published Perth
figure and change it monthly: 60% in October, 80% in November, 90% in December, 100%
in January and February, 80% in March, 60% in April.

**Hand watering.** A watering can or hand-held hose is
[permitted on any day of the week](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/Help-and-advice/Waterwise/Garden/Tips-for-hand-watering),
including during the winter ban. Through the first summer, every tree and larger
shrub gets one deliberate deep soak a week: a slow bucket at the edge of the root
ball, not a spray over the leaves. Two summers for anything 45 litres and up. It is
ten minutes a week and it is the whole difference.

Apply a wetting agent
[in early summer and again at the start of winter](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/Help-and-advice/Waterwise/Garden/Improving-your-soil),
watered in until it foams. A hydrophobic root ball in a wet bed is the classic Perth
death: the plant dies of thirst in ground that looks perfectly watered.

## The weekly walk

Ten minutes, same time each week, for the first month, then fortnightly. Look at
plants, not at the garden.

The most useful question is not what is wrong with this plant, it is **how the damage
is distributed**. If several unrelated species in one area show the same symptom, it
is the irrigation or the soil. If one species is affected everywhere, it is that
species or a pest. That distinction comes from the
[Extension Gardener diagnostic method](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/extension-gardener-handbook/7-diagnostics)
and it will save you from treating a solenoid fault with fungicide.

| What you see | Most likely cause | Do this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole zone wilting together | Station not running: controller, solenoid, or a closed valve | Run each station manually and stand in it |
| One plant wilting, neighbours fine | Dry root ball, blocked emitter, or planted too deep | Dig 100 mm beside it and feel the root ball |
| Wilting in obviously wet soil | Overwatered, or planted too deep — the symptoms are identical | Reduce frequency, check the crown height |
| Brown crisp leaf margins | Underwatering, or salt from bore water | Deep hand water, check bore salinity |
| Yellow new growth, green veins | Iron or manganese lock-up on alkaline sand | Note it, treat in [fertilising Perth sand](/curriculum/maintenance/fertilising-perth-sand/) |
| Water beading on the mulch surface | Soil repellency | Wetting agent, watered in until it foams |

Judge moisture with your fingers, not your eyes. Mulch stays dark long after the sand
under it has dried out.

## Mulch: off the stems, and topped up

Mulch is [5 to 10 cm of coarse material, kept clear of the base of every new plant and tree](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/garden/creating-a-waterwise-verge),
and checked every few months so it stays at that depth as it breaks down and settles.

Mulch piled against a stem holds moisture against bark that is not built for it. The
result is collar rot, and it kills the plant from the base while the canopy still
looks fine. Pull a clear ring back by hand around every stem on your first walk —
it is the cheapest ten minutes in this lesson. Full detail in
[mulch, edging and finishing](/curriculum/build/mulch-edging-and-finishing/).

## Replacing failures early, and in season

Set a hard decision date at **week 8**. Walk the garden with the planting plan and
mark every plant that is dead, or that has not put on a single piece of new growth.
Those get replaced now, while there is still season left, rather than being left as a
gap you fill next spring at twice the cost and half the survival rate.

Before you reorder, apply one rule: **if more than about one in ten of a single
species has failed, it is not bad luck.** It is the species, the position, or the
station it sits on. Replacing like for like just buys the same failure again. Change
the species, or fix the zone, and revisit
[the planting plan](/curriculum/documentation/planting-plan-and-schedule/).

Keep the receipts. Many WA nurseries offer a replacement policy on the original tax
invoice — ask what yours is and get it in writing at purchase. If you planted a
native tree, check whether you can still claim
[Treebate](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/department-of-water-and-environmental-regulation/treebate),
worth up to $150 through the ServiceWA app.

## New turf, specifically

Turf is more demanding than beds for the first three weeks and easier afterwards.
Slow release fertiliser goes on immediately after laying, then
[a light feed of organic fertiliser 14 days after installation](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/lawn-care/establishing-a-new-lawn).
First mow when the roots have taken hold and the turf resists a gentle tug — usually
10 to 14 days in summer, 14 to 21 days in winter — with the mower set high, taking no
more than a third of the leaf. Keep foot traffic off it until after that first cut.
Beyond the starter feeds, hold off on fertiliser: SERCUL's
[Fertilise Wise](https://www.sercul.org.au/fertilisewise/) guidance for Perth sands is
to apply in September, October, November, March and April, and not to fertilise in
summer or winter. See [turf for Perth](/curriculum/build/turf-for-perth/).

## Safety

Establishment care puts you in the garden in February, usually alone. WorkSafe WA
describes the progression from heat exhaustion to
[heat stroke](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/working-safely-hot-conditions) —
confusion, speech problems, hot dry skin, body temperature above 40 degrees — and
recommends 250 ml of water every 15 to 20 minutes plus rest in the shade. Do the
weekly walk before 9am. Tell someone you are out there. And when you top up mulch or
soil, keep in mind that WA bulk soils and bagged mixes have been linked to
Legionnaires' disease: mask, gloves, open bags at ground level, wash your hands.
