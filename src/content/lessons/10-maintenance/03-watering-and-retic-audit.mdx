Most Perth reticulation is programmed once, by someone who has since moved house.
It runs the same twelve minutes in October that it runs in February. Two nozzles
have blocked, a shrub has grown in front of a third, and one solenoid has been
weeping since a winter storm. The garden does not fail all at once — a corner
goes off, then a bed, and by the third summer the owner concludes the plants were
wrong for the site.

They usually were not. The retic was.

This lesson is the routine that prevents that: one full station-by-station audit
before summer, a seasonal adjustment habit for the rest of the year, and a
controller you actually open and read.

## What a professional does at this stage

A waterwise irrigator servicing a system does two separate jobs and never mixes
them up. The first is mechanical — every head, physically, one at a time. The
second is scheduling — what the controller is told to do. Faults in the first
make the second meaningless: you cannot schedule your way out of a blocked
nozzle, you can only over-water everything else trying to compensate.

They also **time it**. The Sprinkler Switch-off ends 31 August, so 1 September is
the first legal sprinkler day of the season, and that is when the trade books
retic work. A fault found in September costs a nozzle and twenty minutes; the
same fault in January costs a dead bed and a replant, because by the time a plant
on Perth sand looks under-watered it has been dying a fortnight.

And they work from **last year's record**, because an audit compared against last
year gives you the rate of change — which is what predicts the next failure.

## The rules you are auditing inside

Getting this wrong is a fine, not an inconvenience.

| Rule | The detail |
| --- | --- |
| Roster | Two days a week in Perth and Mandurah, allocated by your address. Water once on a rostered day, before 9am or after 6pm — not both |
| Testing | Two minutes per station maximum, still only on rostered days and still inside those windows |
| Winter | No sprinklers 1 June to 31 August. Applies to bore water too. $100 fine |
| Bores | Same roster and same windows as scheme water. Licence only needed above 0.2 hectares |
| Exemptions | Waterwise Garden Irrigators are exempt from the roster and the Switch-off and can test any time |

The **two-minute allowance is for fault-finding, not for measuring**. It is
enough to see whether a station energises and whether every head pops up. It is
not enough for a catch-cup test — that is a watering event, so run it as that
day's once-per-day watering, on a rostered day, in the window.

If the renovation reaches new turf or new planting, the roster will not keep it
alive, and watering extra without a
[watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
is a breach. The exemption runs up to 42 days from 1 October to 31 March, or 35
days from 1 April to 30 September, and is applied for on installation day.

## Before you start: the parts that can hurt you

- **Heat.** Take the dawn window, not the evening one. An audit is two hours
  standing still in the open, and the end of a 40-degree Perth day is the worst
  two hours available.
- **Anything at 240 volts is not yours.** Solenoid wiring from controller to
  valve is 24 volt and safe to handle. The controller's fixed supply, a bore pump
  circuit or an outdoor outlet is licensed electrical work, and
  [in WA it is illegal for an unlicensed person to touch fixed wiring](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/electrical-safety-home/having-electrical-work-done).
  Pump work is the dangerous one: mains voltage, standing water, metal housing.
- **Valve boxes.** Redbacks live under the lids and in empty conduit. Lift the lid
  from the far side with a screwdriver, look before you reach, wear gloves.
- **Pressurised mainline.** With a master valve or a bore the mainline is live
  whenever the pump is. Isolate before you cut anything.

## The station-by-station audit

Do this in September, and do it in one session with a pen.

1. **Baseline the meter.** Everything off inside and out, isolation valve closed,
   read the meter, wait fifteen minutes, read again. Movement means a leak before
   you have run a single station. The
   [Water Corporation testing page](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/testing-your-irrigation)
   gives the sequence for isolating it to the master valve, a solenoid or the pipe.
2. **Photograph the controller as-found** — run times, start times, days,
   seasonal adjust. That is the record of how the garden was actually watered
   last summer, and you lose it the moment you change anything.
3. **Check the clock and the date.** A controller that lost time in a storm has
   been watering on the wrong days ever since.
4. **Run each station and stand in it.** From the zone, not from the controller.
5. **Record faults per head**, not per station. "Station 4 patchy" is not
   actionable. "Station 4, second head from the west, arc closed to 90 and
   throwing at the fence" is.
6. **Fix, then re-run the station** and confirm from inside the zone.

### The fault table

| What you see | What it is | What it costs if left |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Head does not pop up | Blocked filter or nozzle, sunken head, or a body full of sand | A dry patch that widens each week |
| Fine fog drifting off the zone | Over-pressure at the head | Up to a third of the water blows away; worst on windy Perth afternoons |
| Weak throw, dry ring around the head | Under-pressure, or too many heads on the station | The zone edges never get watered at all |
| Head buried under mulch or lawn | Turf or mulch built up since install | Water goes 300 mm and stops |
| Head tilted | Vehicle, whipper snipper, or settled sand | Half the arc lands on paving |
| Spraying the fence, path or driveway | Arc knocked out of adjustment | Wet paving and a dry bed a metre away |
| Water still draining from the lowest head after shutdown | Low head drainage | A permanently soggy low corner and root rot |
| Station will not shut off | Failed solenoid or debris on the diaphragm | Runs continuously — the single most expensive retic fault there is |
| Wet ground with no head running | Mainline or lateral leak | Shows on the bill before it shows on the lawn |
| Sprays and rotors on the same station | Mixed precipitation rates | One of the two is always wrong. This is a redesign, not a repair |

That last row is worth dwelling on. Spray applies 35 to 45 mm per hour, rotary 10
to 15. On the same valve, whatever run time you pick, half the zone drowns or
half dies. That fix belongs in
[hydrozoning and stations](/curriculum/build/irrigation-hydrozoning-and-stations/),
not on the controller.

### Then re-test uniformity on the stations that matter

You do not need to catch-cup every zone every year. Do the two or three carrying
the most value — the main lawn, the bed with the expensive plants — and compare
against last year's figure in your
[maintenance manual](/curriculum/handover/the-maintenance-manual/). A distribution
uniformity that has fallen from 0.78 to 0.61 means something physical changed, so
go and find it. The method and the maths are in
[precipitation rate, distribution uniformity and run times](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/).

## Seasonal adjustment: the one number you change

This is where set-and-forget costs the most, and it is the easiest thing here to
fix. Almost every controller has a seasonal adjust or water budget feature that
scales every station's run time by a percentage: set your measured summer run
times once, then move one number through the year. Water Corporation publishes
the [percentages for Perth and the South West](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/why-you-need-to-adjust-your-irrigation):

| Month | Seasonal adjust |
| --- | --- |
| January – February | 100% |
| March | 80% |
| April | 60% |
| May | Manual only, if needed |
| June – August | Sprinkler Switch-off — off |
| September | Manual only, if needed |
| October | 60% |
| November | 80% |
| December | 90% |

Set a phone reminder for the first of each month. If the controller has no
seasonal adjust feature, tape a card inside the door with the four sets of run
times on it. Two habits go with this: **turn it off when it rains**, using the
off or rain-delay function rather than pulling the plug, and in May and September
run manually only when the garden asks — a Perth spring often needs nothing for
weeks.

## The controller check

Open it. Most people never do.

**The battery.** What it does depends on the age of the unit, and this trips
people up. Controllers sold in the last decade generally hold the programme in
non-volatile memory with no battery at all, plus an internal lithium cell that
keeps the clock through an outage — Rain Bird's ESP-TM2 specifies exactly that, a
ten-year lithium battery for time and date only. Older units genuinely do lose
the programme without a working 9V. Read your manual, and if there is a
user-replaceable battery, change it at every audit. It is the cheapest thing
you will do all day.

**The clock and the day.** This is the failure that fines you. After an outage a
controller with a flat or absent backup can restart on a default day and water
outside your roster, and Water Corporation is explicit that
[watering on the wrong days because of a programming error is not a valid reason
for breaching](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/programming-your-irrigation).
Check it after every storm, not once a year.

**The whole cycle.** Add every station's run time plus any soak gaps. The cycle
must start late enough to be legal and still finish before 9am. Rotary stations
at 40 to 60 minutes each add up fast, and a six-station cycle starting at 6:30am
does not make it.

**Wi-Fi controllers** need one more check: confirm it is still connected. A new
router or a changed password strands it on its last-known schedule, and the
weather-based adjustment stops without telling you.

## What happens next

You now have a working system, a measured schedule and a record to compare
against next September. Put the audit date into your
[seasonal calendar](/curriculum/maintenance/the-perth-year/) so it happens
without you deciding to do it.
