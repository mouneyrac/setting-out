Most garden pest spending in Perth is wasted, and it is wasted at the first step.
Someone sees a brown patch, buys a lawn insecticide, applies it, and the patch
gets bigger — because the patch was never an insect. It was a blocked sprinkler,
or hydrophobic sand, or a dog.

This lesson is built around one discipline: **identify before you treat**. It
also covers the one Perth pest that carries a legal duty on you personally — the
polyphagous shot-hole borer — and the one disease that, if you bring it onto your
block, you will never get rid of.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper called back to a garden they built does not open the ute. They walk
the zone with three questions in order.

1. **Is the pattern biological or mechanical?** Insect and disease damage
   spreads, moves and has irregular edges. Irrigation and soil damage sits still,
   repeats with the sprinkler spacing, and stops dead at a hard edge.
2. **What is the actual organism?** Not "grubs". A named species, seen, in a
   sample. Different organisms need different timing and different treatments,
   and the wrong one is money burnt.
3. **What is the least intervention that fixes it?** Broad-spectrum insecticide
   kills the predators that were suppressing the pest, so a spray now often
   guarantees a worse outbreak next season.

Nearly every failure in domestic pest control is skipping to step three.

## Rule one: the damage is not the diagnosis

Before you accept a pest explanation for a dying patch of Perth garden, rule
these out — they cause far more damage here than every insect on this page
combined.

| What you see | Check this first |
| --- | --- |
| Brown patch with a hard, defined edge | Sprinkler coverage. Run the station and stand in it — see [watering and the retic audit](/curriculum/maintenance/watering-and-retic-audit/) |
| Patch that stays dry when everything around it is wet | Water repellency in the sand. Water beads and runs off — see [Perth soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) |
| Yellowing across a whole area, evenly | Nutrition, not pest. See [fertilising Perth sand](/curriculum/maintenance/fertilising-perth-sand/) |
| Spongy lawn, footprints stay visible | Thatch. See [lawn care through the Perth year](/curriculum/maintenance/lawn-care-through-the-perth-year/) |
| Small round dead spots with a green ring | Dog urine |
| Straight brown stripes | Fertiliser spreader overlap, or spilled fuel |

The soap flush settles the insect question in ten minutes and costs nothing. Mix
about 60 mL of dishwashing detergent into 5 litres of water and pour it evenly
over one square metre at the **edge** of the damage, where the pest still is —
not in the dead centre, where it has already left. Wait ten minutes. Caterpillars
come up irritated and visible. If nothing comes up, stop buying insecticide.

## The six you will actually meet

### African black beetle

Two pests in one animal. Adults are shiny black, 12 to 14 mm, and chew stems at
or just below soil level, which kills young plants outright. The larvae are white
C-shaped grubs reaching 25 to 30 mm with a yellow-brown head, and they sever
roots — badly affected turf lifts like carpet. Generally one generation a year,
with new adults emerging late December to early February.

Diagnose by digging, not by spraying. Cut a 200 mm square of turf 100 to 150 mm
deep at the edge of a failing patch, roll it back, count the grubs, and put it
back. A handful of grubs in a healthy lawn is normal and always has been.

### Couch mite

The most misdiagnosed problem in Perth couch lawns, because the animal is far too
small to see unaided — you will never spot it without at least a 10x hand lens.
What you see is its effect: injected saliva shortens the internodes and swells
the leaf sheaths, producing tufted rosettes — "witches' broom" — then thinning
and bare patches. Damage appears in spring and browns off through summer, and
because the life cycle runs 7 to 10 days, a dry summer lets numbers explode.

Cultural control first: keep the lawn properly watered and fed, and mow with a
catcher so infested clippings leave the site. The mite is highly host specific
and attacks couch (*Cynodon dactylon*) only — if your lawn is buffalo or kikuyu,
this is not your problem, and the mites that do affect those grasses are
different species.

### Lawn grub (armyworm)

Night-feeding caterpillars that shear the leaf off at the crown, so the lawn
thins from the top down rather than dying from the roots. The identifying mark is
three parallel white stripes running back from the collar behind the head. Moths
are grey-brown with a 40 to 45 mm wingspan and most active on warm, humid
evenings — a cloud of small moths lifting off the lawn at dusk in autumn is your
two-week warning.

*Bacillus thuringiensis* is the least-toxic effective option: specific to
caterpillars, harmless to predators and bees, and it must be sprayed out within
two hours of mixing. Apply in the evening when the caterpillars come up to feed.

### Argentine ants

Slender dark brown ants 2.6 to 3.2 mm long, moving in continuous, well-defined,
slow trails, with a faint greasy or musty smell when crushed. That trail is the
tell — most native ants forage as scattered individuals. DPIRD's own assessment
is blunt: they are the most difficult common pest ant in WA to control because
they recolonise quickly.

Surface sprays make them worse by splitting colonies. Slow-acting bait carried
back to the nest is the only approach that reduces the population, and expect to
repeat it. In a garden they matter mainly because they farm scale and aphids for
honeydew and drive off the predators — so a sudden scale problem on your citrus
is often an ant problem.

### Portuguese millipedes

Not a plant pest. They eat decaying organic matter, and the damage they do is to
your patience: they swarm buildings after autumn and spring rains, drawn to
light. They were first found in WA at Roleystone in 1986, already well
established by then, and populations peak in the first few years in a new area
before declining as a naturally occurring parasitic nematode catches up with
them.

DPIRD's advice is non-chemical first, and it works: turn off unnecessary outdoor
lighting, set light traps *away* from the house so they walk in the other
direction, and fit door seals and dust strips. If you must use a barrier spray,
use it at the base of walls, not across the garden.

### Phytophthora dieback

The one that is not recoverable. *Phytophthora cinnamomi* was introduced to WA
in the early 1900s on infected nursery plants and now kills around 40% of the
state's native plant species. Once it is in an area it cannot be eradicated.

The pattern that distinguishes it from drought or a retic fault:

- **Complete deaths, not dying limbs.** The plant loses the whole root system and
  dies as a unit, looking exactly like it has run out of water while sitting in
  wet soil.
- **A chronology.** Deaths in sequence, freshest next to healthy plants, moving
  downhill.
- **Indicator species going first.** Grass trees (*Xanthorrhoea*), *Banksia*,
  *Isopogon*, *Leucopogon* and jarrah. Wattles, native peas and WA peppermints
  are not susceptible, so a bed where the banksias die and the acacias thrive is
  a strong signal.

Confirmation is a laboratory job: sample moist soil about 500 mm down at a recent
death, sterilise your tools first, fill a takeaway container, and send it to
DBCA's Vegetation Health Service at 17 Dick Perry Avenue, Kensington.

**Hygiene is the whole game.** You spread this on boots, barrow wheels, machinery
and in imported fill, gravel and mulch of unknown origin. Brush the dirt off
physically first, then spray with 70% methylated spirits to 30% water. Do not
work bushland-adjacent soil in the wet, buy plants from accredited nurseries, and
ask where your fill came from. Phosphite suppresses the disease in plants you
want to save — stem injection or foliar spray, repeated every one to three
years — but it is life support, not a cure.

## The one with a legal obligation: polyphagous shot-hole borer

A 2 mm beetle that tunnels into more than 500 tree species worldwide and farms a
fungus in the galleries. It is established in Perth, the national response has
moved from eradication to long-term management, and **a Quarantine Area Notice
now covers the entire Perth metropolitan area.**

That means legal restrictions on what you may do with prunings and removed trees.
In the Management Zone you must not move untreated or unseasoned wood out of the
zone unless it is chipped to 2.5 cm or smaller, you must not move plant material
greater than 2 cm in diameter out of the zone, machinery must be cleaned before
it leaves, and green waste must go to waste facilities in sealed, covered
containers. Anyone who finds or suspects the borer **must** report it under the
*Biosecurity and Agriculture Management Act 2007*.

Look for 1 mm shot-holes, staining and lesions around them, frass, gumming and
branch dieback. Do not deal with it yourself — DPIRD says plainly not to dispose
of a possibly infested plant on your own initiative. Report through MyPestGuide
Reporter or phone PaDIS on (08) 9368 3080, and disinfect pruning tools with 70%
ethanol between trees. The rules change as the response changes, so read the
DPIRD page before you cut, not after.

## Least-toxic effective option first

Work down this ladder and stop at the first rung that works.

1. **Fix the growing conditions.** Water, drainage, soil, mowing height, mulch.
   Most pest outbreaks are a stressed plant advertising itself.
2. **Physical and cultural.** Hose off scale and aphids, catch and remove
   clippings, lights off for millipedes, seal the door, prune out infested wood.
3. **Targeted low-toxicity products.** *Bacillus thuringiensis* for caterpillars,
   horticultural oil and soap for scale and mites, iron-based snail bait, in-nest
   ant bait. These spare the predators.
4. **Registered broad-spectrum chemistry.** Last, on the smallest area that
   works, at the correct timing.

Two hard rules on rung four. Check the product on
[PubCRIS](https://portal.apvma.gov.au/pubcris) to confirm it is registered for
that pest on that plant, and read the label as instructions rather than
suggestions. And never spray anything in flower — bees are doing more for your
garden than the spray is.

## Safety and who is allowed to do what

Pest management technicians and pest management businesses in WA must be licensed
and registered with the Department of Health under the *Health (Pesticides)
Regulations 2011*. The trigger is doing pest management for gain or reward, so
your own garden is your business and a neighbour's for payment is not — the
Department of Health's [pesticide licensing
FAQ](https://www.health.wa.gov.au/Articles/F_I/Frequently-Asked-Questions-Pesticides)
sets out the scope.

Beyond that: wear the PPE the label specifies, never spray in a Perth sea breeze,
keep concentrates locked away from children and pets, and do not go up a ladder
with a sprayer to treat a borer in a mature tree — that is an arborist's job, and
[tree assessment](/curriculum/site-analysis/tree-assessment/) explains when.

## What happens next

Record what you found, when, where and what you did, in the pest section of your
[maintenance manual](/curriculum/handover/the-maintenance-manual/). Perth pest
problems are seasonal and they repeat: a two-line note from last February is
worth more next February than any amount of re-diagnosis.
