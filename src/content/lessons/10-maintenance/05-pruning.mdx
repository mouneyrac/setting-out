Pruning is the one maintenance job where enthusiasm does more damage than
neglect. An unpruned garden is untidy. A badly pruned one carries permanent
wounds inside the wood, weak regrowth in the wrong places, and — the Perth part —
sunburnt bark on limbs that were shaded the summer before.

You are renovating, so most of what you cut was shaped by somebody else. Assume
the previous owner topped things. Three things decide your outcome: where the cut
goes, when you make it, and whether your tools carry disease from one plant to
the next.

## The only three reasons to prune

Every cut answers one of these. If you cannot name which, put the secateurs down.

1. **Health.** Dead, diseased, damaged wood — plus branches crossing and rubbing,
   because rubbing manufactures the first three.
2. **Structure and safety.** A sound framework in a young plant; removing what is
   already a hazard in an old one — a limb over the roof, a codominant stem with
   bark trapped in the union, growth heading for a powerline.
3. **A stated outcome.** More flowers, more fruit, a denser screen, light into a
   window, a hedge that holds a line.

"It looks a bit big" is not on the list. A plant that must be cut hard every year
to fit is the wrong plant in the wrong place, and the fix lives in
[renovate, replace, rethink](/curriculum/maintenance/renovate-replace-rethink/).

## What a professional does before the first cut

They walk the plant once, looking up, and settle three things. **The target form
five years out**, not this weekend's tidy. **The foliage budget** — leaves are
the plant's income, so no more than about a third of the living crown comes off
in a season, and less on anything stressed. **Where the material goes**, which in
Perth is now a genuine question rather than an afterthought.

Then the order never changes: deadwood, crossing and rubbing limbs, the one or
two structural decisions, shaping. Step back three metres every few cuts — you
cannot see form from inside a shrub.

## Where the cut goes

Two landmarks. The **branch bark ridge** is the raised line of bark in the crotch
where branch meets stem. The **branch collar** is the swelling at the base of the
branch. Both are trunk tissue, and they hold the chemistry the plant uses to wall
a wound off. Cut just outside the collar and leave it intact. That is the whole
technique. Two ways to get it wrong:

- **Flush cut** — hard against the trunk, taking the collar with it. You have
  removed the defence and opened a decay column that never closes. AS 4373-2007
  names flush cutting an unacceptable practice.
- **Stub** — too far out, leaving wood beyond a bud or lateral. It dies back,
  decay walks in, and the plant answers with a fistful of weak shoots.

| Cut | What it is | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Removal** | Whole branch back to its origin, outside the collar | Deadwood, crossing limbs, thinning a crowded crown |
| **Reduction** | Back to a lateral at least one-third the diameter of the stem removed | Shortening a limb; clearance from roof or path |
| **Heading** | Back to a bud or node, sloping away just above it | Formative work on young shrubs, roses, hedging |
| **Topping / lopping** | Indiscriminate cutting to stubs or undersized laterals | Never. Unacceptable under AS 4373-2007 |

That one-third rule is the line between a reduction cut and topping. A lateral
smaller than a third of the stem cannot take over as the leader, so what you have
made is a stub with a leaf on it.

Anything you cannot hold up with one hand gets the **three-cut method**. Undercut
200–300 mm out from the collar, a third of the way through; cut from above
slightly further out until the limb falls; then take the stub back to the collar.
Skip the undercut and the falling limb rips bark down the trunk — a wound ten
times the size of the one you intended.

No wound dressing. It does not reduce decay or speed closure, and on a Perth
summer cut it traps heat and moisture against the wound.

## Tools, and hygiene between plants

Bypass secateurs, not anvil — anvil blades crush the stem against a plate. Above
about 13 mm, move to loppers or a pruning saw. Keep everything sharp: a sharp
blade makes a wound that closes, a blunt one makes a wound that sits open.

Clean sap and soil off first, because disinfectant does nothing through a layer
of gunk, then wipe the blades. DPIRD's stated preference is **70% ethanol**; a
spray bottle of methylated spirits is the practical version.

Disinfect between every plant when working on anything showing dieback, cankers,
oozing or unexplained decline; on roses, which in an established Perth garden are
usually carrying something; on palms, where the saw is a known vector for lethal
wilts; on citrus and other fruit; and on anything from the shot-hole borer host
list. Near bushland or up in the hills, get soil off boots and blades too —
Phytophthora dieback travels in dirt.

## The shot-hole borer changed how you dispose of prunings

Polyphagous shot-hole borer was detected in WA in August 2021, and the national
response has since moved from eradication to **Transition to Management**. DPIRD
has accepted the beetle cannot be eradicated, its own pruning programme now
targets only the outer metro, and **every other landowner in the Perth
metropolitan area is responsible for their own affected trees.** A Quarantine
Area Notice covers the whole metro. While you are pruning:

- Do not move untreated or unseasoned wood out of the Management Zone — the Perth
  metro area — unless it is chipped to pieces 2.5 cm or less in diameter, and do
  not move plant material, including living plants, greater than 2 cm in diameter
  out of the zone at all.
- Take green waste to a local government waste facility in a **fully sealed and
  covered** skip bin, trailer or truck. An open trailer of branches is not
  compliant.
- Clean machinery, including a hired chipper, before it leaves the zone.
- Sanitise tools with 70% ethanol before moving to a healthy tree.

Check whether what you are cutting is a host. DPIRD's WA host list rates box
elder maple, coral tree and black locust **Extreme**; common fig, white mulberry,
London plane, poinciana, Moreton Bay fig and swamp paperbark **Very High**;
marri, red flowering gum, avocado, English oak, liquidambar and mango **High**.
Pruning puts your face closest to the bark, so watch for 1 mm shot-holes, dark
staining, gumming and frass, and report anything suspect to the Pest and Disease
Information Service on 08 9368 3080. More in
[WA pests and diseases](/curriculum/maintenance/wa-pests-and-diseases/).

## A Perth timing guide

Two rules cover most of it. **The flowering rule:** prune after flowering,
because a plant that flowers on last year's wood loses this year's display to a
winter tidy-up. **The sunburn rule**, the Perth-specific one and the mistake that
costs most: hard pruning between December and February strips shade off bark that
has never seen direct sun, and 40 °C plus WA summer UV cooks the cambium
underneath. Blistered, splitting bark on the north-west side of a citrus is
almost always last summer's cut. December to February is deadwood only.

| Plant | When in Perth | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roses | Mid-July to early August | Open the centre, cut to an outward bud. Light tidy late February |
| Citrus | After harvest, Aug–Sep | Light. Skirt clear of the ground; shade any limb you suddenly expose |
| Deciduous fruit and grapes | July–August, fully dormant | The one window for hard structural work. Apricots and cherries are the exception — cut those in dry late summer, or invite canker |
| Frangipani | Only if you must, late Aug–Sep | Latex irritates skin and eyes. Let cuts callus before the wet |
| Hibiscus, bougainvillea | September, once the cold has gone | Both flower on new wood |
| Lavender, rosemary, westringia | Light trim after each flush | Never cut into bare old wood — it will not reshoot |
| Grevillea, banksia, hakea | Immediately after flowering, lightly | Most will not shoot from bare wood. Tip prune from planting |
| Callistemon, melaleuca, kunzea | After flowering | Among the few natives that take hard cuts into old wood |
| Kangaroo paw | Whole clump to ground level, late autumn | Takes the ink spot away with the leaves |
| Lomandra, dianella, grasses | Late winter, every two to three years | Cut to 100–150 mm, or comb the dead out |
| Hedges and evergreen screens | Light cuts Sep–Nov, again Mar–Apr | Batter the sides — wider at the base — or the bottom goes bare |
| Deciduous shade trees | Winter, while leafless | Plane, jacaranda, liquidambar are borer hosts. Contain the waste |
| Eucalypts — marri, tuart, jarrah | Deadwood any time; structural work is arborist work | Gums drop limbs. Do not try to reduce one yourself |
| Palms | Only fully dead, hanging fronds | Never cut green fronds. Sterilise the saw between palms |

## Height, chainsaws, and when it stops being your job

The tree is not worth your spine. Hard limits worth adopting:

- **Never a chainsaw off a ladder.** Not for a short cut, not for a small branch.
- **Both feet on the ground**, or a footed ladder for hand tools only. Eye
  protection and a helmet with a pole saw, and never stand under the cut.
- **Powerlines: hands off.** Western Power's typical minimum clearances are 2.5 m
  to the side and 2 m below in urban areas, 4 m and 2.5 m semi-rural and rural,
  and it asks that overhanging branches be removed so nothing sits in the
  clearance area above the line. Ring them on 13 10 87 for your own street; the
  zones vary with conductor type, fire risk and span. Western Power's own advice
  is to hire a professional tree pruner or arborist, because trimming anywhere
  near powerlines is dangerous. Electricity arcs; you do not need to touch the
  conductor, and a green branch is one.
- **Anything that could swing, split or land** on a roof, fence, pool or
  neighbour. Rigging is a trade skill.
- **Anything on a tree you suspect is compromised** — back to
  [tree assessment](/curriculum/site-analysis/tree-assessment/) first.

Tree work is not a licensed trade in WA the way electrical and plumbing work are,
so there is no licence number to check. Check instead: qualification (AQF Level 3
for climbing and pruning, AQF Level 5 for assessment and written reports), a
current public liability certificate, workers' compensation, and a written scope
saying **"all pruning to AS 4373-2007"**. A quote offering to "lop" or "top" your
tree has told you it does not work to that standard. And do not do this alone at
38 °C — heat is the Perth hazard that does not sound like one.

## Your neighbour's tree, and the verge tree

In WA you may cut an overhanging branch back to where it enters your property and
cut encroaching roots back to the boundary — but not beyond it, and you may not
enter their land or poison the tree. The cuttings legally belong to the tree's
owner, so offer them back. Check two things first: your council may have a tree
preservation order banning the pruning of a listed tree without a permit, and the
verge tree is the council's asset, not yours.
