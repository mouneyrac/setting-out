Nothing in a garden fails on a date you would notice. The paving does not crack
overnight, the screening hedge does not suddenly block the window it was planted
to frame, and the deck does not announce that its bearers have been sitting in
damp sand for eleven years. Decline is gradual, and gradual is invisible to the
person who walks past it every day.

That is what the annual review is for. Once a year you look at the garden the
way a stranger would, with the brief in your hand, and you write down what has
changed. Most years the answer is small — a few plants pruned harder than they
should need, a station running long. Occasionally the answer is that a whole
area has stopped doing its job and no amount of maintenance will fix it.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper on a maintenance contract does not walk a property looking for
things to tidy. They walk it holding a register: every element, its condition,
and the year it is expected to need replacing. That register is how they
forecast next year's budget, and it is why their clients' gardens keep improving
while an unmanaged garden of the same age is quietly getting worse.

The register exists because **planned replacement is dramatically cheaper than
unplanned replacement.** Plan it and you buy in autumn when the nursery has
stock, you get one mobilisation instead of three, and you can bring the new tree
on beside the old one before the old one comes out. React to a failure in
January and you are paying emergency labour, buying whatever is on the bench,
and planting into the worst month of the year.

So the discipline is: forecast the failure, and act one season early.

## The annual review

Do it in Djeran — April into May. Summer has just finished showing you every
weakness, and you are standing at the front edge of the autumn planting window,
which is the only sensible time to act on what you find. See
[the Perth year](/curriculum/maintenance/the-perth-year/) for the wider
calendar.

Take three things outside with you: the
[design brief](/curriculum/brief/writing-the-design-brief/) you wrote in
Phase 2, the [as-built drawings](/curriculum/handover/as-built-drawings/) from
Phase 9, and last year's photos. Then go line by line through the brief and mark
each requirement as **still true**, **drifting**, or **failed**.

| Brief item | Still true looks like | The Perth drift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shade over the alfresco by 3pm | You sit there in February | Tree pruned to fit, canopy never developed |
| Screening from the neighbour's upper window | You cannot see it from the seat | Screen plants thinned out at the base |
| Lawn the kids can use | It takes traffic and recovers | Compacted, thatchy, half in shade the design did not predict |
| Under an hour a week of maintenance | You actually spend that | Two hours, mostly on one bed fighting kikuyu |
| Water bill within budget | Roster days only, no hand-watering | A dry corner being propped up with a hose |

The last column is the point. Drift is not failure — it is the early signal, and
it is cheap to correct. Something marked **failed** goes on the rethink list,
not the repair list.

## Plants that have outgrown their position

The honest test is not "is it too big?" It is: **how much of this plant do I
have to remove each year to keep it where it needs to be?** Light shaping is
maintenance. Taking a third or more of the canopy off annually, or pruning the
same shrub three times a year to hold a line, means the plant is in the wrong
place and pruning has become a permanent tax. Replace it with something whose
mature size is the size you want, using the
[Waterwise plants directory](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/waterwise-plants)
to filter by growth habit and, critically, by availability.

Roots are the other trigger. Lifting paving, a crack tracking toward a wall
footing, or repeated blockages in the sewer junction all mean the tree has found
infrastructure. Water Corporation is blunt: vigorous roots enter pipes through
small cracks, and
[damage to their underground assets may result in a fine](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/Help-and-advice/Waterwise/Garden/Selecting-the-right-tree).
Approval is required for works within 10 metres of their assets.

**Check the law before you book anyone.** Some Perth councils regulate trees on
private land. In the City of South Perth, any living tree 8.0 metres high, or
with a 6.0 metre canopy, or a 1.5 metre trunk circumference is a Regulated Tree
requiring development approval before removal or damaging pruning — with maximum
penalties under the *Planning and Development Act 2005* of $200,000 for an
individual. Other councils have nothing. Find out which yours is, in writing,
before the saw arrives.

**And check for borer.** Polyphagous shot-hole borer has been in WA since August
2021, the national response has now shifted from eradication to transition to
management, and the whole Perth metropolitan area is a quarantine area. That
changes what you may do with the material: wood cannot leave the zone unless
chipped to 2.5 cm or smaller, and green waste must travel in a fully sealed and
covered bin, trailer or truck. Suspect trees get reported through MyPestGuide
Reporter or PaDIS, not quietly taken to the tip. More on identification in
[WA pests and diseases](/curriculum/maintenance/wa-pests-and-diseases/).

Tree work is where home gardeners get seriously hurt. Anything requiring a
chainsaw above shoulder height, a ladder, or work within reach of powerlines is
a job for an insured contractor with a qualified climber. Use
[IACA](https://www.iaca.org.au/contact-iaca/find-an-arborist/) to get an
assessment from someone who is not also quoting the removal.

## Surface wear versus base failure

Same principle as the
[pre-renovation audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/),
applied annually instead of once: the visible layer and the structure beneath it
fail independently and cost completely different amounts.

| Element | Wear (clean, top up, patch) | Failure (rebuild) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paving | Efflorescence, staining, moss, a few chipped units, joint sand low | Ruts or dips following a line, water still ponding 15 min after rain, pavers rocking, joint sand vanishing repeatedly, edge rows fanning out |
| Concrete / liquid limestone | Surface crazing, fading, tyre marks | A crack with a level change across it, a slab that rocks, exposed aggregate ravelling in patches |
| Deck | Greying, cupped or splintered boards, loose fixings | Soft bearers or joists, movement underfoot, rot where the frame meets soil or a planter |
| Limestone wall | Surface flaking, algae, weathered mortar joints | Bulging, a lean that has increased since last year's photo, stepped cracking, no visible drainage |
| Mulch | Thinned to under 50 mm | Hydrophobic crust shedding water off the bed |
| Turf | Thatch, minor wear paths | Compacted base, wrong species for the shade it now sits in |

The paving test takes ten minutes: lift four to six units at the worst spot,
check the bedding sand is a consistent 25 to 30 mm rather than 60 to 80 mm of
correction, and push a screwdriver into the layer below it by hand. If it goes
in easily there is no compacted limestone base and the area will keep moving.
Under roughly 10% of the area affected is a local lift-and-relay; widespread
movement means stockpiling the pavers and rebuilding the base — that is
[base preparation](/curriculum/build/base-preparation/) and
[paving](/curriculum/build/paving/) again.

For decks, put the build date against the timber's AS 5604 durability class.
Jarrah is Class 2: 15 to 40 years above ground, but only 15 to 25 in ground
contact. A twelve-year-old jarrah deck with sound boards and soft bearers is a
frame replacement, not a sanding job.

Cutting pavers, limestone or concrete produces respirable crystalline silica.
Wet-cut or use on-tool extraction, and wear a fitted P2 respirator. WorkSafe's
duties bind businesses, not you in your own yard — the silicosis does not make
that distinction.

## Planning replacement before things die

Three habits do most of the work.

1. **Plant the successor early.** Where a screen or a feature tree is nearing the
   end, get the replacement in beside or behind it and give it two or three
   years before the old one comes out. You lose the function for one season
   instead of five.
2. **Replant in autumn, and mind the switch-off.** April to July is the window.
   But the [winter sprinkler switch-off](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/winter-sprinkler-switch-off)
   runs 1 June to 31 August for bore users as well as scheme users, with a $100
   fine — so a new lawn or garden planted in that window needs an approved
   watering exemption, and breaching its conditions risks up to $500. Plan the
   paperwork with the planting.
3. **Hold an annual renewal line in the household budget.** A number you set
   every year, spent on the top item from the review, is what stops the
   twelve-year mass failure where everything installed in the same month reaches
   end of life in the same month. If a tree is on the list, check whether a
   [Treebate](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/department-of-water-and-environmental-regulation/treebate)
   round is open — up to $150 for one native with a canopy reaching three metres
   at maturity.

## When to rethink instead of repair

Repair restores the thing you have. Rethink is for when the thing you have is no
longer the thing you need. Three triggers:

- **The brief has changed.** The toddler lawn is now a teenager's basketball
  slab; the dog has arrived; someone works from a room that now looks at a bin
  store. Go back to [needs, wants and the five-year view](/curriculum/brief/needs-wants-and-the-five-year-view/)
  and rewrite the brief before you touch anything.
- **The same repair keeps recurring.** Third time you have re-sanded the same
  joints or topped up the same dip, stop repairing and diagnose the base.
- **Compliance has moved.** Rebuilding a patio, pergola or carport puts you back
  under the building rules. A registered building contractor is required where
  the work needs a permit and is $50,000 or more for a Class 10a building, or
  $20,000 or more for other building work — so a modest rebuild may sit under
  the threshold while a larger one does not.

A rethink re-enters the process at Phase 5. Do not design it in the yard with a
shovel; it gets a concept, a cost and a sequence like anything else.
