A landscaping contract is not paperwork you sign once the real decisions are made. It is the document that decides who pays when the excavator finds limestone at 400 mm, when the wall has to be engineered after the geotech comes back, and when a plant on the schedule is not available in the size you specified.

Nearly every landscaping dispute in WA starts the same way: a one-page quote, a deposit paid on the spot, and everything after that agreed verbally on site. The fix is not a lawyer. It is knowing four numbers and refusing to move off them. Usefully, WA has a statute written for exactly this situation, it names landscaping specifically, and its rules fit on two pages.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper who has been trading for a decade does not send a quote. They send a contract with the quote inside it, because they have been burned.

The reasoning is the same reasoning that protects you. An unpriced scope is an unlimited liability. If the contract says "supply and install retaining wall" without a drawing, a height, a material and a footing detail, every argument about what was included is a coin toss the contractor loses in front of a regulator. So they write the scope by reference to numbered, dated drawings, write an exclusions list longer than most clients expect, and price a variation before doing it rather than after. Their payment claims track physical progress, not the calendar — "on completion of retaining walls and backfill" is defensible, "week three" is not.

Hold up your end of that and you will get better prices, because a contractor pricing a vague job prices the risk into it.

## Which law covers your job

| Fixed-price contract value | What governs it |
| --- | --- |
| Under $7,500 | Australian Consumer Law only. No statutory deposit cap, no statutory variation rules. |
| **$7,500 to $500,000** | **Home Building Contracts Act 1991** plus the ACL. This is where almost every residential landscaping job sits. |
| Over $500,000 | ACL and general contract law. The HBC Act deposit cap and variation rules do not apply. |

Two points people get wrong. The Act applies to *landscaping* by name — it is not just for house builders. And a "builder" under the Act is any person carrying on a business performing that work, **whether or not they are registered**. Your unregistered landscaper is a builder for these purposes and is bound by every rule below.

## What must be in writing before work starts

Before you sign, the builder must give you a copy of the [Notice for the Home Owner](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/law_s4507.html), Schedule 1 of the Home Building Contracts Regulations 1992. If nobody mentions it, that tells you something about how the rest of the job will run.

The contract must be in writing, contain all the terms, show the date and be signed by both parties. An oral agreement does not comply. Building and Energy separately tells registered building contractors that under the Security of Payment Act any construction contract over $20,000 including GST must be in writing with mandatory contract information — two laws pushing the same way.

A landscaping contract that will not embarrass you later contains:

- **Parties, ABN, and any registration or licence numbers.** Check them (see below).
- **Scope by reference to numbered, dated drawings** — levels plan, [hardscape details](/curriculum/documentation/hardscape-details/), drainage plan and planting schedule, listed by revision.
- **An exclusions list.** The section that saves money: rock or limestone breaking, imported soil beyond a stated volume, service relocation, verge works, wall engineering, tree removal, make-good on existing structures.
- **Provisional sums, named and capped**, plus **rates for the predictable variations** — excavation per cubic metre, disposal per tonne, crew and machine hourly rates. This turns a variation into arithmetic instead of a negotiation.
- **A payment schedule tied to physical milestones**, not dates.
- **Site logistics**: access, spoil stockpile, water and power, working hours, rubbish.
- **Who obtains permits** — see [structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/) and [retaining walls approval](/curriculum/approvals/retaining-walls-approval/).
- **Practical completion, the defects period, and the final payment trigger.**

## Deposits and progress payments

The deposit cannot exceed **6.5 per cent of the total cost of the work**. On a $60,000 landscape contract that is $3,900 and not a dollar more. Consumer Protection's general rule of thumb for trades outside the Act is around 10 per cent.

Once work has started, a progress payment must only be for **work actually performed or materials already supplied**. A claim for pavers still sitting at the yard is not a valid claim. Rise-and-fall clauses are prohibited, so the price should be fixed and final apart from pass-through of increased taxes or duty.

Treat these as walk-away signals: a request for cash, a demand for payment in full before work begins, or a refusal to put the quote in writing.

Structure the schedule so the money always sits slightly behind the work — deposit, then claims on completion of demolition and earthworks, of retaining and drainage, of paving and structures, of irrigation and soil preparation, with a meaningful final payment released only after the [defect walkthrough](/curriculum/handover/the-defect-walkthrough/) is closed out.

## Variations: the clause that decides how this ends

The rule is short. A variation must be **in writing, dated, signed by both parties, and a copy given to you before the variation work commences**. The exceptions are narrow: a direction from a building surveyor, or circumstances that genuinely could not have been foreseen.

A variation document needs four things: what changed, why, the price adjustment up or down, and the effect on the completion date. Miss the fourth and you will argue about delay later.

On Perth blocks the variations are predictable, which is why you price them in advance: limestone or coffee rock shallower than expected, buried rubble or an old slab, a service found where the [locate](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/) did not put it, levels that differ from the survey once the site is stripped, plant substitutions for availability, and a wall upgraded to an engineered design once loads are checked.

The trap is the verbal one. "While you're here, could you just…" said six times across a build is how a $60,000 job becomes $78,000 with no paper trail. Under the ACL a tradesperson cannot charge you for work you did not approve — but winning that argument costs far more than the two minutes it takes to email "please price this as a variation before you start".

## The landscaping insurance trap

This is the part most homeowners have backwards. Home indemnity insurance is compulsory for residential building work above $20,000 — but **not** for associated work performed alone under a separate contract, and the regulator lists swimming pools, fences, pergolas and landscaping as exactly that. HII is not required for standalone landscaping even over $20,000. It generally only applies where the landscaping sits inside a contract that also includes construction or renovation of a residence.

So a $90,000 landscape contract typically carries **no insolvency protection at all**. If the business fails mid-build you are an unsecured creditor holding a half-finished site.

You cannot buy your way out of that, so you manage it: never let payments run ahead of work, keep the deposit at the cap, [stage the project](/curriculum/costing/staging-the-project/) so each stage is complete and usable, and check the trading history of the entity rather than the brand on the ute.

## Verifying who you are hiring

Do all of this before the deposit leaves your account.

| Check | Where | What you are looking for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entity | [ABN Lookup](https://abr.business.gov.au/) | ABN active, entity name matches the quote, not registered last month |
| Builder registration | [WA Online Licence Search](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-and-energy-licence-and-registration-search) | Required for most building work $20,000+ that needs a permit |
| Electrical | Same search | Any lighting, pump or water feature circuit — non-negotiable |
| Plumbing | Same search | Anything connected to scheme water or sewer |
| Public liability | Certificate of currency | Insured name matches the contracting entity, policy current across your build dates |
| Workers compensation | Certificate of currency | If they have employees on your site |

From **1 July 2026** a Class 10a building valued under $50,000 no longer needs a registered building contractor in WA, and you can build one yourself without owner-builder approval. That covers patios, pergolas, carports, private garages and sheds — so the person building your pergola may legitimately not be registered. It does **not** remove the building permit, and it does not remove the Home Building Contracts Act.

Electrical work is the one place to be inflexible. Garden lighting, pump circuits and anything near water must be done by a licensed electrical contractor. A cheap unlicensed job buried under mulch and irrigation is a genuine electrocution risk, and it voids your insurance. See [garden lighting](/curriculum/build/garden-lighting/).

## If it goes wrong

Put the complaint in writing to the contractor first — and note that written notice of defects within **four months of practical completion** obliges the builder to make them good at no cost to you.

If that fails, Building and Energy handles contract disputes on lump sum contracts between $7,500 and $500,000 within three years of the cause arising, and workmanship complaints of any value within six years of completion, after a notice of proposed complaint served at least 14 days before lodging. Consumer Protection handles the Australian Consumer Law side.

And if someone knocks on the door offering to seal your driveway or trim your trees, that is an [unsolicited consumer agreement](https://www.consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/unsolicited-agreements) over $100 and you have **10 business days** to cancel it. In a dispute the business has to prove it was not unsolicited.
