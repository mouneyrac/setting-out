Almost nobody builds a garden in one continuous run. The money arrives in
instalments, the approvals arrive on their own schedule, and roughly half the
year is the wrong half to be putting plants in the ground. Staging is how you
turn one large job into three or four smaller ones without paying for the same
work twice.

A staging plan has to pass exactly two tests. **No stage requires undoing a
previous stage.** And **at the end of every stage the garden is usable** — not a
fenced construction zone you live beside for eight months while you save up.

On a renovation both tests are harder, because there is already paving, planting
and a retic system in the ground and each of those is either something you are
protecting or something you are demolishing. Decide which, before you write the
stages.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper does not build a programme forwards from "when can we start". They
build it backwards from two fixed points, and forwards from one constraint.

The two fixed points are **the planting window**, which in Perth is autumn, and
**the longest approval**. Everything else is negotiable; those two are not. A
programme that has plants going in during October has already failed, and no
amount of good workmanship fixes it afterwards.

The forward constraint is **depth**. You work from the deepest and most permanent
element to the shallowest and most replaceable. Stormwater and conduit are deep
and permanent. Mulch is shallow and gets topped up every couple of years.
Anything installed out of depth order gets dug through later.

Then there is the habit homeowners almost always miss: **protect the access**. On
a typical Perth block every tonne of limestone, sand, soil and mulch comes
through one side gate about a metre wide, or over the verge and down the
driveway. Whatever sits in that corridor gets built **last**. Pave the side
access in Stage 1 and you have chosen to hand-barrow the rest of the project, or
to lift pavers you have already paid to lay.

## The sequence that does not change

| # | Stage | Why it must come first | What it costs you otherwise |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Approvals, notices, BYDA enquiry | Physical work is illegal or blind without them | Building order to remove; a service strike |
| 2 | Site establishment, protection, demolition, clearing | You cannot work around what you are removing | Damaging the tree or wall you were keeping |
| 3 | Bulk earthworks, cut and fill, setting out | Every level downstream references it | Every subsequent level is wrong |
| 4 | Retaining walls, with drainage and backfill | Nothing can sit behind or above an unbuilt wall | A rebuild plus the excavation to reach it |
| 5 | **Deep services**: stormwater, soakwells, sleeves and conduit under future paving, irrigation mainline, valve boxes, lighting cable | They live under the surfaces | Saw-cutting finished paving or concrete |
| 6 | Structure footings (patio posts, pergola, screens) | Footings go through the base, not the finished surface | Coring a footing through a new slab |
| 7 | Base preparation and compaction | Compaction is impossible once surfaces are down | Settlement, rocking pavers, standing water |
| 8 | Paving, concrete, liquid limestone, decking, structures | Wet trades make mess | Slurry and offcuts through finished beds |
| 9 | Soil preparation and imported soil | Bulk soil is barrowed and tipped | Tipping a tonne of soil onto dripline |
| 10 | Irrigation laterals, risers, dripline | Distribution follows the soil profile | Trenching through root balls |
| 11 | Planting, then turf | — | — |
| 12 | Mulch, edging, lighting fittings, final clean | — | — |

Note what happens to irrigation: it is **split across two stages, not one**. The
buried infrastructure — mainline, solenoid valves, wiring and every sleeve that
crosses under a future path or driveway — goes in at stage 5, before a single
paver is laid. The laterals and emitters go in at stage 10, after the soil and
before the plants. Treat "irrigation" as one stage and you will either bury it
under paving or crush it under a soil delivery. See
[mainline, valves and wiring](/curriculum/build/irrigation-mainline-valves-and-wiring/)
and [emitters](/curriculum/build/irrigation-emitters/).

Three specifics are worth spelling out. **Sleeves** — every crossing you might
ever need, for retic, low-voltage lighting, power to a shed or a future tap —
goes in oversized before the base is compacted, gets capped, and gets
photographed against a fixed feature. **Soakwells and downpipe connections** are
set by the roof and the falls, not by the garden design, so they precede paving
levels; see [drainage and soakwells](/curriculum/build/drainage-and-soakwells/).
**Machine access** is the most expensive staging error on a Perth renovation:
work rear-of-block first, verge and driveway last.

## Approval lead times sit in front of the stages they gate

Put these on the programme as durations, not as reminders.

| Gate | Clock | Stage it blocks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Development approval | 60 days, or 90 days if advertised | Anything that fails the R-Codes deemed-to-comply |
| Building permit — certified (BA1) | 10 business days, **plus** the building surveyor's own turnaround to issue the BA3 | Patios, sheds, larger decks, retaining over 500 mm |
| Building permit — uncertified (BA2) | 25 business days | Class 1a and Class 10 only |
| Adjoining owner consent (BA20) | 28 days for the neighbour to respond, **before** the permit can issue | Boundary walls, boundary retaining |
| Water Corporation section 90 approval | No published turnaround — ask when you lodge | Excavation near sewer, water main or drain |
| Before You Dig enquiry | Members asked to respond within 2 business days; plans expire | Any excavation |
| CTF levy | Payable before construction commences | Day one on site, if project value is $100,000 or more |
| New lawn/garden watering exemption | Apply on the day of installation | The 35 or 42 days after planting |

Two traps. The planning clock and the building clock run **in sequence**, not in
parallel — you generally cannot lodge for a building permit until development
approval, where required, is granted. And a certified application's 10 business
days only starts after your private building surveyor has issued the certificate
of design compliance, which is often the longer half of the wait. Details in
[patios, pergolas, decks and sheds](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/)
and [retaining walls](/curriculum/approvals/retaining-walls-approval/).

One recent change: from 1 July 2026 a Class 10a building — patio, pergola,
carport, shed — valued under $50,000 no longer needs a registered building
contractor in WA, and a homeowner can build it without owner-builder approval.
The building permit is still required. That removes a lead time from many staging
plans; it does not remove the permit.

## Perth seasons: what actually lands when

| Window | Good for | Bad for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **March–May** | Planting, turf, soil preparation, everything | Nothing. This is the window the rest of the programme is arranged around |
| **June–August** | Demolition, excavation in damp sand, structures, base prep between fronts | Sprinklers are prohibited 1 June to 31 August without an exemption; flooded trenches; slow concrete cures; mud tracked through the house |
| **September–November** | Hardscape, structures, soil preparation, lawn removal | Planting — six good weeks, then summer arrives |
| **December–February** | Hard landscaping if the crew can cope with the heat | Planting and turf without a 42-day exemption; concrete and liquid limestone going off before you finish; heat stress and working alone |

Autumn planting is not a preference, it is arithmetic. Plant in late April or May
and the establishment window is covered by a 35-day watering exemption, then the
winter rain does the work, and the plant has five or six months of root growth
before its first Perth summer. Plant the same species in October and you get
42 exemption days followed immediately by February. Same plant, same money,
different survival rate.

Turf can legitimately go down outside autumn, because it is watered daily under
exemption regardless. But laying turf in December buys you 42 days of irrigation
into a Perth summer and almost no margin for a controller fault.

## Safety that belongs in the staging plan, not the risk register

- **Trenching.** Perth sand has effectively no cohesion and gives no warning
  before it collapses. Read the [WorkSafe excavation code of practice](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/publications/code-practice-excavation)
  before the deep services stage, and never enter an unsupported trench alone.
- **Silica dust.** Cutting pavers, limestone blocks or concrete with a demo saw
  generates respirable crystalline silica. Wet cutting, on-tool extraction and a
  fitted P2 respirator — every time, including the one quick cut.
- **Asbestos.** Anything on a Perth block built before about 1990 — super six
  fencing, fibro sheds, old patio infills — is assumed to contain asbestos until
  proven otherwise. That is a demolition-stage decision with its own licensing
  and disposal lead time; the thresholds are in
  [demolition and clearing](/curriculum/build/demolition-and-clearing/).
- **Heat.** Staging heavy work into January means someone labouring alone at 38
  degrees. Move it, or plan for a second person.

## Writing your stages

Break at trade boundaries, not mid-trade. A stage ending halfway through paving
costs you a second mobilisation, a second delivery and a second set-up. Each
stage gets a budget line carried from
[building the quote](/curriculum/costing/building-the-quote/), a target season,
and one sentence describing what the garden looks like when it finishes. If you
cannot write that sentence, the boundary is in the wrong place.

A workable three-stage renovation on a Perth block looks like this. **Stage 1
(winter):** approvals, demolition, earthworks, retaining, all deep services and
sleeves, base prep, then a compacted limestone surface everyone can walk on.
**Stage 2 (spring):** paving, structures, decking — the garden is now usable and
looks finished-ish. **Stage 3 (the following autumn):** soil, laterals, planting,
turf, mulch, lighting fittings. Nothing in stage 3 disturbs anything in stages 1
or 2, and the planting lands in April.

The next lesson turns this programme into a contract — because staged work is
where variations breed, and the stage boundary is exactly where the scope
argument happens.
