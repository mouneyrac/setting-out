A take-off is the bridge between the drawings you finished in Phase 6 and the
money you are about to spend. It is one job: convert every line on the plan into
a quantity, in the unit a supplier will actually sell it to you in.

Everything downstream depends on it. Price the wrong quantity accurately and you
still get the wrong number. On a renovation there is a second half most people
forget — you also have to measure what leaves the site, because demolition,
cartage and disposal are priced by volume and weight, and they are not small.

Set aside a clear three hours, a printed set of drawings, a scale rule, three
highlighters and a spreadsheet. Do not do this on the phone in the garden.

## What a professional does at this stage

Four habits separate a take-off you can trust from a list of guesses.

**They measure on paper, once, and mark it off.** Every element gets
highlighted on the print the moment it is measured. A colour per category —
hard surfaces, soft landscape, services. At the end, anything unhighlighted has
not been measured. This is the entire quality control system, and it works.

**They keep nett and order quantities in separate columns.** Nett is what the
drawing says: 48.6 m² of paving. Order is what you buy: 51 m², rounded up to
whole packs. Wastage lives in the order column and never gets buried in the
nett figure, because six weeks later you will need to know which number was
measured and which was an allowance.

**They keep the working visible.** Every line shows its dimensions —
`5.4 × 3.2 × 0.1` — not just the answer. When the client (you) moves the
alfresco 600 mm, you edit one line rather than redo three hours of work.

**They measure in supplier units.** Not "some limestone". Cubic metres of
crushed limestone, tonnes of aggregate, whole packs of pavers, 25 m coils of
poly, plants by pot size. A take-off in units nobody sells is a take-off you
have to do twice.

## Work in build order, not plan order

Measure in the order the work happens. Following the build sequence is what
stops you forgetting the layers you cannot see once the job is finished.

1. Demolition and disposal — what comes out
2. Excavation and spoil — cut and fill
3. Drainage, soakwells, pits and pipe
4. Retaining walls, footings, backfill, drainage aggregate
5. Sub-base and bedding under every hard surface
6. Hard surfaces themselves
7. Edges and restraints
8. Structures, footings, fixings
9. Services: retic pipe, wire, sleeves, lighting cable
10. Soil, amendment, planting, turf, mulch
11. Establishment: stakes, ties, wetting agent, water

Compare that list to your [schedule of works](/curriculum/documentation/specification-and-schedule-of-works/).
If an item is in the specification but not in the take-off, one of the two
documents is wrong.

## Areas: the wastage is in the cuts

Break every irregular shape into rectangles and triangles and keep them as
separate lines. Measure to a consistent edge — the outside face of the edge
restraint, every time.

Deduct openings over about 1 m². Do not bother deducting a 400 mm planting
pocket; the labour to cut around it costs more than the pavers you saved.

Cut allowance depends on the pattern, not the material:

| Situation | Add | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rectangular area, stretcher bond, running parallel to the edges | 5% | Cuts only at two ends. This is the Midland Brick figure. |
| Herringbone or 45-degree lay | 10% | Every perimeter unit is a cut, and half of each cut is waste. |
| Curves, circles, radius edges | 12–15% | Nothing tessellates. Expect offcuts you cannot use anywhere. |
| Small or fiddly areas under about 15 m² | 15% | Wastage is a perimeter effect, and small areas are almost all perimeter. |
| Natural stone or a discontinued paver | 15% plus attic stock | You cannot go back for four more in two years. |

Then round up to whole packs. Pavers are sold by the pack or the pallet, and
buying 51 m² usually means buying 54.

The cut count is also a safety number. Cutting pavers, limestone, concrete or
stone releases respirable crystalline silica, and the WA exposure standard is
0.05 mg/m³ over an eight-hour shift. Cut wet, use on-tool extraction, wear a
correctly fitted P2 or better, and vacuum rather than sweep. A herringbone
driveway is a day on a brick saw — if that changes your appetite for the
pattern, better to learn it now than on the day.

## Volumes: three multipliers people miss

Volume is where amateur take-offs fail, because the volume you calculate is
almost never the volume you order.

**Compaction.** Crushed limestone is delivered loose and ends up compacted. A
100 mm compacted base over 10 m² is 1.0 m³ finished, but you must buy more than
that. Plan on roughly 25% extra and confirm the factor with your yard for the
specific product — ask them directly, they will tell you.

**Bulking.** Excavated sand swells when you dig it. A 10 m³ hole produces
noticeably more than 10 m³ of loose spoil in the bin. Order disposal on the
loose volume, not the hole.

**Settlement.** Soil blends and mulch consolidate. Order to the depth you want
in twelve months, not the depth on the day.

Useful conversions, all per 10 m² of area:

| Layer | Typical depth | Compacted or finished m³ | Order |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Base under paths and patios | 100 mm | 1.0 | ~1.25 m³ loose |
| Base under a driveway | 150 mm | 1.5 | ~1.9 m³ loose |
| Bedding sand | 30 mm | 0.3 | 0.3 m³ (1 m³ covers about 33 m²) |
| Turf underlay soil | 100 mm | 1.0 | 1.1 m³ |
| Garden bed amendment | 300 mm worked in | varies | measure the bed area, not the block |
| Mulch | 50–100 mm | 0.5–1.0 | 1.0 m³ |

Perth yards sell soil, mulch and sand by the cubic metre and often price
aggregate and road base by the tonne as well. Ask which. A cubic metre of damp
sand is roughly one and a half tonnes, so the two prices are not comparable and
mixing them up is a classic 30% error.

## Linear metres and counts

Edging, pipe, cable and timber are measured along the centre-line of the run,
not the straight-line distance. Add 5% for the wander, then round up to whole
supplied lengths — poly comes in fixed coils, aluminium edging in fixed
sections. Nobody sells 47.3 m of anything.

For [irrigation](/curriculum/documentation/irrigation-plan/), measure mainline
and laterals separately, add 300 mm per riser, and measure valve wire along the
trench route with a 1 m tail at each end.

Counts come straight off the schedules. Plants get counted by pot size, because
that is how a nursery sells them — a 140 mm tube and a 45 L specimen of the same
species are different line items. Count sprinklers, drippers, lights, fixings,
blocks and caps as separate items. Retaining blocks are sold per block in Perth,
not per square metre, so you need the block dimensions before you can convert
wall area into a count.

## Renovation: measure what leaves

Go back to your [existing garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/)
and turn every REMOVE and REPLACE verdict into a volume and a weight.

Rubble is heavy. A cubic metre of broken concrete or limestone is close to two
tonnes, and most skip bins have a weight cap you will hit well before the bin
looks full. Separating clean fill from mixed rubbish is worth real money,
because the WA waste levy is charged per tonne or per cubic metre of what goes
to landfill in the metropolitan area. Confirm bin sizes, weight limits and
whether your operator takes segregated loads before you book.

Then add the things that never appear on a drawing: sand for backfill, wetting
agent, stakes and ties, fixings, hire, and establishment water under a
[watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
if you are laying turf.

## The second pass

Do it on a different day, by a different route. If the first pass went area by
area, do the second by trade. Then sanity-check the totals: your hard surfaces,
garden beds and lawn should add up to roughly the block area minus the house
footprint. If they do not, something is missing or double-counted, and it is
much cheaper to find it now.
