You have quantities from your drawings, prices for the materials, and a list of the
costs beginners forget. This lesson assembles them into one number, in the order a
professional assembles it, so that when the total comes out higher than you hoped you
can see exactly where the money sits and which lever to pull.

One rule before you start. A price is built from the bottom up: real quantities at real
rates, added until you reach a total, never worked backwards from what you wish it
cost. Cutting scope in response to a total you cannot afford is legitimate. Cutting the
estimate is not, and it is the most common way a home project ends half-finished.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper does not price a garden. They price a stack, in a fixed order, and each
layer sits on the one below it.

| Layer | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Direct costs** | Materials with wastage, labour hours, plant hire, subcontractor quotes, disposal |
| **2. Site costs** | Establishment, fencing, temporary water and power, protection of what stays, skip, travel, fuel |
| **3. Statutory costs** | Permits, levies, insurance, surveyor, service locating |
| **4. Overhead recovery** | Ute, insurance, tools, software, quoting time, admin, accountant |
| **5. Margin** | The profit the business runs on |
| **6. Contingency** | The allowance for what is not yet known |
| **7. GST** | 10% on top, unless the operator is under the $75,000 turnover threshold |

Layers 1 to 3 are the same for you as for them. Layers 4 and 5 disappear if you build
it yourself, which is where the DIY saving genuinely comes from. Layer 6 does not
disappear. It gets bigger, because you have less experience of what goes wrong.

Two pieces of arithmetic separate people who price well from people who go broke.

**Markup is not margin.** Adding 20% to a $10,000 cost gives $12,000, of which the
profit is $2,000 — a margin of 16.7%, not 20%. To actually make a 20% margin you divide
by 0.8 and charge $12,500. Landscapers who confuse the two lose several percent on
every job for years without noticing.

**Materials are about a third of a quote.** The [ATO's benchmarks for landscape
construction](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/income-deductions-and-concessions/small-business-benchmarks/in-detail/landscape-construction),
built from actual tax returns for 2023–24, put cost of sales at 27% to 38% of turnover
for businesses over $500,000, labour at 24% to 34%, and total expenses averaging 81%.
So a $40,000 quote is roughly $13,000 of materials, $12,000 of labour, and the rest
carrying vehicles, insurance, tools, quoting time, admin — and the owner's own income.
That last slice is what people try to negotiate away, and a contractor with nothing
left in it is a contractor who cannot afford to come back and fix the wall.

## Pricing each layer honestly

**Materials.** Price against the [take-off](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/),
including wastage, and use the units the supplier actually sells in — half a load of
limestone still costs a full delivery. Delivery, minimum order quantities and the
second delivery you will need are covered in
[Perth material prices](/curriculum/costing/perth-material-prices/).

**Labour.** Every task gets an hour estimate, whether or not you are paying for those
hours. The Fair Work minimum wage of $26.44 per hour from 1 July 2026 is the legal
floor for an employee, not a charge-out rate: a real subcontract rate carries
superannuation, leave, workers compensation, public liability, vehicle, tools and the
unbilled hours spent quoting and travelling.

**Subcontractors.** Get a written quote, not a verbal estimate, and get three of them.
[Consumer Protection WA is explicit](https://www.consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/hiring-tradespeople-0)
that an accepted quote is a binding contract while an estimate can move, and that a
tradesperson cannot do extra work without your approval. A quote must name the ABN, the
scope, materials, labour, timeframe and terms.

**Disposal.** Cost it by weight and by type, from a published schedule.
[Tamala Park's 2026/27 fees](https://www.mrc.wa.gov.au/tamala-park/disposal/fees-charges.aspx)
are $291.50 per tonne including GST for general waste and for green waste, with a
$29.15 minimum for up to 100 kg, and $450 per tonne for asbestos. Sitting inside those
gate fees is the [state waste levy](https://www.wa.gov.au/service/environment/environment-information-services/waste-levy-rate-schedule),
$90 per tonne from 1 July 2026 and rising every July. Old concrete, limestone blocks
and sand are heavy: a small demolition can be a four-figure disposal line on its own.

**Statutory costs.** The [CTF construction training levy](https://ctf.wa.gov.au/levy/)
is 0.2% of the value of construction and is paid by you, the owner, before work starts.
Its threshold rose from $20,000 to $100,000 on 1 July 2026, and soft landscaping is
exempt — so it now catches large hardscape jobs and little else. Add council
development application and building permit fees; those vary by local government, so
confirm the current figures with your council rather than guessing. See
[structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/).

## Safety is a cost line, not an attitude

Put money against these before you put money against plants. They are the lines people
delete first and regret hardest.

- **Cutting pavers, limestone, concrete or tile releases respirable crystalline
  silica.** Budget for on-tool water suppression or dust extraction and P2 respiratory
  protection, or for a supplier who cuts to size off site. Dry-cutting limestone in your
  driveway with a demo saw is how people get silicosis.
- **Trenches and excavations collapse**, and Perth sand has no cohesion at all when
  dry. Battering back, shoring or a shorter open time all cost money and hours.
- **Electrical work near water or in the ground requires a licensed electrician, and
  plumbing connections a licensed plumber.** Not optional. Both belong in the plan as
  subcontractor quotes.
- **Asbestos.** Pre-1990 fibro sheds and super six fencing are everywhere in Perth. Over
  10 square metres of non-friable material is licensed removal work — see
  [auditing an existing garden](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/).
- **Working alone in a Perth summer.** Heat is why a two-day job becomes four in
  February. Price the hours you will achieve, not the hours in the day.

## Contingency, with real numbers

Contingency is not padding and it is not optimism. It is the priced allowance for things
you have not yet found out. That is why the percentage is set by how well defined your
project is, not by how confident you feel.

The Queensland transport department publishes the ranges its estimators must use.
Mapped onto where a garden project sits, they look like this:

| Where your project is | Definition | Contingency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An idea, no drawings | Under 2% | Over 70% |
| Rough scope, sketch on paper | 2–10% | 40–70% |
| Options compared, concept plan chosen | 10–25% | 40–70% |
| Concept locked, costed by rate | 10–40% | 30–40% |
| Design developed, most details resolved | 40–65% | 20–30% |
| **Fully documented, take-off complete — you, now** | 65–85% | **10–20%** |

The same manual sets the matching accuracy expectation: a concept estimate should land
within plus or minus 40%, a fully documented one within plus or minus 10%. That is your
answer when this total differs from the budget you set in
[the money conversation](/curriculum/brief/the-money-conversation/). A 20% to 30%
difference is normal, because that budget was a concept-stage number. A 200% difference
means the brief and the design were never reconciled.

Take **15% as your starting point** and move within the 10% to 20% band on evidence:

- Push **up** for a renovation with services you have not located, unknown fill,
  suspected asbestos, a retaining wall of unknown construction, no test hole, or a stage
  you have priced from a rate rather than a quote.
- Push **down** only where a subcontractor has given a fixed written quote for a fully
  specified scope, or where the material is already on site.

Then write the risks down beside the number. Contingency is drawn against named
events — limestone at 400 mm, an unmarked conduit under the paving, a footing found
where the deck goes — not spent on a nicer barbecue. If a risk resolves in your favour,
that money is released, not automatically re-spent.

## Closing the gap

If the total exceeds your Phase 1 budget, there are four honest levers: reduce scope,
[stage the project](/curriculum/costing/staging-the-project/), substitute materials, or
[do more of it yourself](/curriculum/costing/diy-vs-subcontract/). There are two
dishonest ones: cutting the contingency, and cutting base preparation, drainage or
services because they will be buried and nobody will see them. Both are loans from your
future self at a very poor interest rate.

garden usable, land the planting in autumn, and never require digging up something you
have already paid for.
