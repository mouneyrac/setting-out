You now have nine lessons' worth of findings about your block, and they are
scattered. A base plan in one file. Spot levels in a notebook. Photographs of a
soil hole. A Before You Dig response pack as a PDF. A spreadsheet of every plant
with a keep-or-kill verdict against it.

Nobody can design from that, including you in three weeks' time.

This lesson turns all of it into one drawing. In practice it is the last thing
that happens in site analysis and the first thing that gets opened in concept
design, and the quality of everything downstream is limited by how honest it is.

## What a professional actually does here

In a landscape architecture practice this drawing is a numbered, issued,
billable deliverable. It goes to the client before a single design idea is
shown, and it exists for four reasons.

**It converts findings into spatial facts.** "The soil is sand over limestone at
400 mm" is a note. A hatched zone across the northern third of the block reading
*limestone at 400 mm — excavation cost, restricted rooting depth* is something
you can design around, because it has a location.

**It surfaces the collisions early.** The moment everything is on one sheet, the
conflicts appear: the only level, sunny, sheltered corner is directly over the
sewer junction. The tree you want to keep sits where the machinery has to come
through. Finding that on paper costs an afternoon. Finding it mid-build costs
thousands.

**It is the document you argue from.** When the client wants a pool on the west
boundary and the drawing shows the setback, the drainage low point and the
neighbour's overlooking window, the conversation is about facts rather than
taste.

**It records what was known.** If a constraint is on the drawing and the client
chose to proceed anyway, that is documented. This is not paranoia; it is the
difference between a professional practice and a bloke with a bobcat.

The framing that matters most: **constraints are not obstacles, they are the
other half of the brief.** A design that appears to fit its site effortlessly is
almost always one where every constraint was known before the pen moved.

worse than none, because you will design straight into the hole.

## How to draw it

### Work in overlays, then flatten

Print your base plan at 1:100 — several copies, and never draw on the original.
Then build it up in layers on tracing paper or as separate layers in whatever
software you are using, roughly four:

1. **Legal and services** — boundaries, setbacks, easements, sewer, water,
   power, gas, retic.
2. **Environment** — sun and shade, wind, water movement, soil zones.
3. **Existing garden** — retained plants, tree protection zones, surfaces to
   keep, structures to demolish.
4. **Operations** — access routes, stockpile areas, spoil, crossover.

Then flatten to one sheet. The flattening is where the thinking happens, because
that is where you find out which layers collide.

### Use a consistent graphic language

| Convention | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Red | Constraint |
| Green | Opportunity |
| Orange or a "?" flag | Assumed, not yet verified |
| Solid line | Absolute — cannot move |
| Dashed line | Negotiable, or subject to approval |
| Hatched area | A zone, not a line |
| Arrow | Direction — wind, view, fall, access |

The hatching rule is the one people get wrong. A tree protection zone, a sewer
clearance, afternoon shade and a drainage low point are all *areas*. Drawn as
lines they invite you to design right up to them.

Every version gets a north arrow, a scale, and a date. Site analysis has a
shelf life — a neighbour builds a two-storey and your shade drawing is wrong.

### Annotate with facts, not adjectives

"Hot" is useless. "West-facing rendered wall, 2.4 m, in full sun from 13:00,
still radiating at 19:00 in February" tells the designer to plant a deciduous
screen, build a pergola, or move the seating.

Every annotation should carry a number, a time, a direction or a depth. If it
carries none of those, you have written an opinion.

## The WA layers people forget

Generic site analysis advice will not prompt you for these. On a Perth block
they are often the expensive ones.

- **Depth to limestone or coffee rock**, drawn as a zone. It sets your
  excavation method, your machinery, your drainage and your tree choices.
- **Side access, measured at the pinch point.** Not the gate width — the
  narrowest point between the house and the fence, usually where the meter box
  or the aircon condenser sits. Note overhead clearance too (eaves, the service
  line to the house). This single number decides bobcat versus wheelbarrow, and
  it is a multiplier on your labour cost in Phase 7.
- **The verge and crossover.** Where materials can be stockpiled, where a truck
  can stand, and whether there is a council street tree with its own protection
  requirements.
- **The Fremantle Doctor.** The afternoon south-westerly is an opportunity, and
  a solid 1.8 m screen on your south-west boundary throws it away. Draw it as an
  arrow, in green.
- **Winter sun into the house.** The northern aspect is the one asset you cannot
  buy. Mark where winter sun reaches the living areas and do not plant an
  evergreen in it.
- **Bushfire prone designation**, if you are on the fringe — Roleystone, the
  hills suburbs, the bushland edges out north. It is a yes/no flag here and a
  Phase 4 problem.
- **Overlooking**, drawn as a cone from the neighbour's upper window, not a dot.

## Mark the opportunities, not just the problems

This is the half that gets skipped, and it is why amateur plans read as
defensive. Work through the constraint list and ask what each one *enables*.

- Deep summer shade under a retained marri or jacaranda is the only place anyone
  will sit in February. That is the outdoor room, already located.
- The area you cannot build over because of the soakwell is a perfectly good
  lawn or low garden bed.
- A north-facing wall is a winter sun trap and a place for espalier or citrus.
- The one genuinely level area is the cheapest square metres on the site — that
  is where the large paved space belongs unless there is a reason otherwise.
- A mature tree is fifteen years you cannot purchase.

## Reading it like a designer

When the drawing is finished, interrogate it with the three questions a designer
asks:

1. **Where is the site telling me to put the main outdoor space?** Usually the
   intersection of shelter, level ground, summer shade, winter sun and short
   travel from the kitchen door. Often there is only one such place, and the
   drawing hands it to you.
2. **What can never move?** Services, retained trees, boundaries, and levels
   against the house. Everything else is negotiable.
3. **Where is the money?** Which parts of this site cost three times as much per
   square metre — limestone, steep fall, no machine access — and is the design
   putting the expensive work there for a good reason?

If you cannot walk someone through the drawing in five minutes, it is either
incomplete or over-drawn. Both are fixable in one more pass.

Phase 4 takes each hard constraint and asks whether it triggers an approval —

Then Phase 5 puts tracing paper over this exact sheet and starts drawing
bubbles. [Functional zoning](/curriculum/concept-design/functional-zoning/) is

you spend here is the cheapest design work you will ever do.
