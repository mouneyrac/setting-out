You have a hole, a colour, and a depth to something hard. That is the
[Perth soils lesson](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) done. This lesson
turns those observations into numbers you can cost from.

Four tests, all cheap, none needing a laboratory — although there is one
situation where a laboratory is worth the money, and this lesson says when. When
you get to Phase 7 and price amendment by the cubic metre, these numbers are
what stop you guessing.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper does not send soil to a laboratory on every residential job,
because field tests and laboratory tests answer different questions and only one
set changes the design.

Field tests — repellency, depth, drainage — change what you build: whether the
irrigation cycles, whether a tree pit needs breaking out, whether a bed needs
raising. Laboratory tests mostly change what you feed the garden afterwards, a
maintenance decision you can revise cheaply later. So field-test everything, and
laboratory-test only where something expensive or edible is at stake: a large
lawn, a vegetable garden, a $4,000 specimen tree, or a spot where the last three
plantings have died.

The second habit is **test by zone, not by block**. A renovation site is never
uniform. The strip against the house is builder's fill; the bed under the old
jacaranda has thirty years of leaf litter in it; the side where someone parked a
trailer is compacted to 300 mm. Use the same zone names you used when you
[measured up](/curriculum/site-analysis/measuring-up/).

## Test 1 — The water-drop test

The most important test in the lesson, and it takes ninety seconds.

Find bare soil. Scrape off the mulch and the top 5 mm — the waxes that cause
repellency concentrate right at the surface and you want the layer just under
them. The soil must be genuinely dry, which in Perth means January to April;
testing damp soil in August tells you nothing. Put ten drops of water on the
surface from an eyedropper and time how long they take to disappear.

| Penetration time | Severity | What you do about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 seconds or less | Non-repellent | Nothing. Note it and move on. |
| 10–60 seconds | Low | Wetting agent annually, good mulch cover. |
| Over 60 seconds | Moderate | Wetting agent twice a year, and irrigation designed to cycle and soak. |
| Minutes to hours | Severe to very severe | Clay and organic matter incorporated, not just a surface treatment. This is a real line item. |

Very repellent soil can take longer than three hours to take the droplet in. Do
not stand there — mark the spot, set a timer, come back.

The failure mode this predicts is everywhere in Perth: the sprinklers run, the
surface looks wet, water runs sideways or channels down a few preferential
paths, and the plant dies of drought in what appears to be a watered garden.
Repellency severity feeds the wetting agent programme in
[turning Perth sand into soil](/curriculum/build/turning-perth-sand-into-soil/)
and the run times in
[irrigation scheduling](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/).

## Test 2 — pH, and why your number may be misleading

Take five or six subsamples per zone from about 100 mm depth, mix them in a
bucket, and test the mix. Do not sample within a few weeks of fertilising or
liming, and stay a metre away from limestone walls, concrete paths and anywhere
mortar was cut — those spots read a full unit higher than the bed two metres
away.

Two pH scales are in circulation and confusing them is the standard mistake. WA
laboratories report pH in a weak calcium chloride solution; garden kits give a
water-based reading, roughly 0.7 units higher. Whichever you use, **write down
which one it was**. Most plants sit happily between 5.5 and 7.5 on the CaCl2
scale. On the coastal plain, grey Bassendean sand is often mildly acidic and
rarely the limiting factor, while anything over limestone is alkaline and
coastal calcareous sand strongly so.

The honest professional position on alkaline sand over limestone is that you do
not fix it. Carbonate buffers the soil — you can drop the pH inside a raised bed
of imported mix, but not across a whole garden at a price worth paying. The
consequence is iron and manganese lock-out, showing up eighteen months later as
yellow leaves with green veins on gardenias, citrus, camellias and magnolias. So
this number goes into your
[planting plan](/curriculum/documentation/planting-plan-and-schedule/) rather
than your soil budget.

## Test 3 — Depth to a hard layer

You dug one hole in the last lesson. Now cover the block. A 1.2 m length of
12 mm steel bar driven with a hammer gives you refusal depth in seconds; probe
on a rough 3 m grid wherever you intend to plant or excavate, and record both
the depth and what stopped the bar.

- **Limestone** rings and does not give. A crowbar bounces.
- **Coffee rock** is dark and cemented but crumbles when you hit it.
- **Compaction** stops the bar, then lets it through with more effort. On a
  renovation this is the most common finding and the most misdiagnosed — old
  driveways, scaffold runs and brickie's sand leave a plate at 150–300 mm that
  people call clay. It is not clay. It is a machine.

Under about 400 mm to rock, medium and large trees need the pit broken out
mechanically; 400–800 mm, you can plant trees but should price rock breaking per
pit. Compaction is usually rippable — a labour cost, not a machinery cost. The
free DPIRD
[NRInfo soil-landscape mapping](https://www.dpird.wa.gov.au/environment-and-sustainability/nrinfo-for-western-australia/)
is a cross-check, but trust the steel bar over the map: regional mapping is not
drawn at the scale of a 600 m² lot.

## Test 4 — Drainage rate

Dig a hole roughly 300 mm across and 300 mm deep in each major zone. Fill it,
let it drain away completely, then fill it again — the first fill wets the
surrounding soil so the second measures the soil rather than its thirst. Measure
the drop over ten minutes and convert to millimetres per hour.

Deep Perth sand normally empties absurdly fast. If a zone drains slowly you have
found something worth money: a compacted plate, coffee rock perching water, a
clay lens, or a high winter watertable.

Two Perth warnings. A summer test is optimistic — water tables on parts of the
coastal plain rise substantially through winter, and a hole that empties in two
minutes in February can hold water in July. And this number decides whether
soakwells will work on your block, so take it through to
[water on site](/curriculum/site-analysis/water-on-site/) and the
[drainage plan](/curriculum/documentation/drainage-plan/).

## The nutrition question, and when to pay for a laboratory

Perth sand holds very little of anything. The WA-specific measurement worth
asking for is the **Phosphorus Retention Index**, because deep sandy soils are
the exception to the rule that phosphorus stays where you put it — here it
[leaches](https://www.soilquality.org.au/factsheets/phosphorus), which is why
[SERCUL's Fertilise Wise programme](https://www.sercul.org.au/fertilisewise/)
tells Perth turf managers to measure PRI before planning a fertiliser regime.
More fertiliser is not the fix; more retention is, via clay and organic matter
in Phase 8. And many WA proteaceous natives, banksias in particular, are
phosphorus-sensitive, so a standard high-P lawn food broadcast near them is a
slow execution.

If you send a sample away, ask for pH in CaCl2, organic carbon, Colwell
phosphorus, PRI and electrical conductivity. On an older block where you plan to
grow food, screen for metals as well — old painted walls shed lead, and parts of
Perth carry a market-garden history. VegeSafe does that for a $20 donation, and
DWER's free contaminated sites map is a five-minute check before you dig.

## What this feeds

Repellency sets the wetting agent programme and the irrigation run times. pH
sets the plant palette. Depth to hard layer sets excavation method and tree
selection. Drainage rate sets the drainage design.
