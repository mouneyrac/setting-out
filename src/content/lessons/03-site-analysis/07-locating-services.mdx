There is a particular sound a 5-tonne excavator makes when the bucket finds a
sewer, and everyone on site knows what it was before the operator has finished
the swing. The gas main version is quieter and much worse.

On a renovation this risk is higher than on a new block, not lower. A bare
subdivision lot has services installed to a recent standard with an as-built
plan that reflects it. A 1970s house in Morley has forty years of undocumented
private work under the lawn: a retic mainline someone ran in 1988, a bore power
cable at spade depth, a pool pump feed, a decommissioned septic tank, and a
sewer junction nowhere near where you would put it. This lesson gets all of it
onto your base plan before you draw a line you might have to erase.

## What a professional does at this stage

A competent landscape contractor treats service location as a document trail,
not a hunch. Before quoting they will have lodged a Before You Dig Australia
enquiry, read the plan pack from every responding asset owner, walked the site
looking for surface evidence, and priced potholing into the job. If the design
touches a sewer they raise it at concept stage, not during excavation.

The reason is partly legal. Under the Work Health and Safety Act 2020 (WA) and
the [Work Health and Safety (General) Regulations
2022](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/law_s53267.html),
regulation 304 covers "Excavation work: underground essential services
information", and it sits in the part of the regulations that imposes duties on
a person conducting a business or undertaking. In plain terms: the moment you
engage a landscaper, a bobcat operator or a plumber, that duty is live on your
site — and you are the one holding the site knowledge they need. If you are
digging alone on your own house, whether the WHS duty bites is arguable.
Whether you pay for the repair is not.

The rest is commercial. Moving a retaining wall 600 mm on paper costs nothing.
Moving it after the footing is poured costs the footing.

## Lodging the BYDA enquiry

Do this first, because it is free and it takes ten minutes. Register at
[byda.com.au](https://www.byda.com.au/before-you-dig/for-homeowners/), draw your
dig area on the map, and lodge. Every asset owner with registered infrastructure
near that polygon is notified and each replies to you directly.

Plans usually arrive within minutes, but allow up to two business days — lodge
before the weekend, not the morning you hire the mini-ex. Note that BYDA itself
sends you nothing; the plans come from Water Corporation, Western Power, ATCO
Gas and the comms carriers, so you will get several emails with different
formats and different conditions attached.

### What the plans will not tell you

Three limitations matter more than anything the plans do say.

| Limitation | What it means on your block |
| --- | --- |
| Plans are indicative only | The line on the page is a schematic, not a survey. Treat it as "somewhere near here". |
| Coverage generally stops at the boundary | Mains and lead-ins are shown. What runs across your back lawn often is not. |
| Depth is never guaranteed | Sand moves, levels get changed, kerbs get rebuilt. Never assume a cable is deep. |

That third one is a Perth specific. Our sand is easy to trench, and it is just
as easy to strip 200 mm off a lawn during a renovation without anyone recording
it. A cable buried to spec in 1985 may be 150 mm down today.

## The sewer, and why you cannot build over it

Most Perth blocks have a gravity sewer running from the house to a main, and on
many older lots that main crosses private property. Water Corporation names
sewer reticulation pipes of 300 mm and smaller as the most common asset found
inside private property.

You need approval before building a house, shed, garage, carport, pool, spa or
retaining wall near one, and the clearance depends on how deep the sewer is and
what it is made of. There is a stricter set of distances for conventional
footings and a closer set if you pile past the pipe's zone of influence — but
piling means a structural engineer, drawings and cost, which is usually the
moment a client decides the pergola can move two metres.

Walk the block and look for:

- **Access chambers** — a round or square cover, often cast into a path or lost
  under mulch. It must stay accessible and at finished level, so it constrains
  your paving levels as well as your plan.
- **The inspection opening** near the house, usually a capped riser at ground
  level close to the wet areas.
- **A straight line of settlement or greener grass** between the two.

If the block is old enough to predate infill sewerage, or is semi-rural, it may
still be on a septic tank and leach drains. Those cannot be paved or built over
either, and they are rarely on any plan. Ask the previous owner and look for the
tank lids.

## Power, gas, comms and the bore

**Power.** Western Power requires you to know the location and voltage of any
underground cable before working near it, and points you to BYDA or freecall
1100 for that information. If you expose a cable that is not on your plans, or
find damaged insulation, stop and ring 13 10 87. Look up as well as down —
overhead consumer mains from the pole to the house are a real constraint on
where a tall tree or a pergola can go.

**Gas.** Perth metro reticulated gas is ATCO. Gas services are commonly shallow,
and a nicked polyethylene service is an evacuation, not an inconvenience. Hand
dig near anything the gas plan shows.

**Comms.** NBN and Telstra pits are usually in the verge, which matters because
verge landscaping and driveway crossovers sit right on top of them.

**The bore.** The WA one nobody warns you about. If the property has a garden
bore, a power cable runs from the meter box to the pump at whatever depth the
person who installed it felt like, and it will not be on any BYDA plan because
it is a private asset. Find the pump, find the meter box, and assume the cable
takes the shortest ugly route between them until you have proved otherwise.

## Your existing retic

No asset owner has a plan for this, so you make one. Run each station from the
controller and watch where the heads pop — that gives you zones and rough
alignments. Solenoid valves are the hard part; they get buried and mulched over.
A wire and valve locator, hireable by the day from most Perth irrigation shops,
traces the control wire from the controller straight to the solenoid.

Mark everything with pegs and spray paint, photograph it against fixed reference
points from your [measuring-up survey](/curriculum/site-analysis/measuring-up/),
and get it onto the base plan the same day. The
[irrigation lessons in Phase 8](/curriculum/build/irrigation-mainline-valves-and-wiring/)
assume you know what is already in the ground.

## Pothole before you commit

Plans get you close; only exposing the service tells you where it is. Hand dig
or soft-dig at every point where your design crosses a located service —
footings, soakwells, tree pits, mainline trenches. In Perth sand this is quick,
which removes the usual excuse. Record the depth and the offset from a fixed
point. Those two numbers turn "there is a sewer somewhere around here" into a
line you can design to.

## What this changes downstream

Your services plan becomes a constraint layer that sits under everything in
Phase 5 and Phase 6 — where the pool can go, where the retaining wall footing
lands, where the deep-rooted tree does not go, and where the drainage falls.

The next lesson audits what is already growing and already built on the block,
[assessing the existing garden](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/),
and it uses this plan to decide what can be kept, what must move and what is
sitting on top of something you cannot disturb.
