A twenty-year-old jacaranda or a remnant tuart is the one thing on your block you
cannot buy. Paving, a pergola, turf, even a semi-mature tree in a 1000-litre bag
— all purchasable. Twenty years of root system in Perth sand is not.

That is what makes tree assessment different from the rest of site analysis.
Everything else you record is a fact you can check later. A tree has a delay
built into it: the works happen in autumn, it looks fine through winter and
spring, and it starts thinning out over the second summer after that. By then
the bobcat is long gone and nobody connects the two.

## What a professional does at this stage

Before any concept sketch, a landscaper or landscape architect numbers every
tree worth keeping, measures it, calculates its root zones, and draws those
zones on the base plan as circles at scale. Then the design gets drawn around
them.

That order is the whole point. Amateurs design the garden, then discover the
retaining wall runs through a root plate and hope for the best. Professionals
treat a retained tree as a fixed obstruction — like a sewer line or a boundary —
and shape the design to it. If the design genuinely cannot work around the tree,
that becomes a decision to remove it, made deliberately in Phase 3 with the cost
and the approval understood, rather than an accident discovered in Phase 8.

The second thing a professional does is decide, tree by tree, whether they are
qualified to make the call at all.

## Measuring the tree

Take a tape around the trunk at 1.4 m above ground and divide the circumference
by 3.14 to get the diameter. If the trunk forks below 1.4 m, measure each stem —
multi-stem trees are handled differently, and that is where a calculator earns
its keep. AS 4970:2025 calls this measurement DSH; the 2009 edition and most
council paperwork still call it DBH. Same thing.

### The two circles that matter

**Notional root zone (NRZ).** The formula-derived circle — radius in metres is
roughly twelve times the trunk diameter in metres, with a minimum of 2 m and a
cap at 15 m. This is the area you plan around. In the 2025 standard the tree
protection zone is the physical fenced area implemented on site, which an
arborist sets using the NRZ as the starting point.

| Trunk diameter at 1.4 m | Notional root zone radius | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 150 mm | 2.0 m (minimum applies) | A young street tree |
| 300 mm | 3.6 m | A 15-year-old jacaranda |
| 500 mm | 6.0 m | A solid established shade tree |
| 800 mm | 9.6 m | A mature marri or tuart |
| 1250 mm and above | 15 m (cap applies) | A significant remnant tree |

**Structural root zone (SRZ).** A much smaller circle, calculated from trunk
diameter at ground level, minimum 1.5 m radius. These are the roots holding the
tree upright. Cutting into the NRZ risks the tree's health; cutting into the SRZ
risks the tree falling on your house.

Encroachment into the NRZ is classified as minor at 10% or less, moderate
between 10% and 20%, and major above 20% or anywhere it touches the SRZ.
Anything past minor needs an arborist to sign off on how it is done.

## The works that kill a tree two years later

Almost none of these look destructive on the day.

- **Trenching for retic, power or drainage.** A 300 mm trench across the root
  zone can sever a large share of a shallow-rooted tree's absorbing roots. A
  directional bore or a hand-dug radial trench costs an hour more and does a
  fraction of the damage.
- **Raising the soil level.** Two hundred millimetres of imported loam and new
  turf over the root zone is one of the most reliable tree killers going. Roots
  need air; low-permeability soil over the top suffocates them slowly.
- **Compaction.** A bobcat doing three-point turns over the root zone, a sand
  stockpile, a brick pallet, a site shed. Compacted sand does not recover on its
  own.
- **Sealing the surface.** Liquid limestone is very common in Perth and goes down
  as a continuous slab — a driveway widened to the fence line can cover most of a
  verge tree's root zone in one pour.
- **Washing out.** Concrete slurry, render slop and brick-cleaning acid tipped
  onto the ground near the trunk — a site-management failure, covered in
  [site establishment and safety](/curriculum/build/site-establishment-and-safety/).
- **Changing the water.** A tree that has spent fifteen years drinking spray
  overrun from a lawn will not survive that lawn becoming dripline natives on a
  two-day roster unless it gets a zone of its own.

## The WA specifics

### Council rules genuinely vary — do not take a rule of thumb

There is no state-wide rule about removing a tree on your own land in WA. Three
Perth councils, three different answers, all verified:

| Council | Approval needed to remove a tree on private land? |
| --- | --- |
| City of South Perth | Yes, for a Regulated Tree — 8.0 m or more high, and/or 6.0 m average canopy, and/or 1.5 m trunk circumference at 1.4 m |
| City of Stirling | Only if the tree is on the Significant Tree Register, which the owner has to nominate it for |
| City of Vincent | No — the City treats it as a civil matter between neighbours |

So the only correct answer for your block is the one from your own council. Ring
the planning counter, give them the address and the trunk circumference, and get
it in writing. While you are there, ask whether the property carries a section
70A notification — in Stirling a registered tree binds the title of every lot
containing its structural root zone, so a neighbour's tree can obligate you.

Verge trees are separate and simpler: they belong to the council everywhere in
Perth. Do not prune, poison or remove one, and check before you propose a new
crossover. That intersects with [approvals](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/).

### Polyphagous shot-hole borer

Since 2021 the metropolitan area has been under a Quarantine Area Notice for
this beetle. Practically, that governs where prunings, stumps and mulch can go,
what size material has to be chipped to, and disinfecting pruning tools between
trees. If you are removing a host tree, the disposal path is part of the job now,
and a contractor who is casual about it is telling you something. See also
[WA pests and diseases](/curriculum/maintenance/wa-pests-and-diseases/).

### Perth sand changes the root map

The textbook circle assumes roots spread evenly. On the Swan Coastal Plain they
follow water and air — shallow, wide, and concentrated wherever the ground stays
cool and damp: under paving, along old retic lines, under the mulched bed you
were about to excavate. On a Karrakatta profile with limestone at 400 mm the root
plate is flatter and wider than the radius suggests, because there is nowhere to
go but sideways. Check what you found in
[Perth soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) against the trees you are
keeping.

## When to get an arborist

Pay for a consulting arborist if the council protects the tree and you need a
report to support an application; if any excavation, footing, wall or paving
falls more than about 10% inside the NRZ or touches the SRZ at all; if the tree
overhangs a structure and you are unsure it is sound; if a neighbour's tree is
involved and the conversation is heading somewhere legal; or if you are weighing
up removing something large and want an independent opinion on whether it can be
kept.

Ask for AQF Level 5. That is the consulting qualification and it is what WA
council policies specify. AQF Level 3 is the climbing and pruning ticket —
excellent for doing the work, not the person to tell you whether the tree
survives your driveway. Prefer a consultant who does not also sell removals.

A useful report gives you the tree's health, structure and useful life
expectancy, the SRZ and NRZ dimensions, and a method for protecting it through
the works: fencing position, ground protection, supervision points, and what is
not allowed inside the fence.
