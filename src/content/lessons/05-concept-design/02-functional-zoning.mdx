You have a brief that says what the garden must do and a
[constraints and opportunities plan](/curriculum/site-analysis/constraints-and-opportunities-plan/)
that says what the site will allow. Functional zoning is where those two documents
collide. Before any shape, any material, any plant: which part of this block does
which job, and how big does that job need to be.

The tool is a bubble diagram — blobs and arrows on tracing paper, drawn in eight
minutes and thrown away. It is deliberately ugly, because anything that looks
finished stops you changing it. The whole value of this hour is changing your mind
cheaply, five times at a desk, rather than once on site with the paving already cut.

Renovating makes it harder and more valuable. You already know where everything is,
so the hand wants to trace what is there and improve it. Zoning forces the real
question: is the clothesline in the best place on this block, or merely where a
previous owner put it in 1998?

## What a professional does at this stage

A designer with a functioning practice does not start with a shape. They start with a
schedule of areas, because a shape you cannot fill is a redesign and a shape that does
not fit is a fight on site. Three habits separate them from an enthusiastic amateur.

**They allocate every square metre.** The sliver between shed and fence, the 900 mm
strip beside the garage, the dead corner behind the alfresco — each gets a function or
gets deleted by moving a boundary. Unallocated space in a Perth garden becomes a weed
nursery with a coiled hose in it, because nothing was ever decided about it.

**They draw the service zones first.** Bins, clothesline, storage, side access, pool
plant, condenser, meter box, soakwell lids. These have fixed dimensions and
inconvenient rules and cannot be squeezed in at the end. Amateurs design the pretty
half and then find the bins have to live beside the dining table.

**They produce alternatives on purpose.** Not variations on one idea — genuinely
different strategies. The first arrangement you think of is almost always the
existing arrangement with better paving.

## Build the function list before you draw

Open your [design brief](/curriculum/brief/writing-the-design-brief/) and write every
function down the side of a blank page. Then add the service list below. Almost nobody
puts it in a brief, and every one of these will exist in the finished garden whether
you allocated space for it or not.

| Zone | What it actually needs | Perth notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bins** | Three bins at roughly 585 × 730 mm each. Allow 2.0 × 1.0 m clear to stand them side by side and pull the middle one out; 2.2 × 1.2 m with a screen and gate | FOGO means three bins now, not two. Keep them off a west-facing limestone wall — a lime-green food-waste bin baking in afternoon sun is an odour problem you designed in |
| **Clothesline** | A 6-line rotary needs a clear circle of about 4.2 m (3.2 m head plus 0.5 m clearance each side) and a concreted ground socket | Must be screened from the primary and secondary street. The afternoon sea breeze is a free dryer — do not screen it into stillness |
| **Trailer / boat** | Measure yours including the drawbar; a 6×4 box trailer is 1.8 × 1.2 m of box and rather more overall | Needs a straight run from the crossover and a firm surface — sand and a jockey wheel do not mix |
| **Shed / storage** | Up to 10 m² and 2.7 m wall height is the easiest planning path; larger sheds follow different rules | Bikes, mower, ladder, cushions, pool chemicals. On a renovation this is usually the zone that is currently a pile |
| **Side access** | Measure the narrowest pinch point in millimetres. A bin wants about 800 mm; a mini loader is 750–900 mm; a 1–1.7 t excavator is 1,000–1,300 mm | This number governs every future load of sand, spoil and mulch. See [machinery](/curriculum/build/machinery/) |
| **Compost / worm farm** | 1 × 1 m, shaded, close enough to the kitchen that you will actually walk to it | Full afternoon sun cooks a worm farm in a Perth January |
| **Plant and services** | Pool pump and filter, condenser, hot water unit, gas bottles, meter box, soakwell lids and inspection openings | Soakwell lids must stay accessible. Do not zone a shed slab over one — see [drainage plan](/curriculum/documentation/drainage-plan/) |

Tick each function off as it lands on a diagram. Anything still unticked at the end of
the hour is a function you have decided to live without — and you should know you
decided it.

## Four WA rules that shape the bubbles

These are deemed-to-comply standards in the R-Codes. Failing one does not make the
work illegal; it means your council assesses the application against design principles
instead, with discretion, delay and fees.

1. **The outdoor living area.** 30 m² at R20 and R25, behind the street setback,
   directly accessible from the primary living space, no dimension under 4 m, and at
   least **two-thirds without permanent roof cover**. That last clause is the one
   renovations trip over: roof more than a third with a new patio and it fails.
2. **A tree, with real soil.** At least one tree with a 2 × 2 m planting area free of
   impervious surface and roof cover. Draw that square now, not after the paving is
   set out.
3. **The front garden.** No more than 50 per cent of the street setback area may be
   impervious. Paving the whole front for parking breaches it.
4. **Outbuildings.** One small outbuilding up to 10 m² and 2.7 m wall height, outside
   the street setback areas, not eating into required open space or outdoor living
   area. Larger sheds follow the large-outbuilding standards — see
   [structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/).

Two things here are safety problems rather than design problems. Anything you place
beside an existing pool barrier — a bin store, a shed, a raised bed, a pot, a fixed
bench — can become a climbable object and break compliance; check it against
[pools and pool fencing](/curriculum/approvals/pools-and-pool-fencing/) before it goes
on the plan. And no zone gets drawn over an unlocated service; carry the marks from
[locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/) onto every sheet.

## The method: three diagrams in forty minutes

1. **Print five copies of your base plan** at scale from
   [measuring up](/curriculum/site-analysis/measuring-up/) — house, boundaries,
   crossover and retained elements only.
2. **Tape tracing paper over the top.** Use a fat marker, 5 mm chisel tip. If you can
   draw a detail with it, the pen is too fine.
3. **Draw blobs, not shapes.** Each blob is roughly the area of its function. Write
   the name and the target square metres inside it.
4. **Add arrows for movement.** Kitchen to dining. Back door to bins to verge. Gate to
   shed. Crossing arrows are a problem you will solve in
   [circulation and sightlines](/curriculum/concept-design/circulation-and-sightlines/).
5. **Stop at eight minutes.** Fresh sheet, deliberately different strategy.

Three strategies worth running on a Perth block:

- **North wall.** Living pushed hard against the northern face of the house for winter
  sun, services banished to the south side.
- **Long axis.** A service spine down one boundary, a living spine down the other,
  which suits the standard narrow rear yard.
- **Centre open.** Open ground in the middle, every function on the perimeter. Feels
  bigger, walks further.

## Testing them

Score each diagram against the same five questions, then write the answers in the
margin.

- **The bin walk.** Bin store to verge, 6:30 am, in the dark, dragging a bin that can
  weigh 96 kg full. How many steps, gates, corners and level changes?
- **Sun and shade.** Put your [sun and shade study](/curriculum/site-analysis/sun-shade-and-aspect/)
  beside it. Is the sitting zone shaded at 4 pm in January and sunny at noon in July?
  If not, the zone is wrong — you cannot fix orientation with furniture.
- **Services.** How far is each zone from water, power and a drainage point? A kitchen
  garden 25 m from the nearest tap is a garden that dies in February.
- **Machine access.** Can a machine reach the zone needing the most excavation, or is
  it all barrow work? That can be a five-figure difference on a retaining or paving
  job — price both ways when you get to
  [quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/).
- **Adjacency.** Which pairs must touch — kitchen and dining, shed and side gate?
  Which must never — bins and dining, clothesline and barbecue smoke, play area and
  driveway?

Then the test that catches the most: **colour in every square metre.** White left on
the page is unallocated space, and unallocated space is where the design fails later.

## Choose one, and write down why

Pick the diagram that answers the brief best, not the one that looks nicest — none of
them look nice. Write two or three sentences saying what it beat and on what grounds.
That paragraph is what stops you rebuilding, in six weeks, a decision you already made
properly.

The next lesson takes the arrows off these diagrams and turns them into real
circulation — path widths that work, the desire lines that will be worn into your lawn
whatever you draw, and what the neighbours can see of you while you use it.
