Perth gives you two watering days a week and one window on each of them, before
9am or after 6pm, and the same roster applies whether you are on scheme water or
a bore. That is the container. Everything you plant has to fit inside it.

The only way it fits is if plants that want the same amount of water, at the
same frequency, are grouped together and switched on together. That group is a
**hydrozone**. Josh Byrne's monitored house in Hilton defines it as
[grouping plants based on their common water and management requirements](https://joshshouse.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Landscaping-FAQs-for-Joshs-House.pdf)
so that "various hydrozones can be serviced by different irrigation stations,
and with different water sources". His suburban block runs seven of them.

Do this now, at concept stage, before the planting plan and before the concept
plan is signed off. Hydrozoning is not a plant list — it moves things. It
decides how big the lawn is, where it goes, which beds sit against the west
wall, and which parts of the block get no pipe at all.

## The failure this prevents

A solenoid valve has one run time. Every plant on that valve gets the same
depth, at the same frequency, whatever it wants.

So when a bed contains a hydrangea and an established grevillea, somebody picks
the number that keeps the hydrangea alive, because that is the plant that
visibly wilts first. The grevillea then spends a Perth February with a
permanently damp root zone in sand, which is how established waterwise and
native plants are actually lost — not to drought, to collar and root rot in
midsummer.

The other half of the trap is that the thirsty plant does not thrive either.
Once someone notices the natives dying the run time comes down, and now the
hydrangea gets a compromise that is too little for it and still too much for
everything else. There is no run time that is correct for a mixed zone, which is
why the [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/landscaping-tips) states flatly
that turf and shrub areas "should always be separated into different
hydrozones".

The arithmetic makes it obvious. Using
[Water Corporation's published rates](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/how-long-should-i-run-my-sprinklers-for),
delivering 10 mm takes 13 to 16 minutes on spray, 40 to 60 minutes on rotary and
30 to 40 minutes on dripline. Two plants with different needs on one valve are
not out by ten per cent. They are out by a factor of three.

## The four zones

| Zone | What belongs in it | Summer regime | Emitter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **High** | Lawn, vegetable beds, pots and planters, anything planted this season | Both rostered days, around 10 mm | Rotary or gear drive for lawn, dripline for beds |
| **Medium** | Exotic feature planting, hedges, fruit trees, shaded subtropicals | Both days in January and February, one day in the shoulder seasons | Dripline |
| **Low** | Established waterwise, native and Mediterranean planting | One rostered day a week, deeper and less often | Widely spaced dripline |
| **None** | Established trees, gravel courtyards, mulched paths, decking, dry verge, succulent and rockery beds | Nothing once established | No pipe at all |

Two things to be honest about.

**"None" does not mean never.** Everything needs an establishment season, and
that is a legal matter as well as a horticultural one: Water Corporation grants
a [new garden watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
of up to 42 days from planting between 1 October and 31 March, and up to 35 days
between 1 April and 30 September. Plan for temporary irrigation, or hand
watering, to a zone that will later carry none.

**The low zone has a definition, not a vibe.** Water Corporation's
[Waterwise Plants directory](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/waterwise-plants)
treats a plant as waterwise for Perth if, once established, it only needs
watering once a week in summer. If a species you want cannot meet that, it is a
medium plant and it belongs in the medium zone, however native it looks.

## What a professional does at this stage

Three habits separate a designed garden from a planted one.

**They work backwards from the window, not forwards from the plant list.** A
morning window of roughly 4:30am to 9:00am is about 270 minutes. Lawn on rotaries
is 40 to 60 minutes a station, beds on dripline 30 to 40. That is realistically
five to seven stations on one rostered day. Count the stations your zone plan
implies while it is still a pencil line — if it implies twelve, you have a design
problem now, on paper, rather than later with a trench open.

**They make the high zone small, deliberate, and where it is used.** Around
[36% of household water in WA is used outdoors](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/home/10-easy-water-saving-tips-for-the-home),
mostly through sprinklers, and the high zone is nearly all of it. It does not
just cost water — it eats run time the other zones need inside the same window.
Lawn that is walked on and played on earns its place; lawn that is looked at does
not. See [turf for Perth](/curriculum/build/turf-for-perth/) and
[designing for a Perth summer](/curriculum/concept-design/designing-for-a-perth-summer/).

**They put the unirrigated zone in on purpose.** Josh's House balances irrigated
planting against "areas of decking, gravel and mulch pathways that don't need
water", shaded so they do not become hot spaces and edged with planting so the
garden still reads as lush. Unirrigated area is free capacity in your watering
window — the cheapest square metre in the design.

## Drawing the boundaries

1. **One zone per area, no overlaps, no gaps.** Colour the whole block in. An
   area with no zone is an area nobody has decided how to water.
2. **Follow something physical.** A path, a mow strip, a wall, an edge, a change
   of level, a change of mulch. A boundary you cannot see is a boundary you
   cannot irrigate to, and in five years somebody plants straight across it.
3. **Split by aspect even when the plants are identical.** A bed against a
   west-facing brick or limestone wall takes afternoon sun plus radiant heat from
   November to February. The same plants on the south side are a different
   climate. Carry your findings from
   [sun, shade and aspect](/curriculum/site-analysis/sun-shade-and-aspect/)
   straight onto this drawing.
4. **Split by soil where the profile changes.** Shallow sand over limestone
   drains faster than an improved bed on deep grey sand — see
   [Perth soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/).
5. **Pull pots and planters out.** They dry several times faster than ground
   beds. Their own zone, or hand watering, never sharing with a bed.
6. **Measure each zone in square metres.** These areas are the input to the
   irrigation design, and estimating them later from a photo is how systems end
   up undersized.

## Renovating: reading the zones you inherited

Existing Perth gardens are almost never zoned. They were valved to suit the pipe
on the truck, and the planting arrived afterwards, one nursery trip at a time.

Overlay the station map from your
[existing garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/)
onto your new zone drawing. Every place a station crosses a zone boundary is a
re-valving line item, and far cheaper to find now than after the beds are
mulched. The signs are consistent: half a bed thriving and half sulking on the
same valve; a rotted, black-collared shrub a metre from a wilting one; lawn and
shrubs on a single front station.

Two renovation-specific traps:

- **Plants you are keeping constrain the zones.** A twenty-year-old lemon in the
  middle of what you want as a dry gravel courtyard either changes the zone or
  moves the courtyard. Decide which, and write it down.
- **Do not newly irrigate an established native or a mature tree that has never
  had it.** It has built a root system for the water it gets. Adding summer
  irrigation to that root zone is a common and expensive way to lose a specimen
  that has taken twenty years to grow.

## The seasonal layer

Zones are relative, and the whole set moves together through the year. Water
Corporation publishes the Perth seasonal adjustments as
[percentages of your summer run time](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/why-you-need-to-adjust-your-irrigation):
January and February 100%, March 80%, April 60%, October 60%, November 80%,
December 90%, with the [Sprinkler Switch-off](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/sprinkler-switch-off)
closing everything from 1 June to 31 August.

One global percentage only works if the zones are already correct relative to
each other. Get the hydrozones right once and every seasonal change for the next
decade is a single number on the controller. Get them wrong and no setting on
that controller is ever right.
