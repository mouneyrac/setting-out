You have a concept plan. The temptation is to put it in front of the household
like the reveal at the end of a renovation show and wait for the reaction. Do
not do that. A reveal asks for a verdict on the whole thing, and what you get
back is a feeling.

What you want is a structured hour that produces a written list of specific
problems while the design is still only lines. Moving a wall now costs you an
eraser. Moving it after [documentation](/curriculum/documentation/scaled-layout-plan/)
costs a redraw. Moving it after a contract is a variation with a price on it.

This is the last cheap opportunity you get.

## What a professional does at this stage

Western Australia has a formal model for judging design, and it is worth
stealing. The state runs [design review](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/department-of-planning-lands-and-heritage/design-review):
independent panels of built-environment professionals who assess a proposal
against the design principles in State Planning Policy 7.0 rather than against
anyone's taste, and whose job is to explore alternatives with the designer, not
to score them. The department's own advice is that
[review happens early in the design process](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/document-collections/state-design-review),
because that is when changing something is still cheap.

Three things transfer directly:

1. **The criteria are agreed before anyone sees a drawing.** Yours are already
   written — they are your [design brief](/curriculum/brief/writing-the-design-brief/).
2. **The reviewers did not draw it.** That is the entire value of your
   household.
3. **The advice comes back in writing, against the criteria.** Not as a
   conversation you win in the room.

Then there is the commercial discipline. A Perth firm's published design
packages include *one* round of revisions each, not unlimited rounds. Feedback
in the trade is a scheduled event with a defined end, because an open-ended
feedback loop is how a design dies of a thousand small compromises. Give
yourself the same rule: one presentation, one written feedback round, one
revised plan, sign off.

## Present in this order, and do not deviate

| Order | What | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The brief, read out loud | 10 min |
| 2 | The site analysis and the constraints | 10 min |
| 3 | The design, walked as a journey | 20 min |
| 4 | Silence, and written feedback | 15 min |

The order is the whole technique. Open with the plan and everybody spends the
session reacting to the shape of the paving, because that is the only thing
they have been given to have an opinion about.

**The brief first.** Read it out — must-haves, explicit exclusions, budget,
maintenance level, and the three things that would make the project a failure.
Then ask: is this still what we want? Anything that changes here is a change to
the brief, and that is completely legitimate. Write it down, date it, and only
then move on. What you must not allow is a brief change disguised later as a
comment on the design.

**The site analysis second.** Soil, drainage, services, setbacks, the audit
verdicts on what is already there, and the sun. In Perth the February afternoon
sun on a west-facing wall and the direction of the sea breeze shape a layout
more than taste ever will — see
[designing for a Perth summer](/curriculum/concept-design/designing-for-a-perth-summer/).
Present the constraints before the design and half the "why isn't the lawn over
there" questions are answered before they are asked.

**The design last.** Walk it as a journey rather than a tour of features. "You
come in here, the bins are behind this screen, at five o'clock in February you
would be sitting here because the wall is between you and the sun." Attach every
element out loud to a line of the brief or a constraint from the site. If you
cannot name which, you have found a decision you made for no reason — fix it
before the meeting, not during it.

## Present it on the ground, not just on paper

Print the plan at A3 at a stated scale, 1:100 or 1:50, so people can put a ruler
on it. A phone screen hides scale and everything looks generous on a phone.

Then go outside and mark it out. Marking paint or a bag of lime for edges, a
garden hose for curves, pegs and string for the alfresco and paving footprints,
and something 1.8 metres tall standing where the screening will be. Stand in the
marked-out alfresco at 4pm on a hot day. Most of the feedback you were going to
get in the lounge room evaporates, and what survives is real.

Because you are renovating, mark the **removals** too. Rope off every plant and
structure your [existing garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/)
gave a REMOVE verdict. People agree to "clearing out the shrubs" and mean
something quite different from what you meant.

## Collect the feedback in writing before you respond

Nobody's first reaction is their considered opinion, yours included.

At the end of the walk-through, hand out paper or open a shared document, and
sit in silence for fifteen minutes while everyone answers three questions:

- What in this plan does not do something the brief asked for?
- What here will you personally not use, and why?
- What am I assuming that you know to be wrong?

Then stop. Do not defend, explain or negotiate in the room. Arguing converts a
written record into a debate, and you will win the debate, which is the worst
available outcome: the objection survives, unrecorded, and resurfaces in Phase 8
as "I never liked that anyway". Collect the sheets and end the meeting.

## Sort the feedback into three piles

| Pile | Sounds like | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Misses the brief** | "You said the kids need somewhere to kick a ball and there is nowhere" | Non-negotiable. Redesign. |
| **Different choice** | "I would have done curves there" | A preference against a decision that already satisfies the brief. Answer it once, in writing, with the reason. Do not redraw. |
| **New information** | "The side gate is the only way the bins can get out" | The valuable pile. Update the site analysis, then re-test the design against it. |

The middle pile is dangerous in both directions. Cave to it and you get a design
that satisfies no stated requirement and belongs to nobody. Dismiss all of it and
you will miss the one that mattered — because "I would have done curves" is
sometimes "I am never going to use that space", which is a brief failure wearing
a taste costume.

The test that separates them is one question: **which line of the brief does that
serve?** If they can point at a line, it is pile one and you redesign. If they
cannot, it is pile two and you explain.

A fourth thing turns up in every renovation and fits none of the piles:
attachment. "Not the lemon tree." That is not a preference and not a brief
failure — it is an unwritten must-have that never made it into the brief. Add it
to the brief, dated, then either design around it or agree out loud to lose it.

## Respond in writing, then close it out

Within a couple of days, send one document that lists every piece of feedback
and what happened to it — including, especially, the ones you rejected. Name the
feedback, state the decision, give the reason, say what happens next. Where the
plan changed, show it as "this changed because you said that".

Then revise once, re-present briefly, and get an explicit sign-off: names and a
date written on the revised plan. That sign-off is what
[quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/) and your
[contract](/curriculum/costing/contracts-and-variations/) get built on, and it is
what ends the argument in Phase 8. It also protects you commercially: once you
are inside a fixed-cost home building contract in WA — the
[Home Building Contracts Act 1991](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-or-renovating-your-home)
regime covers $7,500 to $500,000, with the deposit capped at 6.5 per cent —
changes stop being conversations and become priced variations.

One warning before you accept any change. If feedback moves something with an
approval attached — a [retaining wall](/curriculum/approvals/retaining-walls-approval/),
a [patio or shed](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/), or
anything within a [pool barrier](/curriculum/approvals/pools-and-pool-fencing/) —
that is not just a redraw. Re-check the approvals path before you agree to it,
not after.

## What happens next

The signed-off concept is the input to Phase 6. The next lesson turns it into a
scaled layout plan: the same design, measured and dimensioned to the point that
somebody could set it out on the ground without you standing there.
