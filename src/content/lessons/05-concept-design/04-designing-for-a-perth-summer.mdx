Perth Metro averages **26.8 days a year at 35 °C or above and 4.2 days at 40 °C or above**, on mean February and January maximums of 31.7 °C and 31.4 °C. One February day in ten reaches 37.9 °C or hotter. The record is 44.5 °C, and the warmest night on record only fell to 29.7 °C ([Bureau of Meteorology, Perth Metro, 1994–2026](https://www.bom.gov.au/climate/averages/tables/cw_009225_All.shtml)).

Those are air temperatures measured in a shaded screen. The ground in your garden runs far hotter, and that is the number that decides whether anyone goes outside.

Renovating cuts both ways. Any established tree already shading your western side is worth more than anything you can buy this year, and should have been marked KEEP in the [garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/). But you have also inherited someone else's paving — usually the wrong colour, in the wrong place, run hard against the house.

## What a professional does at this stage

They draw the shade before they draw the shapes.

The amateur sequence is to lay out paving, lawn and beds, then look for somewhere to put a shade structure. That produces a patio nobody sits on after two o'clock. The professional sequence is the reverse: fix where the summer afternoon shade will be, put the seating and paving inside it, and let the layout fall out of that.

They design to the **3pm February condition**, not the pleasant August day the client is describing — the [sun and aspect work](/curriculum/site-analysis/sun-shade-and-aspect/) in Phase 3 gives you that on paper. And they treat shade as two purchases: a structure for this summer, a tree for ten years' time.

## Shade is the highest-value element you can buy

The Australian Government's [Your Home shading guide](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/passive-design/shading) gives the scale: direct sun delivers roughly the heat of a one-bar radiator over every square metre, and effective shading blocks up to 90% of it. Nothing else in a garden budget returns that.

Measured midday surface temperatures, from the Western Sydney University [School Microclimates](https://www.climatechange.environment.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-06/School%20Microclimate%20Report.PDF) study:

| Surface | In sun | Shaded |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Artificial grass | 61 °C | 35 °C |
| Black asphalt | 57 °C (air 34 °C) | 40 °C |
| Bare soil | 56 °C, spots to 64 °C | 39 °C |
| Dark bark mulch | 49 °C, max 56 °C | — |
| Concrete | 42–43 °C | 34–35 °C |
| Irrigated lawn | 38 °C on an extreme-heat day | 4–5 °C cooler again |
| Leaves of a well-watered plant | 29 °C | — |

Four things fall out of that. **Colour beats material** — concrete was the coolest hard surface tested, because it is pale. **Artificial turf is the worst surface you can lay**, hotter in sun than asphalt, and NSW's [cool materials](https://www.planning.nsw.gov.au/the-planning-system/resilience-and-natural-hazard-risk/urban-heat/cooler-places/cool-materials) guidance features a development that bans it outright for that reason. **Dark mulch is a hot surface**, which matters because most Perth gardens are mostly mulch. And **shading a hard surface is worth about 20 °C** — more than any material substitution.

Buy a cheap infrared thermometer and read your own garden at 3pm on a hot February afternoon. It will change your paving decision more than any brochure will.

That is a safety line, not a comfort one: a surface above 60 °C burns skin in about **five seconds**. Bare feet, small children, dogs. Around a pool or a play area it is a design fault.

## Shade that works in February and disappears in July

Mean daily solar exposure at Perth Metro is 29.9 MJ/m² in December against 9.5 MJ/m² in June. Permanent roofed shade solves January and ruins July, leaving a dark, cold patio for five months.

| Option | Summer | Winter | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deciduous vine on a pergola | Dense shade by December | Bare frame, full sun | Over seating; north and west walls |
| Deciduous tree | Improves yearly | Sun through bare branches | Where you have root room and ten years |
| Evergreen tree | Excellent year round | Shades winter sun too | West and north-west boundaries, not over seating |
| Fixed patio roof | Reliable, immediate | Permanent gloom | Against the house, where you want rain cover too |
| Shade sail | Immediate, cheap, removable | Take it down in April | Paving, play areas, the first-summer fix |
| Adjustable louvres or blinds | Full control | Full control | West-facing glass; the expensive option |

The default Perth answer for outdoor living is a **deciduous vine on a pergola**, ornamental grape being the workhorse. It is what Josh Byrne used at his own Fremantle house, where the decked living area is shaded by [grape-vine clad pergolas and shade sails](https://joshshouse.com.au/about-the-project/the-property/landscape-plans/) rather than by walls.

Be careful with the word pergola. In the Building Regulations 2012 (WA) a **pergola** is an open structure with no roof, which may carry a covering of open weave permeable material. Shade cloth keeps it a pergola; a Colorbond or polycarbonate roof does not — that is a patio, a Class 10a building, and it needs a building permit. A waterproof sail sits in between, so confirm it with your permit authority before ordering. The exempt pergola is capped at 2.4 m high and 20 m². The rest, including the **1 July 2026** change that lets you build a Class 10a structure under $50,000 without a registered building contractor, is in [structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/).

One more ceiling: under the [R-Codes](https://www.wa.gov.au/system/files/2026-04/r-codes-volume-1-10-april-2026.pdf), the deemed-to-comply outdoor living area — 30 m² at R20, 24 m² at R30, 20 m² at R40, minimum dimension 4 m — must have **at least two-thirds of it without permanent roof cover**. You cannot legally roof the whole alfresco.

## The western wall, and mass that works for you

Mark every west and north-west facing hard surface. From about 3pm in January the sun is low and side-on — the one angle no overhead structure intercepts, which is why Your Home calls for vertical shading or deep verandas on these elevations rather than eaves.

1. **Shade the wall itself**: a canopy, a vine on wires held 100–150 mm off the face, or a tree to the west. Do not plant thirsty shrubs hard against a hot rendered wall; the radiant load there is a different climate to two metres out.
2. **Screen with something air moves through.** Slats at 40–50% open, offset 600–900 mm, kill the low sun without trapping hot air behind them.
3. **Use the mass deliberately.** Perth's January diurnal range is about 13 °C, above the 10 °C threshold where [thermal mass](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/passive-design/thermal-mass) genuinely works. Shaded in summer and open to night air, a limestone wall or concrete seat is comfort. Unshaded in February, it is a radiator running until midnight.

Related trap: paving run right to the slab edge on the west. [Your Home's passive cooling guidance](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/passive-design/passive-cooling) notes that poorly shaded surrounds raise ground temperatures under an earth-coupled slab and turn it into an energy liability. A shaded, planted strip there does more for the house than its wall insulation does.

## Design for the Doctor, not against it

Perth Metro averages 19.0 km/h of wind at 3pm in January against 13.9 km/h at 9am. The afternoon is windier than the morning, and that air is the cheapest cooling in the state.

Keep the south-west quadrant permeable. The instinct that kills a Perth courtyard is answering the western sun with a solid 2.4 m wall, which also removes the breeze and leaves a still, hot box. Shade overhead; screen with slats, trellis or a lifted canopy.

Air across skin does as much for comfort as shade, so put seating in the breeze path, not behind the barbecue. And **evaporative cooling actually works here**: mean 3pm relative humidity in January is 39%, well under the 70% ceiling Your Home gives for evaporation to be effective. A water feature or misting line in the breeze path measurably cools the air in Perth in a way it would not in Brisbane.

## Seating: the two-date test

Test every seating position against two dates. It needs shade at **4pm on 5 February** and sun at **11am on 20 July**. Most positions fail one, which is why a resolved Perth garden usually has two: a summer position under deciduous or permeable cover in the breeze path, and a small north-facing winter position — a bench, a step, two chairs — with mass behind it. You would never draw the second if you only tested one season.

## Working on this in summer

[WorkSafe WA](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/working-safely-hot-conditions) lists radiant heat from surroundings as a risk factor in its own right, and heat stroke — body temperature above 40 °C, confusion, hot dry skin — is potentially fatal. Acclimatisation takes 7 to 14 days and is entirely lost after four weeks off. Start at first light, stop by late morning, do not do heavy work alone on a 40-degree day, and remember that engaging anyone for reward makes those duties legally yours.

Add UV to it. WorkSafe and Cancer Council WA state that [Western Australia gets high levels of UV radiation most of the year](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/sun-safety-workplace), and that sun protection is needed whenever the UV Index reaches 3 or above — which in summer is most of the day, hot or not, because UV is unrelated to temperature. Cutting pavers and limestone adds [silica dust](/curriculum/build/site-establishment-and-safety/) to the same afternoon.

## What this changes downstream

Shade decisions constrain the [hydrozones](/curriculum/concept-design/hydrozoning/), because shaded beds need materially less water than the western strip, and the budget, because a pergola is a build item with an approval attached.
