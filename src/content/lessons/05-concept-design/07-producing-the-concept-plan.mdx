Everything so far has been private. The zoning diagram was for you, the material
board was for you, the hydrozones were a coloured overlay nobody else has seen.
The concept plan is the first document in this course that leaves your head and
starts making other people act: a landscaper prices from it, a neighbour reacts to
it, a partner agrees or does not agree to it.

That changes what it has to be. Not prettier — clearer. A concept plan has one
job: to show what goes where, at what size, so that a competent stranger can
understand the garden without you standing beside them talking.

On a renovation you already own most of the hard part. Your
[measure-up](/curriculum/site-analysis/measuring-up/) gave you the boundaries and
the house, your [audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/)
gave you the KEEP items, and your
[constraints plan](/curriculum/site-analysis/constraints-and-opportunities-plan/)
gave you the no-go areas. This lesson is about drawing over the top of that
without wrecking it.

## What a professional does at this stage

Four habits, and none of them are about software.

**They separate the base from the design.** The base plan — boundaries, house
footprint, doors and windows, levels, KEEP trees, soakwells, easements — is drawn
once, accurately, and never redrawn. Every design idea goes on a layer or a sheet
of tracing paper over the top. Base plan sacred, design disposable. That one
discipline is why a designer can produce a fourth option in twenty minutes while
an amateur is redrawing the house for the fourth time.

**They fix the scale before the first line.** Not "roughly a metre a square" — a
stated scale, chosen for the block and the sheet, printed on the drawing.

**They match the resolution to the decision.** A concept plan does not carry
species names, paving joint patterns, dimensions or construction build-ups. Those
are Phase 6. Putting them in now is not thoroughness, it is committing to detail
you will have to unpick when the layout moves 400 mm.

**They title-block it.** Address, drawing title, scale, sheet size, north point,
date, revision letter, and the words **CONCEPT ONLY — NOT FOR CONSTRUCTION**. The
drawing will outlive the conversation that produced it, and in six months nobody
will remember which of the three versions on the kitchen bench was the current
one.

## Pick the scale, then the sheet

Metric scale is simple arithmetic. At 1:100, one metre on the ground is 10 mm on
paper. At 1:50 it is 20 mm; at 1:200 it is 5 mm.

| Site | Scale | Sheet | 1 m on the ground |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Courtyard or small rear garden, up to about 10 × 15 m | 1:50 | A3 | 20 mm |
| Typical Perth green-title block, 10–18 m frontage | **1:100** | A3 | 10 mm |
| Large block, or whole site including the verge, over about 20 × 40 m | 1:200 | A3 | 5 mm |
| A single element — steps, a seat, a planter | 1:20 | A4 | 50 mm |

Work it out for your block before you print anything. A 12.5 × 36 m lot at 1:100
is 125 × 360 mm, which sits on A3 (297 × 420 mm) with room for a title block. A
20 × 45 m lot at 1:100 is 200 × 450 mm and does not fit — so it is 1:200 on A3, or
1:100 on A2.

Two rules that save you later. Put a **graphic scale bar** on the sheet as well as
the written scale, because the written "1:100" is a lie the moment someone prints
it on A4 and the bar is not. And when you print, choose **100 per cent** or "actual
size", never "fit to page" — the default shrink is around 96 per cent, which is
enough to make every measurement you scale off the drawing wrong by 400 mm over a
10 m run.

## Choosing your route

All of these are free. The bottleneck is decisions, not software.

| Route | Draws to scale? | Best at | Worst at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tracing paper over a printed base plan** | Yes, if the base is | Speed, iteration, curves, thinking with your hand | Tidy output; you will end up photographing it |
| **Graph paper, 10 mm squares** | Yes — one square = 1 m at 1:100 | Starting in the next five minutes, no setup | Curves, revisions, anything organic |
| **[Inkscape](https://inkscape.org/release/)** | Only if you enforce it | A concept plan that looks designed — line weights, transparent planting masses | No dimensions, no scale awareness, will let you cheat |
| **[QCAD Community](https://www.qcad.org/en/qcad-downloads-trial)** | Yes, properly | Real dimensions, layers, printing at a stated scale | Learning curve; ugly by default |
| **[QGIS](https://qgis.org/download/)** | Yes, in a print layout | Sitting the plan on real cadastre and aerial imagery | Everything else; it is a GIS, not a pen |
| **[SketchUp Free](https://sketchup.trimble.com/en/plans-and-pricing/sketchup-free)** | In 3D, not on paper | Checking level changes and screens at eye level | It cannot export a scaled printed sheet |
| **Excalidraw** | No | Two people sketching options together, live | Being mistaken for a plan |

The honest recommendation: print your base plan at 1:100 on A3, tape tracing paper
over it, and draw. Do the whole design that way, then redraw the resolved version
in Inkscape or QCAD only if you need something you can email. Anyone who spends
the first evening learning CAD instead of deciding where the paving goes has
optimised the wrong thing.

## Drawing it

1. **Print three or four copies of the base plan** at your chosen scale. Never
   draw on the original.
2. **Tape tracing paper over one** and trace the fixed geometry in a fine pen:
   boundaries, house wall, every door and window (they govern sightlines and
   access), KEEP trees at canopy spread, soakwell lids, easements, meter box,
   crossover.
3. **Convert bubbles to edges.** Take each zone from your
   [zoning diagram](/curriculum/concept-design/functional-zoning/) and give it a
   real, measurable shape. This is the step people skip. A bubble labelled
   "dining" becomes a 4.2 × 3.6 m rectangle of paving, or it does not exist.
4. **Draw circulation next, with widths.** The working conventions: about
   1200 mm for a main path two people can walk side by side, 900 mm for a
   comfortable single path, 600 mm for service access only. See
   [circulation and sightlines](/curriculum/concept-design/circulation-and-sightlines/).
5. **Show every level change.** A step, a wall, a ramp, a mounded bed. Mark the top
   and bottom levels against the datum you established in
   [levels, falls and datum](/curriculum/site-analysis/levels-falls-and-datum/).
   A level change drawn as a single thin line is how a 900 mm drop with no barrier
   gets built.
6. **Tone the surfaces.** Three or four hatches — paving, deck, lawn, mulch bed,
   gravel — and a legend. Keep it to what you settled in
   [style and materials](/curriculum/concept-design/style-and-materials/).
7. **Mass the planting, do not name it.** Draw canopies at roughly 70 per cent of
   mature spread and label by role and size: "screen, 3 m", "groundcover mass",
   "feature tree". Species lists belong in Phase 6.
8. **Title block, north point, date, revision letter.** True north, not "up".

## The checks before you call it finished

- Every zone from the zoning diagram appears as a real shape with real dimensions.
- Nothing is drawn over a soakwell lid, an inspection opening, an easement, or
  inside a Water Corporation clearance zone. Easements are on your title deed, and
  building near assets can trigger clearance requirements and engineer-signed pile
  details — check
  [approvals](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/) before you
  commit.
- The outdoor living area, the tree planting area and the street setback still
  satisfy the R-Codes minimums from
  [design fundamentals](/curriculum/concept-design/design-fundamentals/). Check
  which version applies against
  [the R-Codes Volume 1 collection](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/document-collections/residential-design-codes-volume-1-including-medium-density)
  — the current one at the time of writing is April 2026.
- No boundary position has been scaled off aerial imagery. Cadastre layers and
  photography are indicative; only a surveyor registered with the Land Surveyors
  Licensing Board can fix a boundary, and anything you build hard against one is
  riding on that distinction.
- Hand it to someone who has not heard the argument. If they ask "what is this
  bit?", the drawing has failed, not them.

Then write **CONCEPT ONLY — NOT FOR CONSTRUCTION** across the title block and mean
it. This drawing is for deciding and pricing, not for setting out.
