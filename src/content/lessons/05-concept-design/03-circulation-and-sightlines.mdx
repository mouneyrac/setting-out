Circulation is the skeleton of a garden. Beds, lawn, seating and screening hang
off the routes people take, and if the routes are wrong the garden fails in a way
no amount of planting fixes.

Renovating means you are not guessing. The routes already exist — worn into the
lawn, scuffed through the mulch, marked by the bare patch where the hose is
dragged and the rut where the bin wheels turn. Read them before you draw
anything. The other half of this lesson is lines of sight rather than lines of
travel: what you see from inside the house, and what your neighbours see of you.
In Western Australia part of that is a legal question with numbers attached.

## What a professional does at this stage

They walk the block once with a camera and once with a tape, and fix three routes
before drawing a single bed.

1. **Arrival** — street to front door. The only route a stranger uses, so it must
   be obvious without a sign.
2. **Service** — bin store to verge, back door to clothesline, tap to the far
   corner. Ugly, daily, and the first thing an amateur design breaks.
3. **Build and machine** — how a mini loader, a trailer of limestone and eight
   cubic metres of soil get in, and get in again in fifteen years.

Every width is then set by what travels along it. The order matters: circulation
constrains zoning, not the other way round. If your
[zoning diagram](/curriculum/concept-design/functional-zoning/) puts the bins
somewhere the bins cannot reach, the zoning is wrong.

## Path widths, in millimetres

Clear width is the gap between finished obstructions — the face of a wall, the
edge of a raised bed, the eventual spread of the shrub, not the line on the plan.
Plants overhang. Design at clear width plus 150 to 300 mm of spill on each
planted side, or your 1,200 mm path becomes an 800 mm path in three years.

| Clear width | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| **600 mm** | Maintenance strip only. Behind a shed, down a blind side. |
| **900 mm** | One person, or a wheelbarrow with knuckles. Secondary paths, side access. |
| **1,000 mm** | Minimum step-free access path under the ABCB standard, with gates giving at least 820 mm clear opening. Your floor for anything that must still work when someone is on crutches. |
| **1,200 mm** | Two people abreast, or one person plus a barrow passing a plant. The right default for a main path. |
| **1,500 mm** | Comfortable two-abreast, wheelchair turning, furniture carried without scraping. Entry paths, and house to outdoor living area. |
| **1,800 mm+** | Reads as a terrace rather than a path. Also what you need to walk a trailer down the side of the house. |

Three geometry rules go with those, all from the
[free ABCB standard](https://www.abcb.gov.au/resource/standard/livable-housing-design-standard):
maximum gradient 1:14 along the path, crossfall no more than 1:40, and a level
landing at least 1,200 mm long at each end of a ramp. WA has not adopted those
provisions into building law, so they are voluntary here — but they are the best
free dimensions available, and a path built to them outlasts one built to taste.

Two more from the trade. Keep 2,100 mm of headroom clear above a path, and
2,400 mm where a machine travels. And never end a route in a 90-degree corner
where people move quickly; sweep it, or they will cut it.

## Desire lines

A desire line is the route people take when nobody is watching. The working rule:
a shortcut saving more than about a fifth of the distance gets taken,
permanently, whatever you plant on it.

Photograph every worn track, every gap in a hedge, every gate that only opens
400 mm because something is stored behind it, and mark them on your base plan
from [measuring up](/curriculum/site-analysis/measuring-up/). Then decide, for
each: **pave it or defeat it.** Paving is cheaper and more honest. Defeating one
needs a real barrier — a raised bed, a change of level, something dense and
thorny — not a low border and good intentions. The hose route counts too, and it
is why the same plant dies on the same corner every second summer.

## The view from inside

You will look at this garden through glass far more than you stand in it, and in
a Perth February that is literally true.

Go inside and sit down. Kitchen sink, the seat you actually use, the desk, the
bed. Photograph each view at eye height — roughly 1,500 mm standing, 1,150 mm
seated — and print them. Those photographs are your composition, and they tell
you what a plan never does: that the alfresco post lands dead centre in the
kitchen window, or that the only thing visible from the couch is colorbond.

Fix each in order: remove the ugly thing, screen it, or put something better in
front of it. Bins, meter boxes, pool pumps, aircon condensers and the clothesline
are the usual offenders. Keep the one good focal point slightly off the centre of
a window — dead centre reads as a target.

## Overlooking: the part with legal numbers

Overlooking runs both ways, and only one direction is your legal problem.

**What they see of you** is not regulated retrospectively. If a two-storey went
up behind you in 2009 and its bedroom looks into your courtyard, that is a design
problem for you, not a complaint you can make now. Respect the geometry: a 1.8 m
dividing fence does nothing against a window three metres up. Only a tall
evergreen, a roofed pergola or a built screen works, and the tree takes years.

**What you see of them** is regulated, and this is where renovations get
expensive. Under the R-Codes, a deck or patio with a floor level **more than
0.5 m above natural ground level** becomes an outdoor active habitable space. If
it overlooks a neighbouring property behind its street setback line it must be
set back within the cone of vision from the boundary, or permanently screened.

| What you are building | Setback in the cone of vision (R50 or lower) | Above R50 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Major opening to a bedroom or study | 4,500 mm | 3,000 mm |
| Major opening to another habitable room | 6,000 mm | 4,500 mm |
| Outdoor active habitable space — raised deck, terrace, patio | 7,500 mm | 6,000 mm |

The cone of vision is 45 degrees either side of the centre line, measured 1.6 m
above the floor level of the space, out to the radius in the table. The
alternative is permanent screening at least 1.6 m high, at least 75 per cent
obscure, fixed and durable.

Hence the cheapest decision in this lesson: **keep the deck at or below 500 mm
above natural ground level** and none of it applies. On sloping ground that means
cutting rather than building up — more earthworks, fewer approvals, screens and
neighbour disputes. Run that comparison before you commit, then take it to
[R-Codes and your council](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/) and
[structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/),
because your local government can modify these clauses by local planning policy.

## The front garden: sightlines, verge and bins

The front is public-facing, and half of it is not yours.

**The driveway truncation is a road safety rule, not decoration.** Nothing over
0.75 m high within 1.5 m of where a wall, fence or structure meets the driveway
where it crosses the street. That rules out the 1.5 m rendered letterbox pillar
and the hedge on the corner of the drive — a driver reversing out cannot see a
child on a scooter past either of them.

**The verge belongs to the council.** In the City of Stirling you may plant lawn,
plants or organic mulch without approval, but paving, synthetic turf and in-situ
concrete need a verge treatment application, and street trees are untouchable.
Widening or adding a crossover needs a permit first: a residential crossover is
3.0 to 6.0 m wide, sits at least 0.5 m off the adjoining boundary, and runs at
right angles to the kerb. Build one without a permit and you may be made to
remove it. Every council differs — get your own council's guideline.

**Bins are a dimensioned problem.** A standard 240 litre bin is
[585 mm wide, 730 mm deep and 1,060 mm high](https://sulo.com.au/product/240-litre-container/)
and up to 96 kg full, and Perth is moving to three of them. Presented to
Stirling's rule — 500 mm apart, 1 m clear of cars, trees, posts and overhead
powerlines — three bins need close to 2.8 m of unobstructed verge. Then check the
route: bin store to kerb must be firm, level, at least 900 mm wide and free of
steps, soft mulch and 100 mm lips, because you are pushing 96 kg on 200 mm wheels
at 6am.

## Access for machinery, trailers and mowers

Measure your narrowest access point now, in millimetres, at its worst point —
including the gate hardware, the meter box and the downpipe. That number governs
your build cost.

| Route width | What gets through |
| --- | --- |
| 750–900 mm | Wheelbarrow; narrow-access mini loader (the narrowest are around 780 mm) |
| 1,000–1,300 mm | Mini excavator, 1 to 1.7 tonne |
| 1,500–2,000 mm | A 2.5 to 5 tonne excavator, or a full-size skid steer |

Allow about 100 mm each side of the machine's transport width and confirm the
real width against the model number with the depot — see
[machinery](/curriculum/build/machinery/). Then check the three things that stop
a machine when the width is fine: **a step** (200 mm will stop a mini loader),
**overhead clearance** (eaves, pergola, branches, and the Western Power service
running to your house — never raise a boom or a tipper tray near it), and
**turning space** at each end.

Two access decisions belong in the concept, not the build. **Sequence anything
that destroys the access last** — if the machine route runs through the side gate
you are about to pave, the machine work happens first, which is a
[staging](/curriculum/costing/staging-the-project/) decision. And **design for the
second time**: a garden with no remaining machine access is one where every future
job is done by hand, at hand rates.

## Thresholds

A threshold is where one zone becomes another — a gate, a step, a change of
paving, a gap between two shrubs. They slow people down and tell them where they
may go. Use them deliberately at the front boundary, at the shift from arrival to
private garden, and at the edge of the outdoor living area; a change of material
underfoot does most of that work for none of the cost. And any level change over
about 190 mm on a route used after dark must be a proper step with a consistent
riser and visible contrast, or it becomes the thing your visitors trip on.

## What this changes downstream

Draw circulation as one overlay on the zoning diagram: three highlighted routes
each labelled with its clear width in millimetres, the cone-of-vision arcs off any
raised deck, and arrows for the views from inside. That overlay becomes
setting-out dimensions in the
[scaled layout plan](/curriculum/documentation/scaled-layout-plan/), and the path
widths become square metres of paving and tonnes of base in
[quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/).

it is usable in a Perth February.
