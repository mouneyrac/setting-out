A garden that feels resolved and a garden that feels assembled cost about the
same to build. The difference is a small number of relationships between sizes,
repeats and views — and every one of them can be checked on a drawing before you
spend a dollar.

That is the whole claim of this lesson. These principles are not taste and they
are not decoration theory. They are tests. If you cannot say which test an
element of your design passes, it is decoration, and it will look like
decoration.

Renovating makes this easier than starting from bare sand, not harder. Your
block already has proportions imposed on it: the house wall, the fence line, the
two mature trees you marked KEEP in your
[existing garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/).
You are not composing from nothing. You are resolving what is already there.

## What a professional does at this stage

They do not apply principles while drawing. They draw fast, then attack the
drawing.

A designer will knock out four or five layouts in an hour, deliberately rough,
then run each one against a fixed set of checks. Most fail on one check, and the
check tells them what to change. The value is not in knowing the word "rhythm".
It is in being able to say: *this fails on rhythm, because nothing repeats along
the western side, so the eye stops at the first shrub and the whole run reads as
short.*

Two other habits worth stealing. First, they test in plan **and** at eye level.
A plan lies about a garden — it shows you a composition you will never actually
stand in. Second, they know the statutory floor before they draw a line, because
in Western Australia the minimum size of your main outdoor space is not your
decision.

## In WA, proportion is law before it is taste

The [Residential Design Codes Volume 1](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/document-collections/residential-design-codes-volume-1-including-medium-density)
set deemed-to-comply minimums that constrain your composition. The current
version is dated April 2026 and took effect on 10 April 2026. For a single house
on land coded R40 or below, Part B applies:

| R-code | Minimum open space (% of site) | Minimum outdoor living area |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R10 | 60% | not specified |
| R12.5 | 55% | not specified |
| R15 | 50% | not specified |
| R17.5 | 50% | 36 m² |
| R20 | 50% | 30 m² |
| R25 | 50% | 30 m² |
| R30 | 45% | 24 m² |
| R35 | 45% | 24 m² |
| R40 | 45% | 20 m² |

That outdoor living area must have a **minimum length and width of 4 m**, sit
behind the street setback, be directly accessible from the primary living space,
and be at least two-thirds free of permanent roof cover. Separately, your
landscaping must include **at least one tree with a 2 m by 2 m planting area**
free of paving and roof, and **no more than 50 per cent of the street setback
area may be impervious**.

Read that last one twice. Paving the entire front yard for parking and low
maintenance is one of the most common Perth renovation moves, and as a
deemed-to-comply proposition it fails. So does putting the required tree in a
1 m wide strip. Councils can vary these in either direction through a local
planning policy or local development plan, so check before you commit —
[R-Codes and your council](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/)
covers how that assessment works.

The useful reframe: the code has already told you your main outdoor room is at
least 4 m in its smallest dimension. That is about the smallest square that
comfortably holds a six-seat table with the chairs pulled out.

## The principles, written as tests

| Principle | What it actually means | The test on your drawing | What it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Proportion** | Sizes relate sensibly to each other and to the house | Measure the smallest dimension of every space and bed. Nothing usable under 4 m; no planting bed under 1 m deep | Beds too shallow to plant, paving too narrow to furnish |
| **Scale** | Sizes relate to a human body | Draw a 1.8 m figure, a 2.4 m table with chairs out, and a 5 m mature tree canopy, at scale, on the plan | Under-sized paving, over-sized feature walls, trees that will eat the garden |
| **Unity** | It reads as one decision, not several | Count materials visible in one view. More than three paving and walling materials is the problem | The "sample yard" look |
| **Rhythm and repetition** | A repeated element carries the eye along | Trace one repeated plant or material across the plan with a finger. If you cannot, there is no rhythm | Gardens that feel short, busy and stalled |
| **Balance** | Visual weight is distributed either side of a main view | Draw the centreline of the view from your busiest window. Is there mass on both sides? | One-sided, lopsided compositions |
| **Focal points** | One thing per view earns your attention | From each key viewpoint, name the single thing you look at. Two is a stretch; three is chaos | Competing features cancelling each other |
| **Transition** | Change is graded, not abrupt | Check heights step up and textures grade from fine at the edges to coarse near the viewer | Hedges that stop dead, jarring level changes |

### Five checks to run before you go further

1. **The greyscale test.** Print your reference images and your sketch in black
   and white, or squint at them. If the composition still reads, it works on
   form and mass. If it dissolves, you were relying on flower colour, which is a
   two-week-a-year asset.
2. **The material count.** Three materials, repeated, beats seven used once.
   This is the single fastest fix available to an amateur design, and it is
   where the [style and materials](/curriculum/concept-design/style-and-materials/)
   lesson goes next.
3. **The odd-number rule.** Plants in threes, fives and sevens. One of anything
   is a specimen and must earn it by being a focal point.
4. **The kitchen window test.** Stand at whatever window you use most, and check
   the design from there before you check it from anywhere else. That is the
   view you will own, and it is the one most amateur plans never test.
5. **The 4 m rule.** Any space intended for people needs 4 m in its narrowest
   dimension. Anything less becomes a corridor, whatever you draw on it.

## What changes in Perth

The principles are universal. Their weighting is not.

- **The light is brutal.** From November to March, midday sun bleaches colour and
  kills subtle contrast. Compositions built on flower colour photograph
  beautifully in the UK and disappear here at 1pm. Form, texture, foliage colour
  and shadow do the work in Perth.
- **Shade is a design element, not a garnish.** The overhead plane — canopy,
  pergola, shade sail — is what makes an outdoor room usable for eight months of
  the year. Design it in now, not later. See
  [designing for a Perth summer](/curriculum/concept-design/designing-for-a-perth-summer/).
- **Local plants are see-through.** Much of the WA palette is open and airy
  rather than dense, so a group of three will not read as a mass. Repetition has
  to work harder here than any overseas guide will tell you.
- **Pale surfaces dominate.** Liquid limestone, white paving and limestone block
  carry enormous visual weight in bright light. A pale surface that is 30 per
  cent of the plan can be 70 per cent of the view. Weigh it accordingly when you
  check balance.
- **Lawn is the honest test of proportion.** A lawn shaped by whatever was left
  over after the paving is the clearest signal of an unresolved design. Draw it
  as a deliberate shape with a purpose, or delete it — it is the most
  water-hungry element on the page.

## Testing three gardens on your street

Walk to three gardens near you, including at least one you like, and write one
line each under three headings: how many materials are visible from the
footpath, how many focal points compete in the main view, and what repeats.

You will find the same three failures over and over in Perth renovation gardens:
too many materials; a fire pit and a water feature and a feature wall and a
sculpture in one view; and single specimens of twenty different plants bought on
twenty different weekends. All three are solvable with a pencil, and all three
are expensive to solve once built.
