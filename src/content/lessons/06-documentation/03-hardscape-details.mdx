The layout plan says where the paving goes. The section says whether it will still be flat in ten years. Almost everything that makes a Perth garden fail is buried: the base that was 40 mm of limestone instead of 100, the missing edge restraint, the retaining wall with no aggregate behind it, the deck post concreted straight into sand.

A detail is a drawing at large scale — 1:10 or 1:20 — cut vertically through one element, showing every layer from the subgrade up and every layer from the face back. You need one for each *different* build-up, not one per area. Three courtyards paved the same way share a detail. A courtyard and a driveway do not, because the base depth changes.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper who has been burnt draws details defensively. Every layer on the section exists because they have seen the version without it fail, and the numbers are there so nobody — including their own crew at 6am — has to make the call in the field.

The second reason is commercial. Once a section says "100 mm compacted crushed limestone sub-base", the sub-base is a fixed quantity: cubic metres in the [quantity take-off](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/), a line in the quote, and a documented departure rather than an argument if someone lays 50 mm. Without the section, "base preparation" is just a word, and words get thinned when a job runs tight. Draw the details before you cost, not after.

## Every detail answers the same seven questions

Any built element — paving, wall, step, deck, edge, water feature — is fully specified when the section answers all seven.

| Question | What goes on the drawing |
| --- | --- |
| **Subgrade** | Stripped to what depth, compacted to what standard, and what happens to soft spots |
| **Sub-base** | Material, compacted thickness, layer depth if built up |
| **Bedding / footing** | Material, thickness, or footing dimensions and concrete strength |
| **Wearing surface** | Product, thickness, pattern, joint width |
| **Edge / restraint** | What stops it spreading, and how big that thing is |
| **Drainage** | Where water goes — falls, agi line, aggregate, outlet |
| **Separation** | Geotextile, damp-proof course, root barrier, movement joint |

Two rules that apply to all seven. Show a dimension, not an adjective — "adequate base" specifies nothing. And show levels: the section is where finished level meets the [levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/), and the two must agree.

## Paving

Perth's standard build-up is compacted crushed limestone sub-base, sand bedding, unit paving. The City of Wanneroo's residential crossover specification is the most useful free benchmark in the state.

- **Sub-base:** minimum 100 mm crushed limestone for a brick-paved crossover, thickness depending on ground conditions. CMHA's equivalent figures are 100 mm compacted for pedestrian areas and 150 mm for residential driveways. On a renovation the existing subgrade is the risk: an old service trench, a filled soakwell or a ground-out stump will settle regardless of what you put on top. Note them on the section and specify removal and recompaction in layers.
- **Bedding:** 20–40 mm pre-compacted, well-graded sand passing a 5 mm sieve. Bricklayers sand and single-sized dune sand are explicitly not suitable, and using them is the most common paving failure in this city. Bedding sand is a bedding course, not a levelling course — if your section allows 60 mm of sand to fix a bad base, it will keep settling.
- **Units:** minimum 60 mm thick for anything a vehicle crosses, laid to 2–4 mm gaps. Compact with not less than three passes of a plate compactor over 12 mm plywood. Joint sand finer than the bedding sand, nominal maximum 2 mm.
- **Edge restraint:** draw it on every single edge. Wanneroo requires 250 mm wide by 80 mm minimum depth; CMHA gives a minimum 200 mm by 200 mm cross section for a hidden poured concrete curb in residential work. A paved area without restraint spreads from the edges inward, joints open and sand washes out — and the failure looks like a base problem when it is an edge problem.

Poured surfaces — concrete, exposed aggregate, and the liquid limestone that is nearly a Perth default — need the same subgrade and sub-base discipline plus jointing. Show control joint spacing, depth and location, because an unjointed slab cracks where it chooses.

## Retaining walls

A retaining wall is a structure, and above your council's threshold it is engineered work. In the City of Stirling a building permit is required where any retaining exceeds 0.5 m, and a wall may only sit on the boundary if it is not more than 0.5 m above natural ground. The City of Joondalup publishes a 0.5 m trigger with additional conditions. Confirm your own council's figure, and see [retaining walls approval](/curriculum/approvals/retaining-walls-approval/).

For a segmental block wall, the section must show:

1. **Base.** Not less than 150 mm of compacted crushed rock, cement-stabilised crushed rock, or lean-mix concrete of at least 15 MPa. Then 25 mm of washed sand to fine-level the first course.
2. **Embedment.** First course bedded roughly half a block below finished ground. Walls that start at ground level slide.
3. **Drainage.** 100 mm agricultural pipe with a sock, laid at 1 in 100 fall, and — the part everyone forgets — vented to daylight at a point you have actually drawn. A drain that discharges into sand behind the wall is decoration.
4. **Backfill.** 200–300 mm width of clean 10–20 mm aggregate against the back of the blocks, with block cores filled, placed one course at a time as the wall goes up.
5. **Geotextile.** Between the aggregate and the retained soil, so fines cannot migrate into the drainage layer and choke it. This is not optional on Perth sand.
6. **Surface water.** Show where water on top of the wall goes. It must not cascade over the face.

Hydrostatic pressure pushes walls over; drainage removes it. A wall that leans, bulges or cracks almost always had no functioning drainage layer.

## Decks, structures and fixings

Deck footings are designed under AS 2870 or AS 1684; anything unusual — a spa, a tiled deck, poor ground — goes to a structural engineer. The details that matter on the section:

- **Galvanised stirrups with at least 75 mm clearance** above finished ground, so posts are off the sand and termite inspection is possible. If a post is embedded directly, use no-fines concrete and slope the top of the footing away from the post.
- **25 mm separation from the building** where a termite barrier under AS 3660.1 is the compliance method, and 10 mm between decking boards and the wall for drainage.
- **Timber grades:** above-ground elements at natural durability Class 1 or 2, or softwood treated to at least H3. In-ground posts at Class 1 or H5. H2, H2F and H2S are not suitable for decks.
- **Fixings:** hot-dip galvanised or stainless steel; electroplated fasteners break down early. Within 1 km of the coast, or within 1 m of a pool edge, fixings must be stainless steel — which in Perth captures most of the coastal corridor and every backyard with a pool.
- **Board gaps:** 3–4 mm is the long-term target, adjusted at installation for seasoned versus unseasoned timber.

For roofed structures, note that from 1 July 2026 builder registration is not required in WA for Class 10a buildings under $50,000 — sheds, carports, patios and private garages. A building permit is still required and the structure still has to comply. See [structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/).

## Where the hardscape meets the house

This detail costs the most to get wrong and takes ten seconds to check. A damp-proof course must be at least 150 mm above adjacent ground level, or at least 75 mm above adjacent paving, concrete or landscaping that slopes away from the wall. Raise a bed or a paved level against the house and you bridge the DPC; rising damp appears inside months later.

On a renovation, measure the existing DPC height *before* you set your finished paving level. If the numbers do not work, the answer is a drainage channel or a step down, not a hopeful 20 mm.

## The safety notes that belong on the drawing

Details create hazards, so name them where the work is specified.

- **Silica.** Since 1 September 2024, cutting, grinding or drilling any material with at least 1 per cent crystalline silica — pavers, blocks, bricks, tiles — is a regulated process in WA. Never dry-cut. Water suppression on the tool, P2 respirator, outdoors, off the wind. A pattern that generates hundreds of cuts is a design decision with a health consequence.
- **Trenches.** Nobody works in an excavation 1.5 m or more deep without shoring, and spoil stays at least 600 mm from the edge, preferably two metres. Perth sand gives no warning before it collapses.
- **Working alone in heat.** Deep footings in February, one person, no water. This kills people. Schedule it out.
- **Old structures.** Anything pre-1990 you cut into may contain asbestos — see [auditing an existing garden](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/).
