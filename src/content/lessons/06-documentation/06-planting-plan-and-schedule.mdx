A planting plan is not a picture of a garden. It is a purchase order with a
drawing attached. Everything on it has to survive contact with a nursery that
sells by botanical name and container size, and with a delivery truck that turns
up on a date.

Two mistakes account for almost every failed planting plan in Perth. The first
is drawing plants at the size they come out of the pot, which produces a plan
that looks full, a quantity three times too high, and a garden that strangles
itself in year three. The second is specifying a species nobody in WA grows in
the size you wrote down, which you discover after the design is signed off.

Both are fixed in the same afternoon, and this lesson is that afternoon.

## What a professional does at this stage

A designer draws the circle at mature spread first, then counts, then prices,
then rings a wholesaler — in that order — before the plan leaves the office.

The reason is that the schedule is the document that gets quoted, ordered,
delivered and checked off a truck. Nobody plants from the drawing. So the
schedule has to be correct in the terms a nursery understands: botanical name,
container size, quantity, delivery month.

The availability call is what separates a plan from a wish. A professional will
substitute a species at concept stage without regret and refuse to substitute
one at delivery stage, because by then the spacing, the quantities and the
irrigation zoning were all set by the plant that is no longer coming.

## Drawing at mature spread

Each plant is a circle whose radius is half its mature spread, with a cross or a
dot at the centre marking where it goes in the ground. The centre is what gets
set out on site; the circle is only there to prove the plants fit.

The common convention, [set out clearly by Ross Uebergang](https://www.rossu.com.au/education-planting-plans),
is to draw trees at around 80% of mature spread so the plan stays readable where
canopies overlap. Use one figure for the whole drawing and put it in the legend.

Then apply the overlap rules:

| Layer | How the circles should sit at maturity |
| --- | --- |
| Trees | Canopies clear of each other and clear of the house, unless you are deliberately building a grove |
| Shrubs | Circles just touching — that is a closed mass with no bare sand and no shearing |
| Groundcovers | Circles overlapping by roughly 20%, so the bed closes in two seasons |
| Feature or specimen plants | Circles standing alone with clear space around them, or they stop being features |

You are renovating, so there is one more layer: the plants you decided to KEEP in
your [existing garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/).
Draw those at their **current** spread plus five to ten years of growth. An
established lemon-scented gum or a mature olive will take more room and cast
more shade than it does now, and everything under it inherits that shade. This
is the most common way a renovation planting plan goes wrong: new plants sized
for the sun that reaches that spot today.

## Turning spacing into quantity

Spacing is a decision you make from mature width, and quantity falls out of it
arithmetically. Do not count circles by eye.

- **Square grid:** plants per square metre = 1 ÷ (spacing in metres)²
- **Triangular (staggered) grid:** plants per square metre = 1.155 ÷ (spacing in metres)²

Triangular is what you want for mass planting — same spacing between every
neighbour, better coverage, about 15% more plants. A 24 m² bed at 600 mm
centres, triangular, is 1.155 ÷ 0.36 = 3.2 plants per square metre, so 77
plants. Add about 5% for losses in transit and planting, and order 81.

One rule that saves rework: set the outer row half a spacing in from the edge,
not on it, or the plants flop over the edging and into the paving within a year.

## The schedule columns, and what each one is really for

| Column | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Code | Two to four characters, tying the symbol on the plan to the row. A single uppercase letter for the genus plus three lowercase for the species reads well: `Afl` for *Agonis flexuosa* |
| Botanical name | The only unambiguous identifier. Cultivars in single quotes: *Agonis flexuosa* 'Burgundy'. Nurseries order by this and nothing else |
| Common name | For your client and yourself. Never for the order |
| Quantity | From the spacing arithmetic, not from counting circles |
| Pot size | Trade size, e.g. tube, 140 mm, 200 mm, 25 L, 45 L, 100 L. These are trade names, not exact volumes — for anything above 45 L, state the height and stem calliper you expect as well |
| Spacing | In millimetres, centre to centre. Blank means the planter guesses |
| Mature height × width | From the grower's label or an authoritative list, not from memory |
| Hydrozone | Carried straight from your [hydrozone plan](/curriculum/concept-design/hydrozoning/) |
| Aspect | Full sun, part shade, shade — plus which wall, in Perth |
| Rate and total | Ex GST, with a separate delivery line |

The Water Corporation's [Selecting the right tree](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/Help-and-advice/Waterwise/Garden/Selecting-the-right-tree)
list fills several of these columns for you for trees: it gives a mature size
band (small 4–8 m, medium 8–16 m, and larger), a water rating scored as no
supplementary water, once a week, or twice a week once established, and a root
invasiveness rating of low, moderate or high.

Then run two checks on the finished schedule. Sort by **hydrozone** — any plant
whose water rating disagrees with its zone is a fault, and it is cheaper to fix
here than in the [irrigation plan](/curriculum/documentation/irrigation-plan/).
Sort by **aspect** — a part-shade plant on the western wall of a Perth house is
a plant you will replace in February.

## Checking availability before you commit

Start on the Water Corporation's [waterwise plant search](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/waterwise-plants),
the only Perth database with an availability filter built in. That removes
species no WA grower produces at all, but it will not tell you whether one is
available *in the size you specified, in the month you need it*. For that:

1. Email your schedule — botanical name, container size, quantity, delivery
   month — to two or three wholesalers and ask for availability and price.
   [Greenlife Industry WA's member list](https://greenlifeindustrywa.com.au/find-a-member/)
   is how you find the right ones: local native tubestock, advanced trees and
   volume shrub production are three different businesses.
2. Expect a wall on pricing. [Benara](https://www.benaranurseries.com/), the
   state's largest trade nursery, says "sign in for pricing" and only approved
   trade accounts get in. Without one, ask a garden centre to order in, or price
   the retail equivalent and accept the margin.
3. Add a **substitute** column and nominate one yourself for every line. Leave
   it blank and the nursery substitutes for you — on availability, not on mature
   width.
4. Order advanced trees early. Anything in a 100 L bag or larger is a two-person
   or machine lift, which is a delivery and placement cost that belongs on the
   schedule as a note before it surprises you on site.

## Four legal checks before you sign it off

These are quick and they are not optional.

**Polyphagous shot-hole borer.** The entire Perth metropolitan area is inside
the Quarantine Area, and the national response has shifted from eradication to
long-term management. Do not specify the top reproductive hosts —
[maple, black locust, coral tree, plane tree, fig, poinciana, mirror bush and castor oil plant](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/department-of-primary-industries-and-regional-development/polyphagous-shot-hole-borer-which-plants-are-affected).
Box elder maple (*Acer negundo*) is the worst of them. The movement rules bite
on the demolition side too: green waste must travel in a fully sealed, covered
bin or trailer, and living plant material over 2 cm in diameter cannot leave the
Management Zone.

**Declared plants.** Check anything ornamental but vigorous against the Western
Australian Organism List. Specifying a
[declared pest](https://www.dpird.wa.gov.au/businesses/pests-weeds-and-diseases/plant/declared-plants/)
creates a legal control obligation for the owner of the land.

**Tree locations, underground.** Under the Utility Providers Code of Practice
WA, trees must be planted 2.7 m from the property boundary line and at least
0.5 m from water assets, and damage to a Water Corporation asset can be charged
back to you. Check every tree centre against the services you plotted in
[locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/).

**Tree locations, overhead.** Take the mature height column and hold it against
the overhead service line to your house.
[Western Power](https://www.westernpower.com.au/safety/safety-at-home/make-the-safe-call/managing-trees-near-powerlines/)
makes homeowners and occupiers responsible for keeping trees on their property
clear of powerlines all year round, and once vegetation is close to a line the
trimming is qualified work — not yours. Specifying a 12 m tree under a service
line is committing yourself to a lifetime of paid pruning, or a vegetation
notice.

## Totalling a real plant budget

Extend every row — quantity × rate — and total it, then add:

- **Delivery**, which is a real line, not a rounding error, especially for bags
- **5% contingency** on quantities for losses
- **GST**, because wholesale quotes come ex GST
- A note that soil amendment, mulch, stakes and ties are not in this total. They
  belong in [quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/), but
  say so here so nobody reads the plant total as the planting total.

The anchor price worth knowing is the bottom of the range: WA native tubestock
from a community grower runs a couple of dollars a plant — the
[Trillion Trees 2025 catalogue](https://trilliontrees.org.au/assets/uploads/images/2025-Nursery-catalogue-FINAL-small.pdf)
listed $2.10 to $2.60 each ex GST. An advanced tree in a bag is orders of
magnitude above that. The mix of pot sizes in your schedule, more than the
species list, is what decides the number at the bottom.

Then claim your [Treebate](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/department-of-water-and-environmental-regulation/treebate):
$150 back for every WA resident over 18, on one native tree reaching at least
three metres of canopy at maturity — a figure you now have in a column. Confirm
the current round is open first.

## What happens next

The next lesson lays the lighting plan over this drawing. It needs your tree
positions and mature spreads fixed, because uplighting is aimed at a canopy that
does not exist yet, and the cable runs have to miss root balls you have just
located. After that, the schedule goes into the
[specification](/curriculum/documentation/specification-and-schedule-of-works/)
and the totals into [Phase 7 costing](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/).
