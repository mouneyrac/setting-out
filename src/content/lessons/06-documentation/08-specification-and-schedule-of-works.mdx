Your drawings say where things go and how big they are. They do not say what
grade of limestone, how thick the compacted base is, which fixings, what mix, or
what "finished" looks like. That is the specification. And no drawing tells
anyone what order to build in, how long each step takes, or who is legally
allowed to do it. That is the schedule of works.

[NATSPEC](https://www.natspec.com.au/images/TECHnotes/NTN-GEN-005-Specifications.pdf),
the not-for-profit that owns Australia's national building specification, lists
eight jobs a specification does: it records design decisions, demonstrates
compliance with statutory requirements, and acts as an estimating document, a
tendering document, a contract document, an on-site working document, a
dispute settlement document and a project management tool. Seven of those eight
only work if it is written down.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper writing a spec is not writing it for the client. They are writing
it for the person on the tools, for the three subcontractors who will price it,
and for themselves in six months when someone asks why the paving is that
colour.

The test is blunt: **could a competent stranger build this without ringing me?**
If not, the spec is not finished.

The commercial reason is quoting. Three quotes against a vague scope are not
three quotes — they are three different jobs at three different prices, and the
cheapest is cheapest because that bloke assumed 50 mm of base and no compaction.
A precise spec is the only way to make quotes comparable, and the only defence
against the variation that arrives on day nine.

## The four ways to specify anything

NATSPEC recognises four methods. Good specs mix all four, and knowing which one
you are using stops you writing nonsense.

| Method | What it looks like | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reference** | "Mulch to AS 4454." "Tree stock to AS 2303." | Anything an Australian Standard already defines. The shortest way to import a whole quality regime in six words. |
| **Performance** | "Paving to fall away from the dwelling at not less than 1:100, with no ponding 15 minutes after rain stops." | Results you can measure but do not want to dictate the method for. |
| **Description** | "Base: crushed limestone, 100 mm compacted thickness, placed and compacted in two layers, watered during compaction." | The bulk of a landscape spec — materials, workmanship, installation procedure. |
| **Direct / proprietary** | Brand, product name, size, colour, supplier product code. | Anything where a substitute would visibly change the result: pavers, lights, controllers, plants. |

Two traps. "Or equal approved", with nobody named as the approver, is an
invitation for the cheapest substitute to arrive on a pallet — write "or
equivalent, approved in writing by the owner before ordering". And do not repeat
drawing information in the spec. Drawings govern location, dimension and
quantity; the specification governs material and workmanship. Say that in one
line at the front, because when two documents state the same fact they
eventually disagree.

## Anatomy of a worksection

NATSPEC divides every worksection into **General**, **Products** and
**Execution**, with selections either in the text or on the drawings. Copy that.
One element, written out:

> **Paved courtyard — General.** Referenced standard: AS 3727.1. Submission: a
> 1 m × 1 m sample panel laid dry, approved before ordering. Hold point: base
> compaction inspected and photographed before bedding sand is placed. Warranty:
> 12 months on workmanship.
>
> **Products.** Base: crushed limestone, 100 mm compacted. Bedding: washed sand,
> 25–30 mm screeded, not to be used to correct base level. Pavers: *brand,
> product, 400 × 400 × 40 mm, colour, supplier code*. Jointing sand: fine washed,
> swept and vibrated. Edge restraint: concrete haunch to all unrestrained edges.
>
> **Execution.** Subgrade proof-rolled. Base compacted in two layers, watered.
> Falls not less than 1:100 away from the dwelling. All cutting to be wet cut or
> with on-tool dust extraction; no dry cutting on site. Tolerance: no lipping
> greater than 3 mm between adjacent units; no ponding deeper than 5 mm 15
> minutes after wetting. Finish: joints filled, surface swept and washed down.

Set your own tolerances if you like, but they must be measurable and you must be
willing to enforce them. "Neat and tidy" is not a tolerance.

That no-dry-cutting line is not decoration. Cutting pavers, limestone block,
concrete or brick dry releases respirable crystalline silica, and
[WorkSafe WA](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/managing-crystalline-silica-dust-workplace)
requires the processing of crystalline silica substances to be controlled —
water suppression or on-tool extraction, plus respiratory protection selected in
accordance with AS/NZS 1715. Put it in the spec so whoever prices the job has to
price the control.

Write in *must* and *will*, never *may* or *should*. AS 4970 was revised in 2025
to do exactly that, and in the same revision it split its documentation into a
Tree Protection Plan (the drawing) and Tree Protection Specifications (the
words). That is this entire lesson expressed as a standard.

## Build order: the sequence that avoids undoing things

1. Approvals, service locations, permits, levies.
2. Site establishment, protection of what stays, demolition and disposal.
3. Anything needing machine access, **before** you build the thing that blocks
   access. The classic Perth mistake is paving the side access, then discovering
   the bobcat cannot reach the back.
4. Bulk earthworks and levels, then retaining walls.
5. Everything underground, in one pass: stormwater and soakwells, irrigation
   mainline and sleeves under future paving, lighting conduit, power to the shed.
6. Base preparation, then hard surfaces.
7. Structures, then soil improvement, planting, turf.
8. Anything damageable, last: light fittings, gates, screens, furniture, mulch.

Two rules cut across all of it. Sleeve under every future hard surface even
where you have no plan to use it — a 100 mm conduit costs almost nothing now and
a saw cut later costs a day. And never book a trade whose predecessor task has
no confirmed completion date; a plumber who turns up to an unexcavated trench
charges for the visit and goes to the next job.

## Durations, and being honest about them

Durations you invent are wrong. Ask each trade for their own duration when they
quote, write it in as *their* number, and add float you control. For your own
tasks, time the first square metre, multiply, then double it — you are learning.

Weather is a real line item. Perth's rain is concentrated from about May to
August, which is when earthworks and base preparation lose days; December to
February you lose the middle of the day to heat. Do not programme a concrete
pour or turf laying into a heatwave week.

## What this changes downstream

The specification is what [quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/)
measure against — you cannot count what has not been described. The schedule of
works becomes the staging plan in
[staging the project](/curriculum/costing/staging-the-project/) and the scope
attached to your contract in
[contracts and variations](/curriculum/costing/contracts-and-variations/), where
the Home Building Contracts Act rules above stop being background and become the
document you sign.

That closes the documentation phase. Phase 7 starts by measuring everything you
have just specified and turning it into quantities you can price.
