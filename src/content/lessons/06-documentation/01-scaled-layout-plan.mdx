Your concept plan is a picture. Paving roughly there, a curved bed edge roughly
like that, drawn to be understood at a glance. Nobody can build from it. Hand it
to two people and ask them to peg out "the paving" and you get two rectangles
200 mm apart, and the one that gets built is whichever got dug first.

The setting-out plan is that layout with every position pinned to a number. It
answers one question about every element on the block: **if this garden were
stripped to bare sand tomorrow, could a stranger with a 30 metre tape put it back
exactly where you intended?**

On a renovation that is not hypothetical. Most of what you would instinctively
measure from — the lawn edge, the old shed, the clump of agapanthus, the retic
valve box, the garden wall you are removing — is on the demolition list.
Dimension off it and your drawing destroys itself in week one.

## What a professional does at this stage

Four habits, and they are the whole lesson.

**They choose datums that survive demolition.** Before a single dimension is
written, two reference points are picked, labelled on the sheet and described in
words. Chosen for permanence, not convenience.

**They dimension every element twice, from two datums.** One dimension puts a
point somewhere on a line; two from separate points fix it absolutely. It is the
triangulation from [measuring up](/curriculum/site-analysis/measuring-up/) run in
reverse — you are no longer recording where things are, you are telling someone
where to put them.

**They make the drawing close before it leaves the desk.** Every chain of
dimensions is added up against its overall. A drawing that does not close still
gets found out — by a bloke with a shovel at seven in the morning, who stops work
and rings you.

**They write down what the dimension goes to.** Face of wall, centre of post,
outside face of edge restraint. An undefined dimension is a 50 to 100 mm argument
waiting to happen, and on a 3 metre path that is the gate opening or not.

## Choosing your two datums

A datum is a fixed reference you measure from. You need at least two, a decent
distance apart — the further apart, the less an angular error costs you.

| Use as a datum | Never use |
| --- | --- |
| An external house corner — hard, permanent, and the only reliably straight line on most blocks | A tree. Trunks thicken, lean, and sometimes get removed |
| A long house wall, as a baseline for perpendicular offsets | The lawn or bed edge. It is being demolished — the classic renovation mistake |
| The lot boundary, because approvals are measured from it | A fence. Perth dividing fences sit routinely 100–200 mm off the true boundary |
| The crossover or garage slab edge — concrete that is staying | A shed, pot, tap or valve box. All removable, all quietly moved by trades |
| A slab or wall your audit gave a firm KEEP | A dimension copied off an older drawing. Copy the error and you own it |

### The fence is not the boundary

If a dimension controls a legal setback, a wall near the line, or anything you
lodge with council, it must be dimensioned from the **boundary**, not the fence.
Where the boundary position is genuinely in doubt — and in 1960s to 1990s Perth
suburbs it often is — only a licensed surveyor can resolve it. Landgate is
explicit that land surveying in WA is restricted to people registered with the
Land Surveyors Licensing Board under the *Licensed Surveyors Act 1909*, and that
Landgate does not do it for you. See
[dividing fences](/curriculum/approvals/dividing-fences/) and
[the R-Codes and your council](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/).

### If you do not have a second good datum, build one

Plenty of blocks have a house at one end and nothing usable at the other. Fix it
for about fifteen dollars: set a star picket or a short concrete peg in a corner
the works will never touch, dimension it from two house corners, label it on the
plan as **DP2** with a written description, and photograph it. Every dimension at
the back of the block now hangs off it — and the same peg can carry your level
datum in the
[levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/).

## The dimensioning rules

Work at 1:100 for the whole block on A3, and pull congested areas — a paving
junction, a step, a bin store — out at 1:50 on a separate sheet. Millimetres only,
no decimals, no mixed units.

**Run chains, do not scatter.** Along a house wall, take cumulative running
dimensions past every feature and finish with the overall. Separately measured
gaps each carry their own error and they add up; a chain ending in an overall
checks itself.

**Every element gets a position and a size.** Two dimensions locate one corner
from two datums; the rest give length and width. Do not dimension a rectangle by
all four corners — that states the same thing three times, and any disagreement
between them stops the job.

### Curves and radii

A curve needs three things on the drawing: **the radius, the position of the
centre point, and the two tangent points** where it meets the straights. Locate
the centre like any other element — two dimensions from two datums.

Perth blocks being what they are, that centre will often land inside the house or
over the fence, where nobody can put a peg. That does not make the curve
unbuildable, it changes what you dimension: give a **chord with perpendicular
offsets at stated intervals** instead, so the curve is set out from a string line
between the tangent points. [Setting out obstructed
arcs](https://www.pavingexpert.com/setout05) covers all three ways of doing it.

### Free-form edges

For a meandering bed edge, forget radii. Draw a straight baseline you can
reproduce on site — the house wall, or a line between your two datums — and give
**perpendicular offsets at a fixed interval**: every 1000 mm on a tight curve,
every 2000 mm on a lazy one. State the interval and name the baseline on the
drawing. Anyone can then rebuild the shape with a tape and a bag of pegs.

### Say what the dimension goes to

Put a note on the sheet: *all dimensions in millimetres to the finished face of
masonry and the outside face of edge restraints unless noted otherwise*. Then add
**DO NOT SCALE — USE FIGURED DIMENSIONS ONLY**. PDFs get resized and printers
rescale, and someone will eventually hold a scale rule against a page that came
out at 94%.

## Making the drawing close

This is the step people skip, and the one that pays.

1. **Every chain sums to its overall.** If the running dimensions along the rear
   of the house add to 12,340 and the overall says 12,400, one figure is wrong —
   find it now.
2. **Cross the block.** House wall to paving edge, plus paving width, plus the
   gap, plus bed width, plus path — add the sequence and compare it to your
   dimensioned house-to-boundary distance. This one check catches most layout
   errors.
3. **Check the second fix.** On the points that matter, calculate the diagonal
   implied by your two dimensions. If the arcs would not cross where you drew the
   point, the point is wrong.
4. **Take it outside and try to measure three of them.** A dimension needing you
   to stand inside a wall, measure through the shed, or hold a tape over a
   1.8 metre fence is not a dimension. This separates a drawing that looks right
   from one that can be set out.
5. **Check the module.** Get the real unit size and joint width from your
   supplier and do the arithmetic on every dimensioned width — paving, decking
   boards, limestone or block courses. Rounding a courtyard to whole units costs
   nothing now and saves a day of cutting later.

That last one is a safety check as much as a cost one. Cutting concrete pavers,
limestone, fibre cement or masonry releases respirable crystalline silica. Every
cut you design out is a cut nobody has to make.

## Before any peg goes in the ground

Two things belong on this drawing because they stop work later.

**No-dig zones.** Lodge a free [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/before-you-dig/duty-of-care/)
enquiry — BYDA asks for at least one day before the job starts, and says to
contact the asset owner directly if plans have not arrived within two business
days. Hatch the service corridors that come back as no-dig zones, and move
anything you had drawn on top of them now. Those plans are indicative positions,
not a marked-up site; BYDA's own guidance is to use a skilled locator and hand-dig
to expose assets. Cross-check against
[locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/). Hitting a live
Western Power cable or a Water Corporation main is not a budget problem, it is an
ambulance problem.

**Tree protection zones.** Any tree your audit marked KEEP gets a circle on this
drawing that machinery does not enter, and the setting-out must respect it. Roots
severed in week two kill the tree in year two, and by then it is your replacement
cost.

## What goes on the sheet

Scale, north point, date, revision number and your name. Both datums labelled and
described in words — "DP1: south-east external corner of house brickwork at ground
level". The dimension convention note. DO NOT SCALE. And a demolition note or a
companion demolition drawing, because on a renovation half the setting-out only
makes sense against what is going. Print it at the stated scale on A3 and take it
outside.

## What this feeds

Every drawing after this sits on top of it. The
[levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/)
adds the third dimension to these exact shapes;
[hardscape details](/curriculum/documentation/hardscape-details/) section through
them; [quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/) read areas off
your figured dimensions instead of scaling them; and in
[earthworks and setting out](/curriculum/build/earthworks-and-setting-out/) this
drawing becomes profiles, string lines and pegs in the sand.
