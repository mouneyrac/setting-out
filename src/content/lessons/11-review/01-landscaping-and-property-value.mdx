Search "does landscaping add value" and you will find percentages: 10 per cent, 15
per cent, sometimes 20. Follow any of them back to a source and the trail ends at
another landscaping company's website. I went looking for the Australian sales
data behind those numbers and could not find it.

What does exist for Perth is two peer-reviewed studies, by the same team at UWA,
built on real sale prices from this city. They are worth more than every marketing
page combined, and what they found is not what the marketing pages say.

You have just spent a lot of money on your block. Some of it is an asset. Most of
it is something else, and it is better to know which is which now than at
settlement.

## What the Perth evidence actually says

Both use spatial hedonic modelling: take thousands of real sales, control for
everything measurable about each property and its location, and see what is left
attached to the feature you care about.

| Study | Data | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Pandit, Polyakov, Tapsuwan & Moran (2013)](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/the-effect-of-street-trees-on-property-value-in-perth-western-aus/) | 23 Perth suburbs | A **broad-leaved tree on the street verge** raised the median property price by about **AU$16,889**. Broad-leaved trees **on the property**, or on neighbours' properties, had **no significant effect**. **Palms had no significant effect anywhere.** |
| [Pandit, Polyakov & Sadler (2014)](https://research-repository.uwa.edu.au/en/publications/valuing-public-and-private-urban-tree-canopy-cover/) | 5,606 Perth house sales, 2009 | Tree canopy **increases** value when on adjacent public space, and **decreases** value when on your own property or a neighbour's within 20 metres of the boundary. |

Read that twice. In Perth sales data, the greenery that reliably moved the price
was on land you do not own, planted by a body you do not control, and it cost you
nothing.

Three caveats before you act on it. The sales are from around 2009, so $16,889 is
in 2009 dollars — against today's
[$950,000 Perth median house price](https://reiwa.com.au/the-wa-market/perth-metro/)
it is under 2 per cent. A hedonic model cannot see design quality; it sees canopy
and species type. And a negative price signal on private canopy is a statement
about what buyers paid, not a reason to cut down your trees — the shade that drops
your summer electricity bill never appears in a sale price.

The actionable part is free: if your verge has no tree, ask your council for one.
Most Perth local governments will
[plant and establish it for you](https://www.southperth.wa.gov.au/residents/services/street-trees),
in winter, with two summers of contractor watering. It is also an offence to prune
or remove a street tree yourself, so the verge is a request, not a project.

## What the evidence does not say

REIWA is the peak real estate body in this state. Its own guidance to sellers on
[renovations](https://reiwa.com.au/the-wa-market/resources/articles/renovations-should-add-value-to-your-house/)
sets one benchmark — **add two dollars in value for every one dollar spent** — names
kitchens and bathrooms as the highest-potential rooms, warns against improvements
that narrow the marketability of your home, and does not mention landscaping at
all. On pools it goes further and
[declines to give a figure](https://reiwa.com.au/the-wa-market/resources/articles/will-a-swimming-pool-increase-property-value/),
saying there are no clear answers and it comes down to buyer preference.

Hold your garden budget against that two-for-one rule. Most of it will fail. That
is not an argument against building the garden — it is an argument for knowing
which part of the spend you did for money and which part you did for yourself,
which is what the
[five-year view](/curriculum/brief/needs-wants-and-the-five-year-view/) in your
brief was for.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper who does pre-sale work for agents spends the budget in a completely
different order to one building a garden for an owner who is staying.

For a sale, the money goes where the photographs go. REIWA's own advice is that
[snap judgements are made from the street or from online images](https://reiwa.com.au/the-wa-market/resources/articles/six-ways-to-add-value-to-your-home/),
so the front setback, the entry path and the view from the back door take almost
everything. Mulch, edges, a clean lawn, one good tree, working lights. The far
corner of the block gets tidied and nothing else. The whole job is presentation of
a property, and it is often done in a week for a few thousand dollars.

For an owner staying put, the money goes into things a camera cannot see:
drainage, base depth under paving, hydrozoned irrigation, soil. Those decide
whether the garden still works in year eight. Both are landscaping, which is why
"landscaping adds X per cent" is a meaningless sentence — they return value in
different currencies.

A valuer is a third framing again. They work from comparable sales, and a garden
does not usually get its own line and its own number. It moves your property
within the range already set by your street, and it moves how fast you sell and
how many buyers compete. Neither shows up in a percentage claim; both are real.

## What a buyer pays for, and what they do not

| Buyers respond to | Buyers largely ignore |
| --- | --- |
| A verge tree and an established street | Rare or collector plants |
| A clean, resolved front setback | Anything behind the shed |
| An undercover outdoor room adjoining the house — it reads as living area | Soil amendment, however expensive |
| Level, safe, obviously sound paving and steps | Which paver you agonised over |
| Lawn a child or dog can use | Extra beds that mean extra work |
| Working retic on a controller | The hydrozoning logic behind it |
| No visible problem — no bulging wall, no ponding, no dead patches | Your plant schedule |

None of that means the ignored column was wasted. Soil and irrigation are why the
visible column still looks like that in five years. But do not expect to be paid
for them twice.

## What narrows your buyer pool

This is the column that actually costs money, because a smaller buyer pool is a
longer sale and a weaker price regardless of quality.

- **A pool.** Genuinely wanted by some Perth buyers, actively unwanted by others,
  with annual barrier inspection fees, pumping, cleaning and chlorinating on top.
- **A garden that visibly needs you.** Clipped hedging, a large kitchen garden,
  extensive beds. Buyers price maintenance hours.
- **Removing every square metre of lawn.** Correct for water use; a problem for
  families in a family suburb.
- **A single dominant aesthetic.** A Balinese courtyard, a formal parterre, a full
  desert palette. Somebody will love it. Fewer people will bid on it.
- **Losing usable hardstand.** Trading the second parking bay or the boat pad for
  garden reads as a loss on plenty of Perth blocks.

You are allowed to do every one of these. Do them knowingly.

## The bill that arrives whether you sell or not

[Landgate](https://www.landgate.wa.gov.au/valuations/valuations-for-rates-and-taxes/gross-rental-value/)
redetermines the gross rental value of every Perth metropolitan property every
three years, and below-ground pools are explicitly among the residential
attributes it takes into account. Your council rates, your Water Corporation
service charges and the emergency services levy are all struck against that
number. Add water, power and chemicals, and a pool is an annual cost with an
uncertain resale return — the part of a landscape budget where "it will add value"
is most often said and least often demonstrated.

## The paperwork is worth more than the plants

This is the part of a garden that unambiguously carries money to settlement.

An unapproved structure found during a sale is not a negotiation, it is a
condition. Retrospective approval under section 51 of the Building Act costs
[0.38% of the value of the unauthorised work](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-approvals)
for a building approval certificate (minimum $121), plus the 0.274% Building
Services Levy on work over $45,000 — and if it cannot be made compliant, it comes
down.

Note the recent change, because it is easy to misread.
[From 1 July 2026](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/media-statements/Cook%20Labor%20Government/Red-tape-cut-for-low-risk-sheds,-garages-and-carports-20260614)
a registered building contractor is only required for Class 10a work — sheds,
carports, private garages — valued over $50,000, up from $20,000. **Building permit
requirements did not change.** "No registered builder needed" is not "no permit
needed", and the permit is the piece a buyer's settlement agent asks for.

Two more WA duties that a garden project often triggers:

- **RCDs.** A home being sold, rented or hired must have
  [at least two RCDs](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/announcements/rcd-requirements-after-1-january-2019)
  covering all socket outlet and lighting sub-circuits, with no more than three
  sub-circuits per RCD. If you added outdoor power or garden lighting, it was
  licensed electrical work and it should have come with a certificate. Keep it.
- **Pool barriers.** Water more than 30 cm deep needs a compliant barrier, and
  [local governments inspect at intervals of no more than four years](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/swimming-pool-and-spa-safety-barrier-requirements)
  and can charge you for it. A raised garden bed or a new step beside a pool fence
  can quietly break compliance. See
  [pools and pool fencing](/curriculum/approvals/pools-and-pool-fencing/).

Everything else worth keeping is covered in
[warranties and records](/curriculum/handover/warranties-and-records/) and
[as-built drawings](/curriculum/handover/as-built-drawings/): permits, engineer's
certification for retaining walls, the retic plan, the plant schedule, invoices
and photographs. Filed together, that folder is the only proof a buyer has that
your base was 100 mm of compacted limestone rather than sand.

## The honest answer

Most of what you spend on a garden is consumed, not banked. You buy the years you
live in it. A well-built Perth garden should hold its cost at sale, present the
house better than a bare block would, and sell faster — that is a good outcome,
not a disappointing one.

The parts that behave like an investment are narrow: a verge tree you did not pay
for, a resolved front setback, an outdoor room attached to the house, paperwork
that survives a settlement, and the absence of anything a building inspector can
point at. Everything past that, you did for yourself.

Write down which is which. The next lesson scores the finished garden against the
brief you wrote in Phase 2, and this distinction — resale value against amenity
value — is one of the things you will be marking yourself on.
