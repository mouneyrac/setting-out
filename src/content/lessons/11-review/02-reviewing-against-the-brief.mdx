The garden is finished, the defect list is closed, and everyone who visits says
it looks great. This is the worst possible moment to form an opinion about how
the project went, and the best possible moment to collect the evidence.

There are two ways to waste this hour. The victory lap, where you look at the
result and score the brief backwards from it. And the guilt spiral, where you
fixate on the one thing that went wrong and write the whole job off. Both
produce exactly zero information you can use next time.

The way through is arithmetic. Get the [charter](/curriculum/trade-and-process/being-your-own-client/)
out, get the [brief](/curriculum/brief/writing-the-design-brief/) out, put the
real numbers beside them, and force a single dominant cause against every gap.

## Do the time-critical part first

If any part of this job was done by someone you paid, there is a clock running
and it is shorter than people expect. Under the Home Building Contracts Act
1991 — which expressly covers landscaping work, sheds, pergolas, garages and
pools — a builder is liable to make good defects **at no cost to you where they
are notified in writing within four months of practical completion**. Four
months. If your review turns up something that belongs to a contractor, the
email goes today, with photographs and dates, not when the review is tidy.

The longer windows are six years from completion to complain to the Building
Commissioner about the standard of workmanship, and three years for a
contractual dispute on a contract between $7,500 and $500,000 — and in both
cases you must give the other party a notice of proposed complaint at least
14 days before you lodge. If you are still holding an invoice for work you never
authorised, Consumer Protection WA is blunt: you do not have to pay for it.

Anything on your list that is a safety item does not wait for the review either.
A retaining wall that has moved, an odd step riser in a flight, a light circuit
terminated in a junction box in a garden bed, a raised bed that has broken the
non-climbable zone of a pool barrier — fix or isolate those now. See
[warranties and records](/curriculum/handover/warranties-and-records/) for the
paperwork you should already be holding.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaping business that does not close out its job files prices the next job
with the last job's fantasy numbers. So the file gets closed with three things:
final cost against quoted cost, actual days on site against programmed days, and
a coded reason for every gap. Not a story — a category, because categories can
be counted across ten jobs and stories cannot.

The evidence that this matters is not soft. HM Treasury's optimism bias
guidance, used to appraise public projects run by full-time professionals,
recommends that absent better data a standard building project has its capital
cost uplifted by up to 24 per cent and its duration by up to 4 per cent, and a
*non-standard* project — innovative, largely unique, or complex to construct,
which is what renovating an occupied site is — by up to 51 per cent on cost and
39 per cent on duration.

The more useful half is the breakdown of causes. For standard buildings the
single largest contributor to cost overrun is *inadequacy of the business case*
at 34 per cent, followed by disputes and claims at 29 per cent. Out of Treasury
language: the most expensive thing on a project is a badly defined brief. Which
is the document you are about to score.

## Score the brief line by line

Print it. Do not read it sympathetically. Take each requirement in the order you
wrote it and give it a score with evidence you could show someone.

| Score | Means | Evidence you must be able to point at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **2 — Met** | Delivered as written | The thing exists and does what the brief said |
| **1 — Partly** | Delivered smaller, later or cheaper | The shortfall, stated as a number |
| **0 — Not met** | Not delivered | Where the money or time went instead |
| **B — Brief was wrong** | Delivered, but the requirement was mistaken | What you know now that you did not then |

That last row is the one people leave out and the most valuable. A requirement
can be met perfectly and still have been the wrong requirement — that is a
briefing lesson, not a build lesson, and filing it as a failure teaches you
nothing.

Start with the three things you wrote in Phase 2 that would make you call the
project a failure. If any of them is true, the project failed however good the
paving looks; if none is true, say so plainly before you go picking at details.

Two categories that get scored dishonestly by default:

- **Maintenance.** The brief committed to a maintenance level. Score it against
  the hours you are actually spending, from the log you started in
  [the first twelve weeks](/curriculum/maintenance/the-first-twelve-weeks/). If
  the brief said low maintenance and you are doing four hours a week in October,
  that requirement is a 0 no matter how the garden looks.
- **The five-year view.** Screening that is 900 mm tall, a tree that will shade
  the western wall in 2033. Score these as *pending*, with the date you will
  check, not as met.

## The charter: three numbers

Phase 1 asked you to commit to a budget, a timeframe and hours per week. Put the
actuals beside them and calculate the variance as a percentage — the percentage
is what compares across projects, the dollar figure is what makes you flinch.

| Charter commitment | Actual | Variance | Dominant cause |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Budget | Total spend, everything in | % | one only |
| Duration | Elapsed weeks, first demolition day to closed defect list | % | one only |
| Hours per week | Real weekly average | % | one only |

**Count everything in the cost.** Tool purchases and hire, skip bins and tip
fees, permit and application fees, delivery charges, the plants that died and
were replaced, wetting agent, fuel, the retic fittings you bought twice, and
your own labour at a notional rate — even $40 an hour. If you cost your own time
at zero, do-it-yourself always wins the comparison and you will make the wrong
call on the next project. [Labour, hire and hidden costs](/curriculum/costing/labour-hire-and-hidden-costs/)
lists the ones people forget.

**Measure duration two ways.** Elapsed weeks and hours actually worked. Elapsed
is the honest number, because it includes the fortnight the job sat waiting for
a delivery and the month you did nothing.

## Root-cause the variance, do not excuse it

Here is the test. **An excuse is a cause you could not have known about. A reason
is one you could.** Almost everything moves to the second column once you check.

Weather is the most-used excuse in Perth landscaping and the easiest to check.
Pull the actual rainfall and temperature for the months you were on site from
the Bureau of Meteorology's records for your nearest station and compare them
with the long-term average. If July was a normal Perth July, the eight days you
lost were a programming decision, not a weather event.

Then code every variance line to exactly **one** dominant cause. Splitting a
variance across three causes is how people avoid the conclusion.

| Cause | What it looks like on a Perth block | Where the lesson belongs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope never properly defined | "A nice area out the back" became a 40 m² alfresco | [Phase 2](/curriculum/brief/writing-the-design-brief/) |
| Scope added mid-build | The fire pit that was not on any drawing | [Phase 2](/curriculum/brief/needs-wants-and-the-five-year-view/) |
| Site not investigated | Cemented limestone at 350 mm, buried builder's fill, an abandoned soakwell, services not located | [Phase 3](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) |
| Take-off or rate wrong | Sand ordered loose and paid for compacted, no wastage allowance, base depth guessed | [Phase 7](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/) |
| Approval time | The permit took eleven weeks, you allowed four | [Phase 4](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/) |
| Sequencing | Paving laid before the retic sleeves, drainage after the levels were set | [Phase 6](/curriculum/documentation/specification-and-schedule-of-works/) |
| Supply | Six-week lead time on the pavers you specified | [Phase 7](/curriculum/costing/building-the-quote/) |
| Capability | The first retaining wall took three times as long as the second | [Phase 7](/curriculum/costing/diy-vs-subcontract/) |
| Weather | Only after you have checked the record | — |

Then ask why three times. *The wall cost 60 per cent more* → *the excavation hit
cemented limestone at 400 mm* → *nobody dug a test hole deeper than a spade.* The
lesson lives in Phase 3, not Phase 8. On a Perth block most build overruns trace
back to a shortcut taken in site analysis or in the take-off.

## Score what the garden does, not how it photographs

Sit in it at four o'clock on a February afternoon, and again at 8pm. Does the
shade land where the design said it would? Is there a worn line across the lawn
where you did not put a path? Where do people actually stand at a barbecue, and
is that where you put the light?

Then the numbers that only exist after a full season:

- **Water.** Your first full summer's consumption against the same period the
  year before. The [irrigation design](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/)
  made a promise; this is the test of it.
- **Power.** Lighting and any pump, if you metered or estimated it.
- **Maintenance hours per month**, against the level the brief committed to.
- **Plant losses.** Count them by species and position, not as a total. Three
  dead plants in one bed is a hydrozoning or soil problem, not bad luck.

## And the three things you got right

This is not a consolation prize, it is the half of the review that transfers.
Name them so specifically that they are instructions. "The paving looks good" is
useless. "Ordering 8 per cent extra pavers meant the cuts around the soakwell lid
did not stop the job for a week" is something you will do again.
