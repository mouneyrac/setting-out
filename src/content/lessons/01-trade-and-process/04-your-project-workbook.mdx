This course is not a reading list. Thirty of its lessons end by asking you to
produce a document about your own block — a measured plan, a soil report, a
quantity take-off, a defect list. Those thirty documents are the point. Stacked
in order they are a landscape design and construction package for your property,
the same package a designer would charge you for, except that you understand
every page because you wrote it.

This lesson is the ten minutes you spend deciding where that package lives
before you start filling it.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper opens a job file before they open a ute door. Everything measured,
photographed, quoted, changed or paid gets written into it, on the day, with a
date. Not because they enjoy admin — because four things go wrong without it.

**Quoting.** You cannot price what you have not measured. A designer who loses
their site notes does not re-guess the numbers, they drive back out — an hour of
someone's day, or on your own project, a Saturday.

**Disputes.** In WA a home building work contract must be in writing and signed
by both parties, and the deposit before work starts is capped at 6.5 per cent —
see [Building and Energy](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-or-renovating-your-home).
A complaint about that contract has to be lodged within three years. Every one of
those protections is worth exactly as much as your ability to produce the
document. A verbal variation agreed on site is not a variation, it is two
people's memories, and they will differ by about $3,000 by the time the job ends.

**Handover between trades.** The retic contractor needs the drainage layout; the
paver needs to know where the sleeves went. On a professional job that
information is on paper. On yours it has to come from you.

**Future you.** Everything under the ground goes invisible the moment it is
backfilled, and stays invisible for the twenty years you own the house.

There is a fifth benefit specific to a homeowner. If you put the work out to
three Perth landscapers, handing all three the same plan and the same written
specification is the only way the three prices mean anything. Three quotes
against three different assumptions is not a comparison.

## Where to keep it

If you have an account on this site, each deliverable saves as you write it and
assembles at **/my/workbook/** in course order, with a counter, a print button
and a download-as-Markdown button so you can take the whole set elsewhere.
Nothing is hidden behind the account — every lesson is readable signed out.

A hardcover notebook works just as well. This is not a trick answer — the trade
ran on carbon-copy site books for fifty years. What a notebook does not do is
back itself up, so photograph its pages occasionally.

Either way you need somewhere for the things that are not text: photos,
receipts, quotes, PDFs of plans, the emailed asset-owner drawings. One folder,
one hierarchy, backed up somewhere that is not the phone in your pocket. Name
files so they sort themselves — `2026-08-02-back-corner-northwest.jpg`, not
`IMG_4471.jpg`. Keep every receipt, including the $40 ones. If you ever sell,
rent out or insure the improvement, what the work cost is a question someone
asks, and receipts are the only answer that counts; ask your accountant how long
to hold them.

## Taking "before" photos you can actually repeat

Most people take fifty before photos and none of them are comparable to
anything, because every shot is from a different spot at a different height on a
different lens. The discipline that fixes it is called photopoint monitoring,
and the [DWER guide](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/publications/photopoint-monitoring-guide-establishing-sites-and-taking-consistent-photos)
is the free WA reference. Its core warning: small changes in position, height,
angle or direction have a large effect on your ability to see change.

Set up six to ten fixed positions and shoot the same set every time.

1. **Choose the positions.** Each corner of the back garden looking in, standing
   at the back door or alfresco, the view from the window you look out of most,
   down the side access, and from the verge looking at the house. Add one close
   position per problem area.
2. **Fix the spot and the aim.** Stand on something that will not move — a
   specific paver corner, the edge of the slab, a peg. Aim the centre of the
   frame at a fixed object still there in two years: a fence post, the downpipe,
   the meter box. Note the compass bearing off your phone as backup.
3. **Fix the height.** Eye level, roughly 1.5 metres, and hold it. Crouching for
   one shot and standing for the next ruins the pair.
4. **Fix the lens.** Modern phones have three. Pick the main one, use it every
   time, and never let it slip to 0.5x ultrawide — the guide is explicit that
   wide-angle and telephoto alter perspective and make a shot impossible to
   repeat. Landscape orientation, highest resolution, two shots each.
5. **Pick your light.** Bright overcast, early morning or late afternoon. A
   Perth midday summer sky gives you blown highlights and black shadow, and
   nothing in either.
6. **Write the set down.** Position number, what it aims at, bearing, height.
   That sheet is what lets you repeat the set in eight months.

Then repeat: before anything happens, after demolition, after earthworks, before
each backfill, at completion, and each season afterwards.

### The photos that pay for themselves

- **Every open trench, with a tape measure in shot, before it is backfilled.**
  Retic mainline, laterals, sleeves under paving, conduit, drainage runs. This is
  the highest-value photo set on the whole job, and it feeds
  [as-built drawings](/curriculum/handover/as-built-drawings/).
- **The existing retic controller program**, before you change a setting. It
  tells you how the garden has been watered for years.
- **Cracks that already exist** — in the neighbour's fence, the driveway, the
  render, the paving — before machinery arrives. Dated photographs are what end
  the argument about who cracked what.
- **Deliveries**, so quantities can be checked against invoices.

Photograph trenches from the edge, standing back from the spoil. Do not climb
into an unsupported excavation to get a better angle; trench collapse is a
routine way to be killed on a small site, and no photo is worth it.

## What the finished set is worth

Comparable quotes. Variations you can price instead of argue about. A council
enquiry answered in one email. A maintenance manual that tells you what the
plant in the west bed actually is. An as-built plan that means the next trench
you dig misses your own irrigation mainline. And an honest
[review against the brief](/curriculum/review/reviewing-against-the-brief/),
which is only possible because you wrote the brief down.

That is the end of Phase 1. The next phase starts the way every professional job
starts: with the enquiry call, and the questions a good landscaper asks before
they will even quote a site visit.
