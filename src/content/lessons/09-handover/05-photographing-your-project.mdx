Your garden will never look like this again. In six months the plants will have
doubled, the mulch will have faded from chocolate to grey-brown, and the paving
joints will have picked up their first summer of dust. The handover photographs
are the only record of the day the work was finished, and they are the evidence
you will argue from later — with a defect claim, with a maintenance contractor,
with a valuer, and with yourself when you review the project in Phase 11.

An hour with a phone, done properly, beats a professional shoot done badly a
month later when the retic has already burnt a patch of lawn. The method is
short: right light, right position, right height, right list.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper photographs at handover for three reasons, in this order.

1. **Evidence.** Dated proof of the condition of the work at practical
   completion. If a wall moves in eighteen months, the argument is about what
   changed, and this set is the baseline. It belongs in the
   [warranties and records](/curriculum/handover/warranties-and-records/) file,
   not a phone gallery.
2. **Evaluation.** The camera is a brutally honest second opinion. Lines you
   swore were straight are not straight in a photograph, and professionals shoot
   the detail specifically to find their own faults before the client does.
3. **Marketing.** Last, and the only one most amateurs think about.

The other thing the trade knows: **a new garden photographs badly.** At handover
the mulch dominates, the plants are half their intended size, and the hardscape
looks stark. The good images come from the second shoot, twelve to eighteen
months on, once the planting has closed. The WA industry awards accept a project
completed within five years of the entry deadline for exactly that reason. Take
the handover set as a record, diarise a second set at twelve months, and shoot
the same positions both times.

## Light: the one variable you cannot fix afterwards

Perth's midday sun is the enemy. Directly overhead, it blows limestone and light
paving out to featureless white and drops hard black shadows under every pergola
and shrub. No editing recovers it.

Shoot in the first hour after sunrise or the last hour before sunset. Get the
times for your date from
[Geoscience Australia's sunrise and sunset calculator](https://www.ga.gov.au/scientific-topics/astronomical).
Roughly, in Perth mid-January sunrise is around 5:25am and sunset around 7:25pm;
near the winter solstice it is closer to 7:15am and 5:20pm. Winter is a far more
civilised time to do this.

Which window you use depends on aspect, and Perth is dominated by west-facing
back gardens:

| Outdoor area faces | Morning | Late afternoon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **West** (very common) | Soft, even, lit from behind you — usually the better shot | Sun in the frame and harsh, but good for backlit grasses |
| **East** | Sun in the frame, backlit | Soft and even — use this one |
| **North** | Workable | Workable; avoid the middle of the day entirely |
| **South** | Even light most of the day — the easiest garden to shoot | Same |

Two Perth reasons to favour the morning. The afternoon sea breeze arrives some
time after lunch, and moving foliage, rippling pool water and flapping umbrellas
all cost you sharpness. And a 6am summer start is comfortable where 6pm is still
34 degrees with the concrete radiating.

One trick from the DWER photopoint guide: a camera **facing south** never has the
sun in the frame, because here the sun tracks through the northern sky. Use it
when a shot has to happen at an awkward hour.

## Repeat the "before" positions exactly

This is where most people fail. A before-and-after pair is only honest if the
camera did not move. The photopoint method fixes three things:

- **Position.** Stand on something you can find again — a specific paver, the
  corner of a slab, a downpipe, a fence post. Note it. Do not pace it out.
- **Height.** Around eye level, and the *same* eye level every time. If you
  crouched for the "before" shot, crouch for the "after".
- **Direction.** Aim the centre of the frame at a fixed object roughly ten metres
  away — a gate latch, a tree trunk, a meter box. That one choice locks your angle
  better than any amount of eyeballing.

Then keep the camera the same. Highest resolution, landscape orientation, fully
zoomed out, and **one lens**. On a phone that means the standard 1x lens, not the
0.5x ultra-wide. Ultra-wide bends the perspective, exaggerates the foreground and
makes the shot impossible to match later.

You took the "before" set back in
[your project workbook](/curriculum/trade-and-process/your-project-workbook/).
Find it now, before you start, and work from that list.

## The shot list

Twenty deliberate photographs beat two hundred random ones.

| Type | How many | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Repeat positions** | Every "before" position, no exceptions | The honest comparison. Non-negotiable. |
| **Wide establishing** | 4–6 | The whole space, from the corners and from the main doorway |
| **Inside-out** | 1 per major window or door | Gardens are looked at from the kitchen sink far more than they are stood in |
| **Medium** | 6–10 | One area at a time: the fire pit, the lawn edge, the raised bed |
| **Detail** | 8–12 | Where your work shows: paving cuts at an angle, capping mitres, the join between deck and paving, edge lines, step nosings |
| **Function** | 2–4 | The space in use — furniture out, table set, lights on |
| **After dark** | 3–4 | Shot at dusk, not full black, so the sky still holds colour |

The detail shots matter more than you think. Nobody remembers a wide shot of a
courtyard, but a clean 45-degree cut around a soakwell lid is the picture that
proves the job was built properly.

## Getting it right on a phone

- **Clean the lens.** It is a landscaping site. The lens is dirty.
- **Set exposure by tapping.** Position the camera first, then tap the part of
  the frame that must be correctly exposed — usually the paving or the lawn, not
  the sky. Tapping sets focus and exposure together.
- **Turn the grid on** and keep verticals vertical. A leaning fence line is the
  most common flaw in amateur garden photos, and it comes from tilting the phone.
- **Do not zoom.** Walk.

## Prepare the site first

Half of a good photograph is tidying. Run the checklist from
[final clean and detail](/curriculum/build/final-clean-and-detail/), then coil the
hose away, move the bins and the barrow, sweep the paths, park cars off the
driveway and take the washing off the line.

Do not wet the paving to "bring out the colour". On Perth limestone and exposed
aggregate it dries unevenly and gives you a blotchy grey mess in ten minutes.
Shoot it dry.

The only real hazard here is the raised viewpoint. A two-step ladder on firm level
ground is enough. Do not climb onto a new retaining wall, a pergola beam or the
roof for a better angle.

## Permission, privacy and what you publish

Your own block is yours to photograph. The frame is the problem.

Under [section 6 of the Surveillance Devices Act 1998 (WA)](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/legislation/statutes.nsf/main_mrtitle_946_homepage.html)
it is an offence to use an optical surveillance device — a camera counts — to
record or observe a **private activity** you are not a party to, carrying a
penalty for an individual of $5,000 or twelve months. Section 9 separately
prohibits knowingly publishing such a record. The Act excludes unintentional
recording, and a "private activity" does not cover something the parties ought
reasonably to expect may be observed, so an accidental glimpse of a neighbour's
roofline is not the issue. Deliberately framing over the fence into their pool
area, or posting it, is.

The practical rule: keep neighbours' windows, yards and children out of frame, and
ask anyone who is unavoidably in a shot you intend to publish. The WA industry
works to the same standard — LIAWA requires a signed owner declaration before any
project image is published and identifies entries by suburb only. If your
landscaper wants the job for their portfolio, agree in writing what may be
published and whether the address appears.

## File them the same day

Name the folder `YYYY-MM-DD handover`, keep the repeat-position shots in their own
subfolder named per position, and back them up somewhere that is not the phone.
Strip location metadata before you post anything publicly. Then diarise twelve
months from today to shoot the same positions again.
