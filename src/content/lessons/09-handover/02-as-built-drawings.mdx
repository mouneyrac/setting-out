Every job drifts. The soakwell moved 600 mm west because the excavator found
limestone. Two stations became three when the lawn grew during construction. The
lighting cable took the long way around a tree root. None of that is failure — it
is what building is. The failure is not writing it down.

An as-built is not a new drawing. It is your Phase 6 documentation, corrected on
site in a colour that stands out, and dated. An hour of that on the day you
backfill beats a full locate three years later.

The reason this matters more in Perth than almost anywhere is the sand. Sand
leaves no scar. Six weeks after backfill there is no trench line, no settlement,
no colour change — nothing. Poly, cable, soakwell lids and drainage disappear
completely and permanently, and the free service that finds buried assets does
not help you: [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/before-you-dig/for-homeowners/)
plans "may not contain specific details within your property boundary", and
nothing you install privately is ever added to them. You are now the asset owner
of record, and there is no other record.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper does not build from a clean plan and hand back the same clean plan.
They designate one printed set as the **field record set** on day one, keep it in
the ute, and mark deviations the day they happen — not at the end, when memory has
already converted "1.2 metres off the fence" into "about a metre". That is
section 3.5 of the
[Landscape Irrigation Best Management Practices](https://www.irrigation.org/IA/FileUploads/IA/Advocacy/Landscape-Irrigation-BMP.pdf):
record deviations daily, then convert the schematic layout into the precise
physical location of all piping, sprinklers, valves and other components.

They do it because they are the ones who get called back. Every warranty callout,
every future stage, every "can you add a station for the new bed" starts with
finding the mainline. A contractor with as-builts spends ten minutes on that. One
without them spends half a day and charges you, or guesses and puts a shovel
through the retic. You are both parties in that transaction — which is the whole
argument.

## The rule that makes an as-built usable

A dimension from one point is a circle. A dimension from two points is a
position.

For every buried thing, record **two measurements to two permanent features**,
and record the **depth**. Permanent means it will survive the next renovation:
house corners, the meter box, the crossover edge, a boundary corner, a structural
post footing. Not a shrub, not a garden edge, not the temporary peg.

- Straight pipe runs: dimension both ends and every change of direction.
- Point items — solenoids, junctions, soakwell inlets, transformer, isolation
  valve — get their own two dimensions each.
- Depth to the top of the pipe or cable, measured off finished surface level, not
  off the trench floor.

If you want a formal baseline, the deposited plan for your lot costs $33.90 from
[Landgate](https://www.landgate.wa.gov.au/land-and-property/property-ownership/survey-plans-and-documents/)
and gives verified boundary dimensions — though for most gardens the house corners
are the better datum, because they are visible without a survey.

Two cheap habits pay for themselves: lay marking tape or a tracer wire over the
mainline before you backfill, and put every valve in a lidded box rather than
burying the solenoid loose in sand, as
[mainline, valves and wiring](/curriculum/build/irrigation-mainline-valves-and-wiring/)
sets out. Both make the as-built verifiable later rather than merely plausible.

## What has to be recorded, by system

| System | Record on the as-built | Common Perth mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Irrigation mainline and laterals | Route with dimensions, depth, pipe size and class, sleeve locations under paving | Marking the design route rather than the route the trench actually took around a root or rock |
| Valves and wiring | Every solenoid position, station number, wire route, and every joint | Wire joints buried loose. Record them, or better, put them in the valve box |
| Controller and schedule | Model, station-by-station schedule, operating pressure | No station map at the controller, so nobody knows what station 6 waters |
| [Drainage and soakwells](/curriculum/build/drainage-and-soakwells/) | Position, diameter, depth to lid and depth to invert, connected downpipes | Lid buried under 100 mm of mulch and turf, then lost |
| Electrical | Cable route and depth, conduit, transformer and outlet positions | Treating garden lighting as unregulated. It is not |
| Hardscape | Real finished levels, base depth, actual falls, edge restraint | Assuming the drawn fall is the built fall |
| Planting | Substitutions, actual positions, spacing as planted | Recording nothing, then not knowing what died |

The irrigation half of this is easiest to capture with a station schedule table.
Appendix 4 of the
[Water Corporation guide](https://irrigationaustralia.com.au/downloads/eKnowledge/Resources/water-efficient-landscape-and-irrigation-guide.pdf?downloadable=1)
is a ready-made sheet: controller type, water source, bore licence number, meter
number, program start times, and per station the area, sprinkler type, run time
and operating pressure. Fill it in and tape a copy inside the controller door.

## Photograph the trench before it is backfilled

This is the highest-value ten minutes in the phase, and the one people skip
because the excavator is waiting.

Shoot **before** any backfill, with a tape measure or marked rod lying in shot for
scale and a written card in frame giving the date and what you are looking at.
Take one along the trench, one across it, and one wide shot that captures a
permanent feature — house corner, meter box — in the same frame as the service. A
photograph with no fixed reference in it is a photograph of some sand.

DWER's [photopoint monitoring guide](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/publications/photopoint-monitoring-guide-establishing-sites-and-taking-consistent-photos)
is the discipline in full: fix the position, fix the direction, record height and
bearing, label everything. Even a stripped-back version — same three shots, same
order, every trench — gives you a set you can navigate in five years.

Now the safety part, and it is not optional. **Do not get into an open trench to
take a photograph.** In the Mosman Park case WorkSafe prosecuted, a worker in a
trench between 2.2 and 3 metres deep drowned in sand and water when an unisolated
main burst; the fines totalled $720,000. Sand walls give no warning before they
fail. Shoot from ground level, stand back from the edge, keep spoil heaped on the
far side, and if the trench is deep enough that you would have to climb out, treat
it as an engineered job rather than a photo opportunity. Water Corporation is
equally blunt about live services: potholing is the only way of determining
pipeline location with certainty, and alignments vary.

## Substitutions, plants and materials

Substitution is normal. The nursery had no 45 litre *Corymbia*, so you took two
25 litre. The *Westringia* came as a different cultivar. The limestone block was a
different batch and reads warmer.

Mark every one against the original schedule from
[the planting plan](/curriculum/documentation/planting-plan-and-schedule/), with
the actual botanical name, cultivar, pot size and supplier. Three reasons, in
order of what they cost you:

1. **Warranty.** A plant guarantee is worthless without proof of what was bought,
   when, and from whom — the subject of
   [warranties and record-keeping](/curriculum/handover/warranties-and-records/).
2. **Replacement matching.** When one of five dies in year two, you need the
   cultivar, not the genus.
3. **Diagnosis.** A substituted cultivar with different water needs sitting in the
   wrong hydrozone explains a lot of mysterious decline.

Do the same for materials: paver name and colour, mulch type and depth, soil
blend, wall block, exact controller model.

## The paperwork that travels with the drawings

The drawing is half of it. The other half is compliance paper, and some of it is
legally owed to you.

- **Electrical safety certificate.** Any electrical work — including the fixed
  wiring feeding a lighting transformer or a pump — must be done by a licensed
  electrician, and your contractor must give you the certificate within 28 days.
  Chase it if it does not arrive.
- **Backflow prevention.** If your retic ties into scheme water, a licensed
  plumbing contractor must install and commission the device, and boundary
  containment devices must be registered with Water Corporation. Record the type
  and location.
- **Building permits and certificates.** BA7 notice of completion and BA18
  certificate of building compliance for any permitted structure. From
  **1 July 2026** the registered building contractor threshold for Class 10a
  buildings such as sheds, carports and private garages rose to $50,000 — but
  building permit requirements did not change, so a permit may still have applied.
- **Bore licence or exemption details**, meter numbers, and the controller
  programme as actually set.

## Storing it so you find it in five years

Assume you will forget this exists, and design for that.

Keep **one PDF**, not thirty files. Scan the marked-up drawings the day you finish
them, combine them with the station schedule, the trench photos and the
certificates into a single dated document, and name it so a search finds it:
`as-built-<street>-2026.pdf`. Loose photos in a camera roll are lost the moment
you change phones.

Store it in three places that fail independently: the cloud folder you actually
use, a drive at home, and a printed set in the house file with the property
paperwork. Tape a laminated station map inside the controller door as well — that
copy gets used constantly.

Then tell someone. Councils will sell you archived *building* plans years later,
as the [City of Stirling's service](https://www.stirling.wa.gov.au/developing-property/planning-advice/request-archived-plans)
shows, but no council holds a record of your dripline. Hand the file over when you
sell, and add to it every time you change something. The as-built is a living
document, not a monument.
