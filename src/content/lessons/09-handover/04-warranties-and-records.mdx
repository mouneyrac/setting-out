The build is finished and paperwork is the last thing you want to touch. Do it
now anyway, because three of the documents described here are ones a WA
tradesperson is legally obliged to hand you, and the window for chasing them
without an awkward phone call is measured in weeks, not years.

The rest matter on a day you cannot predict. A retaining wall moves. A pump
fails in its second summer. A settlement agent asks whether the alfresco was
approved, and the buyer's finance is sitting on the answer. Records are cheap
to keep and impossible to reconstruct. Renovating adds a second job: you are
also inheriting somebody else's missing paperwork, and the best time to find
that out is while you are already filing.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscape contractor treats handover paperwork as a liability boundary, not
as customer service. The certificates prove the licensed work was done by
someone licensed. The dated photographs and the signed variation record prove
what was agreed. The maintenance manual proves the client was told how to look
after it. Together they decide who pays when something goes wrong eighteen
months later.

On your own block the counterparty is not a client — it is a future buyer, an
insurer, or a council. Build the same file. The test for every document is
simple: **if this fails in five years, or if I sell next spring, does this
piece of paper answer the question that gets asked?**

## The three certificates you are legally entitled to in WA

Electrical, gas and plumbing work in Western Australia are licensed trades.
There is no do-it-yourself exemption for 240 volt work, gasfitting, or major
plumbing. Water, sand and buried cable is a combination that kills people, and
the licensing regime exists because of it. If a garden lighting circuit, an
outdoor power point, a pool pump feed, an alfresco gas point or a new
stormwater connection formed part of your project, a licence holder did it —
and each of those trades owes you a document.

| Work | Document you must be given | Deadline | Who else is involved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electrical installation work (not maintenance, not like-for-like replacement, not repairs) | Electrical safety certificate | Within **28 days** | If it never arrives, Building and Energy says to contact your network operator — in Perth, Western Power |
| Major plumbing work | Certificate of compliance — the **blue copy** | Within **5 working days** | Contractor keeps the green copy for six years |
| Gasfitting other than servicing | Notice of Completion | Within **48 hours** | The gas supplier, who must have it before connecting |

If the certificate does not arrive, chase the contractor first; for electrical
work, Building and Energy's escalation is your network operator. Then check the
licence number on the document against the
[Building and Energy licence and registration search](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-and-energy-licence-and-registration-search).
A certificate carrying a lapsed licence number is not a certificate.

One poolside trap worth stating plainly: any metal within **1.25 metres of the
pool edge** — a steel screen, a handrail, gates, a metal-framed pergola post,
even the reinforcing in a new coping slab — must be bonded to the main earthing
system by a licensed electrical contractor. Landscapers create this problem
constantly by putting something metal where nothing metal used to be. Get the
electrician involved when you draw it, not when you have built it. See
[pools and pool fencing](/curriculum/approvals/pools-and-pool-fencing/).

## Why these matter when you sell

Two WA rules turn garden electrical work into a settlement issue.

First, **RCDs**. Residential premises being sold, rented or hired must have
RCDs protecting all circuits supplying power points and lights, installed
before the land title is transferred. Two is the minimum; more may be needed.
If your project added a circuit, that circuit is now in scope.

Second, **unapproved structures**. If a patio, pergola or shed on your block
has no building permit, REIWA's guidance is blunt: a buyer can make settlement
conditional on structures being council approved, a seller who knew and did not
disclose can be pursued for damages, and an unapproved structure found to be
unsafe can be ordered remedied or removed at the owner's cost. Retrospective
approval is possible, but it is the buyer's problem to chase and your discount
to wear.

## What is actually warranted

There are four separate layers here and they expire at different times.

| Layer | Covers | Rough life |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer guarantees (Australian Consumer Law) | Goods of acceptable quality; services with due care and skill | Automatic, cannot be excluded, can outlast any written warranty |
| Supplier or manufacturer warranty | The extra promise printed on the docket — pump, controller, decking board, paver | Whatever it says, and only if you can produce the receipt |
| Building service complaint to Building and Energy | Defective building work | Cannot be considered more than **six years** after completion |
| Home building work contract complaint | Contract disputes, $7,500 to $500,000 contracts | Generally **three years** from contract or dispute |

The point the ACCC makes and most people miss: a written warranty is an
*addition* to your rights, not a substitute. For a major failure you choose
between refund and replacement; for a minor problem the business may elect to
repair it free of charge. But you still have to prove you bought the thing, so
the receipt is doing more work than the warranty card.

**Home indemnity insurance** is required for building work requiring a permit
and valued at more than $20,000, covering deposit loss up to $40,000 and
incomplete or defective work up to $200,000. Find the certificate, file it, and
confirm the period of cover with the insurer named on it.

Also note the change that took effect this year: **from 1 July 2026,
construction of Class 10a buildings valued at less than $50,000 no longer needs
to be carried out by a registered building contractor in WA** — that is patios,
pergolas, carports and sheds. The building permit requirement has not changed.
Neither has the insurance threshold. If you used an unregistered trade under
that new rule, you have fewer avenues if the work is defective, which makes the
contract and the photographs matter more, not less. See
[structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/).

### Plant guarantees, and what voids them

Plant guarantees are real but conditional. Waldecks, for example, guarantees
plants for one year from purchase, and a claim requires proof of purchase plus
photographs of the plant and of where it is planted, assessed by a
horticulturist. That tells you the two things to keep on the day you buy: the
receipt, and a photo of the plant in the ground.

What voids a guarantee, across almost every supplier and product on a Perth
job:

- **Planting outside the recommended window.** A tree planted in January on the
  Swan Coastal Plain is not a warranty claim, it is a decision.
- **No irrigation, or irrigation that was never commissioned.** Suppliers ask
  for the schedule. Your
  [maintenance manual](/curriculum/handover/the-maintenance-manual/) is the
  answer.
- **Installation by someone other than the supplier**, where the guarantee is
  written that way — common with advanced trees.
- **Unlicensed alteration.** Rewiring a controller yourself, or adding to a
  circuit, voids the electrical work and the product warranty at once.
- **No proof of purchase.** The most common failure of all.

## Filing it so it exists in seven years

Six years is the outer building complaint limit, so build a file that survives
seven. One folder, scanned, backed up to cloud storage, and a printed copy in
the house.

1. **Compliance certificates** — electrical, plumbing, gas, with licence
   numbers legible.
2. **Permits and approvals** — building permit, development approval, pool
   barrier documentation, and the BA7 notice of completion your builder must
   lodge with the council within seven days of finishing.
3. **Contracts and variations** — every signed change, from
   [contracts and variations](/curriculum/costing/contracts-and-variations/).
4. **Receipts and invoices** — trades, materials, plants, turf. Name the files
   by date and supplier.
5. **Warranty documents** — with the purchase date written on each, because a
   warranty with no start date is unenforceable in practice.
6. **As-builts** — from [as-built drawings](/curriculum/handover/as-built-drawings/),
   including retic zones, cable runs and soakwell positions.
7. **Photographs** — trenches open, base compacted, membrane laid, before
   anything covered them.

Then close the loop on the inherited paperwork. Your local council holds the
building records for your property, and most Perth councils will release
archived plans to the owner on request — the
[City of Stirling's archived plans service](https://www.stirling.wa.gov.au/developing-property/planning-advice/request-archived-plans)
is a typical example, requiring written permission from the property owner and
a fee. Order the file now. Finding out that the previous owner's patio has no
permit is far cheaper today than during a settlement.

## What happens to this next

This file plus the as-builts and the maintenance manual is your project record,
and it is what makes the garden a documented asset rather than a set of
assumptions. The next lesson photographs the finished work properly — the
images that go in this same folder, prove the condition on handover day, and
end up with the selling agent years later.
