Two unrelated obligations share this lesson because they share a mechanism: both are about
what you bring onto the block and what you plant near the house. One is enforced by your
council with an infringement notice. The other is enforced by nothing at all — you quietly
kill a piece of bushland, sometimes your own, and it does not come back.

Most of suburban Perth is not mapped as bushfire prone. The fringe is, and so is a surprising
number of infill blocks backing onto a reserve, a rail corridor or a remnant strip of banksia
woodland. You cannot tell by looking out the window.

## What a professional does at this stage

Two lookups, both before pricing a single item.

First, the DFES map. If the lot is designated, the materials, the position of structures and
the planting near the house are all constrained, and a patio that would have been a
fortnight's work becomes a BAL-rated build. Finding that out after the concept is drawn is how
a landscaper eats a variation.

Second, where the imported material comes from. A renovation swallows sand, soil, limestone,
gravel and mulch by the tonne, and every load is a possible dieback vector.

## 1. Find out whether you are on the map

The Map of Bush Fire Prone Areas is designated by the Fire and Emergency Services
Commissioner under section 18P of the *Fire and Emergency Services Act 1998*. The current
version took effect on **13 December 2025**, and the four-month transition for newly
designated areas ran out in April 2026 — so a block caught in that round is now fully
designated. Search your address in the
[state map viewer](https://www.wa.gov.au/service/community-services/emergency-services/view-map-of-bush-fire-prone-areas):
pink means designated, blue cross-hatching means newly designated and still in transition.
The map is redrawn periodically, so being off it in 2020 tells you nothing.

## 2. What a BAL rating actually changes

A Bushfire Attack Level is the design exposure a building must resist, assessed under
**AS 3959:2018**. The number in the name is the radiant heat flux in kilowatts per square
metre. Check the current edition and amendment with your building surveyor before you rely
on a clause number.

| Rating | Means | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BAL-LOW | Insufficient risk | No bushfire construction requirements |
| BAL-12.5 | Ember attack | Gap sealing, screening and material limits start here |
| BAL-19 | Embers plus radiant heat | Tighter glazing and cladding |
| BAL-29 | Significant radiant heat | Expensive; what an asset protection zone is sized to |
| BAL-40 | Radiant heat plus likely flame contact | Very restricted palette |
| BAL-FZ | Flame zone, direct exposure | Engineered solution, or do not build there |

Building and Energy confirms the rating applies to Class 1a, 1b, 2, 3 and 9 buildings — **and
that garages, carports, decks and verandahs attached or adjacent to the house can be caught
with it.** The alfresco extension, the patio off the family room and the deck bolted to the
back wall inherit the house's BAL. A freestanding shed at the back fence usually does not, but
"adjacent" is your building surveyor's call, not yours.

On the ground that means decking species, subfloor enclosure, gap sealing and mesh, and
screening of openings — escalating sharply from BAL-29 up. Get the requirements from an
accredited Level 1 BAL Assessor, then feed them into
[structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/),
[style and materials](/curriculum/concept-design/style-and-materials/) and
[decking](/curriculum/build/decking/).

## 3. The first 20 metres: three separate rules

People conflate these constantly. They do different jobs.

| Instrument | What it is | Binds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bush Fire Risk Treatment Standards 2020** | A *permission* to clear near a building despite other clearing laws | Older buildings in bushfire prone areas |
| **Council firebreak / fire hazard notice** | An *obligation* under s.33 of the *Bush Fires Act 1954*, reissued yearly | Every landowner in the district |
| **Bushfire Management Plan** | A site-specific plan from a subdivision or development approval | Its own lot — and it overrides the notice |

The **Treatment Standards** define a risk treatment area as land within **20 metres** of a
relevant building, split into a **10-metre inner zone** and a 10-to-20-metre outer zone. Across
all 20 metres you may clear vegetation, defined to exclude trees of 5 metres or more. In the
inner 10 metres you may also remove trees and prune overhanging branches to the collar; in the
outer zone you may only prune branches sitting below 2 metres. A contiguous carport, veranda,
patio or pergola counts as part of the building when you measure.

Three catches. A **relevant building** must have been built under a development approval
obtained **before 8 December 2015** — newer houses were designed to a BAL and get nothing
here. You may not touch a tree on a significant tree register. And none of it applies within
100 metres of the coast, or on riparian, Bush Forever, covenanted, heritage, reserve or
nature-strip land, or on a lot holding threatened flora or a threatened ecological community.

The **council notice** generates the fines. Kwinana's 2025/2026 notice ran 1 December to
31 May with a penalty up to **$5,000**, and on a suburban-sized lot required dead flammable
material below 8 tonnes per hectare and grass mown to 50 mm year-round; larger lots add a
defendable space, firebreaks and an asset protection zone. Your council's numbers will differ,
so read your own notice. Two lines land straight on landscaping: 15 mm of fine fuel depth is
roughly 8 tonnes per hectare, and **mulch piles, stored firewood and burn piles count as fuel
load.** That stack of jarrah offcuts against the shed is a compliance item.

## 4. Mulch and planting near the house

Here a Perth garden and a bushfire rating pull opposite ways. Mulch is how you keep water in
sand, so do not delete it — relocate it.

CSIRO's guidance is specific: avoid mulch finer than about 5 mm, avoid shredded pine bark and
pine needles, and never run combustible mulch hard against the house or under windows, doors
and subfloor vents. Use pebble, gravel or shell in that first band, then coarse jarrah or pine
chip further out where it can do its water-holding job, and break long runs of mulched bed
with paving, path or lawn so there is no continuous fuel path to the wall. Planting follows
the same logic: no canopy over the roof, no fuel ladder — low shrub under tall shrub under
canopy is a wick. Carry it into
[designing for a Perth summer](/curriculum/concept-design/designing-for-a-perth-summer/)
and [mulch, edging and finishing](/curriculum/build/mulch-edging-and-finishing/).

**The build-day trap.** During a total fire ban you must not use angle grinders, welders, gas
cutting or soldering outdoors, and must not run internal-combustion equipment on vegetated
land — a $1,000 infringement, or up to $25,000 and 12 months on conviction. Cutting limestone
or pavers in February is exactly the job it catches, so check emergency.wa.gov.au that
morning. And when you cut, cut wet and wear a P2: respirable crystalline silica is a separate
and slower killer, covered in
[site establishment and safety](/curriculum/build/site-establishment-and-safety/).

## 5. Dieback: what it is, and why it is your problem

Phytophthora dieback is a soil-borne water mould, principally *Phytophthora cinnamomi*,
introduced to WA on infected horticultural plants in the early 1900s. It attacks roots, so an infected
plant dies whole rather than limb by limb and looks exactly like drought. DBCA rates **more
than 40 per cent of native plant species in the south-west bioregion as susceptible**, and about
56 per cent of threatened flora. Once it is in an area it cannot be eradicated.

The indicator species are the ones Perth gardeners buy: grass trees, banksias, isopogons,
leucopogons, jarrah. Wattles, WA peppermints and native peas are largely unaffected, which is
why a dieback front reads as dead banksias and grass trees with healthy acacias between them.

It travels in soil and water, so anything carrying soil is a vector — boots, tools, barrows,
dog paws, and above all machinery. A bobcat straight off a job in Roleystone can put it in your
bed on a Tuesday.

## 6. The hygiene practice

1. **Work in dry soil.** Wet soil sticks and carries. Do not move machinery or turn ground
   during and immediately after rain.
2. **Clean on entry, clean on exit.** Both directions — you do not know your own status.
3. **Dry clean first.** Stiff brush, stick or air compressor. Bag what you remove and dump it
   on already-infested ground or somewhere with no native vegetation.
4. **Wash down only on a hard, well-drained surface** away from native vegetation, and never
   let the effluent run into a bed or a reserve.
5. **Disinfect last, and only once all soil is gone.** Metho over hand tools and footwear, or
   bleach at 1 per cent sodium hypochlorite left a few minutes then rinsed. Disinfectant on a
   muddy boot does nothing.

In practice: a stiff brush and a spray bottle of 70 per cent metho to 30 per cent water at the
gate, plus a written condition in every machinery hire and contractor booking that the machine
arrives clean. Ask where it worked yesterday.

## 7. Asking suppliers the right question

There is one question, and it is not "is this dieback free".

> Where was this material won, and was that site assessed as uninfested by a registered
> Phytophthora Dieback Interpreter?

That is the DBCA standard, plus hygienic extraction and transport since. The Dieback Working
Group is blunt about the rest: **no certification system for dieback-free raw material has ever
existed, and DBCA does not accept certificates as proof.** Screened gravel still carries risk,
because screening removes size, not pathogens, and material won from cleared farmland is
*uninterpretable* rather than clean, because no native vegetation is left to read symptoms
from. There is no risk once raw material is inside brick or concrete — the risk is exactly in
the uses you are about to make of it: fill, road base, garden soil and potting mix.

| Material | Ask | A good answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sand, gravel, limestone | Which pit, and was it interpreted? | A named pit with a current dieback management plan |
| Garden soil and blends | What is the raw material, from where? | Traceable to a source, not "we blend it" |
| Mulch | Feedstock, source, does it meet AS 4454? | Composted green waste to standard, source named |
| Plants | Which nursery grew it, is it NIASA accredited? | A production nursery you can look up |
| Grass trees | Where was it dug, was that site assessed? | A straight answer, with paperwork |

For plants, filter the [Greenlife Business Directory](https://greenlifeindustry.org.au/greenlife-search)
to WA and to NIASA accreditation. If you suspect dieback is already active on your block, DBCA's
[Vegetation Health Service](https://www.dbca.wa.gov.au/management/threat-management/plant-diseases/phytophthora-dieback/vegetation-health-service)
at Kensington will test a soil and root sample taken from a recent death, about 500 mm down,
with sterilised tools.

## What happens to this next

Your **BAL status** becomes a hard constraint on materials and where structures sit. Your
**council's fuel and clearing requirements** become recurring obligations the design must
satisfy without being dismantled every November. Your **dieback position** becomes a
purchasing rule and a line in every contractor booking.

Phase 4 is done. Phase 5 takes every constraint collected since Phase 3 — soil, levels,
services, trees, R-Codes, setbacks, and now fire and hygiene — and turns them into a concept
plan that can actually be built.
