[Base preparation](/curriculum/build/base-preparation/) gave you a compacted
sub-base and a bedding layer screeded to a consistent depth. Nothing in this
lesson can fix a bad one, so if the base is not right, stop and go back.

What follows is the visible part: where the pattern starts, where the cuts land,
how the edges are held, and what stops the joint sand disappearing in the first
winter storm. It also covers the part that can shorten your life. Cutting pavers
releases respirable crystalline silica. Read that section before you hire a saw.

## The dust is the real hazard here

Concrete pavers, clay pavers and reconstituted limestone are all crystalline
silica substances — WA defines that as any material at least 1 per cent
crystalline silica by weight — and WorkSafe WA lists
[cutting and laying pavers and surfacing](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/frequently-asked-questions-silica)
by name among the activities that generate respirable crystalline silica, the
fraction fine enough to reach the deepest part of your lungs and stay there.

Since 1 September 2024, processing a crystalline silica substance with a power
tool has been regulated as high-risk work under WA's Work Health and Safety
(General) Regulations 2022. The
[workplace exposure standard is 0.05 mg/m3](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/silica-0).
A few minutes of dry cutting with an angle grinder puts you far above it.
Silicosis is irreversible and there is no treatment.

Six rules, in order of how much they protect you:

1. **Cut less.** The biggest control happens at set-out, not at the saw. Every
   cut you design out is exposure that never occurs.
2. **Wet cut, always.** A brick saw with a water feed, or a demolition saw or
   grinder with an integrated water attachment. Soak the paver first — Midland
   Brick's own advice — because a dry paver drinks the slurry and stains
   permanently.
3. **If you must dry cut**, use on-tool extraction into an industrial vacuum
   rated for particulates hazardous to health. A workshop vac blows the fines
   straight back out.
4. **Wear a respirator.** P2 minimum, selected under AS/NZS 1715 and made to
   AS/NZS 1716. Tight-fitting types must be fit tested and worn clean shaven;
   stubble breaks the seal. For a full day of cutting, a powered air-purifying
   respirator with a loose hood is easier and does not care about your beard.
5. **Never dry sweep and never blow dust around** with compressed air or a leaf
   blower. Wet sweep or vacuum. Let slurry settle in a tub, decant the water
   somewhere that is not a bed, the stormwater system or a soakwell, bin the
   solids.
6. **Cut outdoors, downwind of yourself, away from the house.** Keep children,
   pets and the neighbours' washing out of the plume, and change out of dusty
   clothes before you go inside.

If you pay someone to cut, they are a PCBU and the duties are real: risk
assessment, controls, accredited silica training, air monitoring, health
monitoring, records kept 30 years. A contractor who dry cuts on your driveway
with a 125 mm grinder, no water and no respirator is breaching the regulations
and dosing your household. That is a legitimate reason to stop the job.

Related, and recent: engineered stone has been banned nationally since 1 July
2024 — manufacture, supply, processing and installation. If your alfresco scheme
has an engineered-stone benchtop, change the material now.

## What a professional does at this stage

Before a single paver goes into sand, three things happen.

**They calculate the module and shift the whole field.** Measure the area,
divide by the paver dimension plus the joint, and look at what is left over at
each end. Then move the entire pattern so the cut at each end is at least half a
paver. A 25 mm sliver cannot be cut cleanly, cannot be restrained, and lifts out
within a year.

**They decide where the ugly cuts go.** Cuts belong against the house wall,
along the fence, under where the barbecue will sit. Full pavers land at the
entry, the threshold, the top of the steps and along the primary sight line.
Your [hardscape details](/curriculum/documentation/hardscape-details/) should
already say which edge is primary.

**They blend from at least three packs at once.** Clay and concrete pavers vary
between production batches, and laying pack by pack gives you bands no amount of
cleaning will remove.

Then they order extra — about 5 per cent for a running bond, 10 per cent for 45
degree herringbone or any diagonal, plus a small stack for future repairs — and
feed it back into the
[quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/).

## Setting out

Pick the **primary line** first: the longest, most visible edge, usually the
house wall or the main path. Everything references it. Check it is actually
straight and square, because a Perth brick wall often is not; a 3-4-5 triangle
takes two minutes and saves a fanned-out pattern. Run a string along it and a
second at right angles, and move the working string forward every three or four
courses rather than trusting one line for ten metres.

Three levels to fix before you start. **Fall:** 1:100 minimum, 1:80 preferred on
sand-jointed paving, one direction per surface — see
[levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/).
**Against the house:** the ground must slope away at least 50 mm over the first
metre, and the slab must finish at least 50 mm above paving that slopes away,
under [clause 3.3.3 of the ABCB Housing Provisions](https://ncc.abcb.gov.au/editions/ncc-2025/adopted/housing-provisions/3-site-preparation/part-33-drainage).
Paving over a weep hole or above the damp-proof course invites damp and gives
termites a bridge; if the numbers do not work you take material out, never add.
**Height:** lay 10 to 15 mm proud of final level, because the plate compactor
beds the pavers down to it.

| Pattern | Waste | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Running (stretcher) bond** | ~5% | Paths, small terraces. Fast, but unforgiving of a crooked line |
| **45 degree herringbone** | ~10% | Driveways and anything with wheels. Best interlock of any pattern |
| **90 degree herringbone** | ~8% | Same job, easier to set out, reads more formal |
| **Basketweave** | ~5% | Small square areas only. Any cut destroys the logic |
| **Stack bond** | ~5% | Large-format units, pedestrian only. No interlock at all |

## Laying

Work off the laid pavers, forward. One knee on the screeded bedding sand undoes
twenty minutes of work and the dip stays there forever.

Place each paver down against its neighbours rather than sliding it in; sliding
ploughs bedding sand into the joint and lifts the unit off its bed. Keep joints
at 2 to 3 mm — the spacer nibs on most Perth pavers give you that if you butt
them properly. Check the line every four or five courses: one millimetre of
creep per joint is invisible, and forty joints later it is 40 mm you cannot take
out. Leave 10 to 15 mm control joints at no more than 6 m spacing on large
areas.

Lay the whole field first and leave every perimeter cut until last. Then mark
them all, soak them, and cut them in one session with the saw set up properly —
that is how you get the respirator out once instead of nine times. Avoid any cut
smaller than about a third of a paver, and wash the slurry off before laying;
dried slurry on a clay paver is close to permanent.

## Edge restraint on every free edge

A free edge is any edge not already held by a building, a retaining wall, a kerb
or an existing slab. On Perth sand there is nothing for the perimeter row to
push against. Without restraint it creeps outward a few millimetres a season,
joints open, jointing sand washes out, interlock is lost, and the field starts
rocking from the edge inward. This is the commonest reason a five-year-old Perth
terrace looks twenty.

| Restraint | Best for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Concrete haunch** | Almost any edge; the Perth default | Cast behind and under the edge course, finished below paver level so mulch or turf hides it |
| **Mortared header course** | Formal edges, driveway perimeters | Doubles as a design line; needs proper base under it |
| **Plastic or aluminium paving edge** | Curves, bed edges, foot traffic | Only works spiked into real compacted base. Useless in sand |
| **Precast kerb or limestone block** | Level changes, holding a raised bed | Also retains; set on compacted base, not bedding sand |
| **Timber or garden edging in soil** | Nothing structural | It will move. Do not restrain paving with it |

Two details people miss. The compacted base must extend past the paved edge so
the restraint bears on base rather than loose sand — CMHA's edge restraint note
gives the extension distances and spike detail. And the restraint goes in
**before** final jointing and compaction, not after you have admired the
finished surface.

## Jointing and compacting, in this order

1. Field laid, all cuts in, restraint installed and set.
2. Surface bone dry and swept clean of slurry and debris.
3. Sweep jointing sand diagonally across the joints until they look full.
4. Plate compactor with a rubber or carpet pad, two or three passes in different
   directions. Bare steel on clay pavers chips the arrises.
5. Sweep in more sand, compact again, repeat until the joints stop taking sand.
6. Final sweep and clean.

Do not compact before the joints are filled — unsupported edges chip. Do not
compact within a metre of an unrestrained edge, or when the bedding sand is
saturated.

Plain washed jointing sand is cheapest and washes out first. A polymeric or
stabilised jointing sand costs more per square metre but resists storm washout,
ant excavation and weed germination, and in Perth all three happen — use it on
anything steeper than about 1:50 and anywhere a downpipe or spoon drain
discharges across the paving. Top the joints up at four weeks and again after
the first winter. Sand that keeps vanishing from the same spot is a base
problem, not a sand problem.

Compact before anyone uses the surface. The first loaded wheelbarrow across
un-bedded pavers leaves a rut you will be lifting and relaying.

## Tying into an existing garden

Assume you cannot match old pavers. Perth colours and profiles get discontinued,
and a close-but-wrong match reads worse than an honest change of material.
Usually the better move is to lift the old paving, relay it in a secondary area
such as a side access or shed apron, and lay the new material as one complete
field. Reused pavers always need new bedding sand; the old bed is full of fines,
roots and organic matter and will neither screed nor drain. What you recorded in
[auditing an existing garden](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/)
decides whether you relay onto the existing base or rebuild it.

## What happens next

You now have paved areas with real finished levels, restrained edges and a
compacted, jointed surface that will not move. The next lesson covers the
surfaces you cannot lift and relay — concrete, exposed aggregate and liquid
limestone — where formwork, falls and curing have to be right the first time,
and where every silica rule above applies again the moment a grinder touches a
control joint.
