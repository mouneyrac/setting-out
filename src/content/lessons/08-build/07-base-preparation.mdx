A paved area is a structure with four layers and you only ever see the top one.
Underneath sit a bedding layer about as thick as your finger, 100 to 150 mm of
compacted rock, and the ground itself. When paving fails in Perth — a rut where
the car parks, a birdbath still holding water twenty minutes after the rain
stops, pavers that click underfoot, joint sand that disappears every winter — the
pavers are almost never the problem.

This is the one stage with no second chance. Pavers can be lifted and relaid. If
the thing under them is wrong, you lift and relay them again in three years and
pay for the base twice.

Renovating adds a hazard a new block does not have: your sub-grade is not virgin
sand. It has been trenched for power and retic, backfilled around a soakwell,
stumped, and driven over by a bobcat. Those are exactly the places your new
paving will settle, and none of them are visible once the site is graded flat.

## What a professional does at this stage

Two things, and neither of them is laying material.

**They treat the base as the finished level.** The trimmed top of the compacted
base is a scale model of the finished paving, dropped by exactly the paver
thickness plus the compacted bedding. Every fall, every crossfall, the drop below
the door threshold, the tie-in to the existing alfresco slab — all of it is set
and signed off in the base, with a string line and a straight edge, before a grain
of sand is spread. The City of Canning's wording is the instruction: base material
is compacted to thickness and **trimmed to conform with the finished surface
levels**.

**They never use the bedding layer to fix anything.** The ACT's segmental paving
specification states it without hedging: do not use sand bedding as a levelling
material to compensate for base finishing outside tolerance; if the bedding would
exceed tolerance, rework and recompact the base. Sand is not a structural
material. A 70 mm patch of it consolidates under load, and that is a dip in your
paving by the first summer. If your
[garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/) turned up
60 to 80 mm of bedding sand under a failed area, that is what it looks like when
someone used sand instead of trimming a base.

## The four layers

| Layer | What it is | Typical depth | Its job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sub-grade** | The existing ground, cut or filled to formation level | — | Carries everything. Must be uniform, not just firm |
| **Sub-base / base** | Compacted crushed limestone or road base | 100 mm foot traffic, 150 mm driveway | Spreads load, sets the shape |
| **Bedding** | Well-graded washed sand, screeded loose | 25 mm compacted | Seats the pavers. Nothing else |
| **Surface** | Pavers, flags, concrete | 40–60 mm pedestrian, 60–80 mm vehicular | Wearing course |

## Step 1: prove the sub-grade

Dig to formation level — finished level minus surface minus bedding minus
compacted base, the arithmetic you did in
[earthworks](/curriculum/build/earthworks-and-setting-out/) — then stop and test
what you are standing on.

The WA field test is the **Perth Sand Penetrometer**: a standard rod dropped by a
standard hammer, counted in blows per 300 mm. The City of Canning requires eight
blows per 300 mm and states that this equals 95% maximum dry density. That is what
a Perth geotechnical lab measures, and the number to quote if a contractor tells
you the sand is "fine". Without one, proof-roll: run the plate compactor or a
loaded barrow over the whole formation, watch for movement, then walk it
heel-first. You are looking for **differences**, not softness — uniform
medium-dense sand carries a patio all day. What kills paving is one soft strip
beside firm ground.

The usual Perth culprits on a renovation:

- **Service trenches.** Retic, shed power, an old sewer connection. Backfilled by
  hand, never compacted, running in straight lines exactly where your ruts appear.
- **Soakwell surrounds.** The ring of sand around a soakwell is nearly always
  loose. Check against your [drainage plan](/curriculum/build/drainage-and-soakwells/).
- **Ground-out stumps and one-lift backfill** behind pools and retaining walls.
- **Organic material.** Old mulch beds, buried turf, roots — all of it comes out.
  East Fremantle's rule is the right one: excavations free of depressions, soft or
  loose material and foreign materials.

Fix a soft spot by digging it out and replacing it with compacted limestone, never
by adding base over the top. And damp the sand first: dry Perth sand vibrates past
itself under a plate and does not compact at all.

## Step 2: choose the sub-base material and depth

| Use | Compacted depth | Material |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Path, patio, courtyard, foot traffic only | 100 mm | 19 mm crushed limestone |
| Domestic driveway, single car | 100–150 mm | Crushed limestone or road base |
| Crossover, or a driveway a trailer or delivery truck uses | 150 mm+, and to council spec | Crushed limestone or road base to Main Roads WA spec |

**Crushed limestone** is the Perth default for good reason: quarried locally
around Yanchep, Neerabup and Moore River, cheap, and it self-cements when laid
damp and compacted, which crushed granite road base does not. Main Roads
Specification 501 is what the words mean here — minimum 60% calcium carbonate, a
defined grading envelope, minimum soaked CBR of 50%. Recycled crushed concrete is
a legitimate alternative where it carries a Main Roads WA certificate.

Three things not to put under paving: **cracker dust or limestone dust alone**
(a bedding and jointing product, not a structural base), **sand of any
description**, and **whatever came out of the hole**.

## Step 3: lay and compact in layers

Loose limestone loses roughly a fifth to a quarter of its depth under a plate, so
100 mm compacted needs noticeably more than 100 mm spread. No Perth council or
Main Roads specification publishes a bulking figure, so confirm yours with the
supplier and prove it on a test lift rather than ordering to a fixed number.
Spread it, water it until damp through but not running, and compact.

Layer thickness is what separates a base from a pile of rock. Main Roads works
sub-base in 100 to 250 mm compacted lifts behind a roller; behind a hand-guided
plate, cap it at 75 to 100 mm compacted per lift. A 100 mm base is one lift, a
150 mm driveway base is two. The [machinery lesson](/curriculum/build/machinery/)
covers plate selection; for limestone you want at least 80 to 100 kg, not the
small unit that suits bedding. Overlap each pass by half the plate width, then run
a final pass at 90 degrees to the first.

**Install the edge restraint before the bedding, not after.** Wanneroo specifies
250 mm wide and at least 80 mm deep; Canning specifies a 100 mm thick 20 MPa
concrete wedge extending 100 mm under the pavers. Without it the perimeter rows
creep outward, the joints open, and the pavement unlocks itself.

## Step 4: trim, then screed

Trim the compacted base to the finished profile with a straight edge and string
lines, testing against your
[levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/).
Water must run off freely with no ponding. Fill low spots with limestone and
recompact; do not fill them with sand.

Only then screed the bedding.

- **Material:** well-graded, washed, non-plastic sand passing a 5 mm sieve —
  concreting sand or a graded paving sand. Not bricklayers sand, not single-sized
  dune sand, not crusher dust.
- **Depth:** 20 to 40 mm loose, giving 25 mm compacted. Set screed rails to hold
  that depth, screed between them, then lift the rails and fill the grooves.
- **Do not walk on it, and do not compact it before laying.** Re-screed any
  depression over 5 mm.
- **Screed only what you will lay that day.** Overnight rain on a screeded bed
  means re-screeding.
- If your screed rails will not sit level, or the loose sand is running deeper
  than 40 mm anywhere, stop. That is a base problem. Go back to Step 4.

## Safety, and the one approval

Cutting pavers, limestone blocks or concrete releases respirable crystalline
silica. The WA exposure standard is 0.05 mg/m3, and since 1 September 2024 cutting
and grinding material containing 1% or more silica is regulated high risk
processing for a person conducting a business or undertaking. Cut wet or with on-tool
extraction, outdoors, wearing a properly fitted P2. Limestone is not soft harmless
rock. Add the two that actually put Perth landscapers in hospital: a plate
compactor walked off a soft edge or down a slope, and a full day of shovelling
limestone in February heat.

If any part of this crosses the verge or meets the kerb, it is a crossover and it
needs council approval **before** you start — see
[R-Codes and your council](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/).

## What happens next

You now have a compacted, trimmed base sitting exactly one paver plus 25 mm below
finished level, with edge restraints in and falls proven. The next lesson lays the
surface on it: pattern, cutting, laying sequence, compaction over the pavers and
jointing. Every level decision has already been made — [paving](/curriculum/build/paving/)
is the easy part, and only because of what you did here.
