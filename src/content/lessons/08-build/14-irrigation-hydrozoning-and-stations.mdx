A **hydrozone** is an area of planting that wants the same amount of water, at
the same frequency, under the same conditions. A **station** is the group of
emitters one solenoid valve switches on together. This lesson makes those two
line up, then proves the result survives two limits you have already measured:
how much water your block delivers at once, and how long the law lets you run.

Josh Byrne's
[landscaping FAQ](https://joshshouse.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Landscaping-FAQs-for-Joshs-House.pdf)
for his own house in Hilton defines hydrozoning as "grouping plants based on
their common water and management requirements" so that "various hydrozones can
be serviced by different irrigation stations". On one suburban block he runs
seven, each with its own emitter type and its own station.

You are renovating, so you have inherited a station layout someone else drew,
usually to suit the pipe on the truck. Assume it is wrong until your plan says
otherwise.

## What a professional does at this stage

Two rules and two tests, in that order. Everything else is detail.

**Rule 1: one station contains exactly one hydrozone and exactly one emitter
type.** No exceptions, no "it's only a small bed".

**Rule 2: one hydrozone may need several stations.** Flow can split a hydrozone
across two valves. Flow is never a reason to merge two hydrozones onto one.

Get those the wrong way round and you produce the commonest fault on Perth
blocks: a front station with pop-up sprays doing the lawn and the shrub bed
behind it. The lawn wants about 10 mm twice a week into the top 150 mm; the
shrubs want a deeper soak far less often. One of them is always being punished —
usually the shrubs, watered onto their foliage at lawn frequency while the lawn
looks fine.

Hydrozones get drawn before any pipe, because station boundaries set valve
positions, valve positions set the mainline route, and the mainline route sets
the trenching. Redrawing them on paper costs ten minutes; redrawing them with
the trench open costs a day.

## What makes two areas different enough to need separate stations

Any one of these is sufficient on its own.

| Difference | Why it forces a split |
| --- | --- |
| **Lawn versus bed** | Root depth 100–150 mm versus 300–600 mm, different emitter, different frequency. Never combine. |
| **Sun versus shade** | A west-facing bed against brick or limestone can want 40–50% more water in February than the same plants on the shaded south side. |
| **Emitter type** | Spray applies 35–45 mm/hour, rotary 10–15. On one valve, one is always wrong. |
| **Water need rating** | Established natives, thirsty exotics and a vegetable bed are three hydrozones even where they touch. |
| **Slope and soil** | A verge falling to the road, or a bed on shallow sand over limestone, drains faster than flat improved soil. |
| **Containers** | Dry out several times faster than ground beds. Separate station, or hand water. |
| **New planting** | Needs its own frequency for its first summer. Give it a station or a separate program. |

Aspect is the one people skip, and in Perth it is the expensive one. Afternoon
sun from November to February plus radiant heat off a limestone wall or a
colorbond fence is a different climate from the south side of the same house.
If one bed has always struggled while the identical bed opposite thrives, check
the station numbers before you blame the plants.

## Test 1: does each station fit inside your available flow?

From [Irrigation 2](/curriculum/build/irrigation-water-source/) you have a
measured flow. Set a **design flow** at about 80% of it and never exceed it on
any single station.

The arithmetic, using published spray flows of roughly 2.3 L/min for a 90 degree
arc, 4.5 for 180 and 9.1 for 360:

- Design flow 24 L/min
- Proposed lawn station: 4 × 90° (9.2) + 2 × 180° (9.0) + 2 × 360° (18.2) = **36.4 L/min**
- Over by 12.4 L/min, so this hydrozone becomes two stations of 18.2 L/min each

Note what did not happen: nothing got added to the second station to "use up"
the spare capacity. Spare capacity is free; a mixed station is not.

Overloading a station does not announce itself by failing. It shows up as heads
misting instead of throwing and a dry arc on one side of the lawn, which people
then fix with more run time, which drowns everything else.
[Irrigation 1](/curriculum/build/irrigation-hydraulics/) covers why.

## Test 2: does the whole thing fit inside the watering window?

This is the constraint that catches people, and it is a legal one.

Perth and Mandurah are on a **two-day-per-week roster**, and you may water
**once on a rostered day, either before 9am or after 6pm**
([DWER](https://begroundwaterwise.wa.gov.au/garden-bore-regulation/)). One
window, not two, and bore users are on the same roster. Breaches attract a
**$100 on-the-spot fine**, and a controller malfunction is not a defence.

So: **every station scheduled on a given day must finish inside one window.**
Legally the morning window opens at midnight, but design to roughly 4:30am to
9:00am — about 270 minutes — so watering ends near dawn and foliage is not wet
all night.

An example on a modest block, using the
[Water Corporation application rates](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/how-long-should-i-run-my-sprinklers-for):

| Station | Hydrozone | Emitter | Run time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Rear lawn (west half) | Rotary | 50 min |
| 2 | Rear lawn (east half) | Rotary | 50 min |
| 3 | North beds, full sun | Dripline | 35 min |
| 4 | South beds, shaded | Dripline | 30 min |
| 5 | Verge, full sun | Rotary | 45 min |
| 6 | Pots and planters | Drippers | 10 min |
| | | **Total** | **220 min** |

Start at 5:20am and that finishes at 9:00am exactly — too tight, for the reason
everyone forgets. **Seasonal adjustment multiplies every run time.** Set the
controller to 120% in February and 220 minutes becomes 264. Design the window
against your peak-summer setting, not your mild-month one.

Three ways to buy back time, in order of preference:

1. **Do not run every station on every rostered day.** Established natives may
   need one watering a fortnight in summer, not two a week. Put them on a second
   program on the alternate day. Your peak day is the day two programs coincide,
   so make sure they never do.
2. **Change the emitter, not the schedule.** A rotary station at 12 mm/hour
   takes 50 minutes for 10 mm; spray at 40 mm/hour takes 15 — but sprays lose
   far more to wind and evaporation here. See
   [Irrigation 4](/curriculum/build/irrigation-emitters/).
3. **Reduce irrigated area.** Mulch, paving and dry gravel need no station.

One nuance in your favour: **cycle-and-soak costs little extra elapsed time once
you have six or more stations**, because station 1 soaks while stations 2 to 6
run. On a two-station system it adds dead time you cannot afford —
[Irrigation 5](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/).

## Renovating: when to re-valve rather than reuse

Overlay your new hydrozone plan on the station map from your
[existing garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/),
then apply one test: **does any existing station boundary cross a hydrozone
boundary?** If it does, that station gets re-valved. Do not try to save it with
clever nozzle selection or a shorter run time — you cannot schedule your way out
of a mixed station.

You may legally run each station for **two minutes** to test it, on rostered
days, before 9am or after 6pm. To map a large system in winter during the
switch-off, a
[Waterwise Garden Irrigator](https://www.waterwiseprograms.com.au/find-a-water-specialist)
may test outside those windows.

Three points that decide the budget:

- A solenoid, a valve box and a length of poly are among the cheapest items in
  the whole build. Replacing plants that were on the wrong station is not.
- Existing mainline and trench routes are often reusable when the station layout
  is not. Reuse the corridor, re-cut the boundaries.
- **Count your controller stations before you finish the plan.** Domestic
  controllers come in 4, 6, 8 and 12 station sizes; if you need nine, buy the
  twelve. Name each one now — "S3 north beds, sun, dripline" — because that list
  becomes the controller labels and the as-built in
  [Irrigation 8](/curriculum/build/irrigation-install-and-audit/).

## New planting, the exemption and the winter ban

New plants cannot be established on a two-day roster. Apply for a
[watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
**before** you plant. It covers new gardens and new lawn including roll-on,
runners, shredded lawn and seed, and runs up to **42 days from planting between
1 October and 31 March**, or **35 days between 1 April and 30 September**. The
published schedule starts at 3.5 mm three times a day and tapers — three
separate start times, so check the establishment program still fits the window.
Watering outside the conditions of an exemption risks a **$500 fine**.

The other date to design around: the **Sprinkler Switch-off, 1 June to
31 August** every year, bore water included. If the build finishes in May, new
turf is hand-watered through winter or you apply for an exemption — see
[turf for Perth](/curriculum/build/turf-for-perth/).

## Safety

Nothing here is physically dangerous, but two things downstream are. The mains
supply to a controller and any backflow device on the scheme connection are
trade work, not weekend work — see
[Irrigation 6](/curriculum/build/irrigation-mainline-valves-and-wiring/) and
[Irrigation 7](/curriculum/build/irrigation-controllers-and-compliance/). Before
any trench is cut for a new valve,

You should now have a numbered station list: for each one, the hydrozone it
serves, its aspect, area, emitter type, total flow and provisional run time.
