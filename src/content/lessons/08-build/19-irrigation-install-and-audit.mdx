Seven lessons of planning end here. This is the week the pipe goes in the ground,
and it is the one part of the mini-course where every mistake becomes invisible
the moment you make it. By knock-off, whatever you got wrong is under 200 mm of
sand and looks exactly like whatever you got right.

Two rules carry the whole lesson. **Nothing gets a head fitted until the pipe has
been flushed clean.** **Nothing gets backfilled until it has run under pressure
and been photographed.** Everything else here is detail hanging off those two.

There is also a legal trap on day one, and people fall into it before they have
laid a metre of pipe: running water through a new system is watering. Water
Corporation limits testing to
[two minutes per station, on your rostered days, before 9am or after 6pm](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/testing-your-irrigation).
That is enough to flush and fault-find. It is not enough for a catch-cup run,
which is a watering event and has to be your one watering for the day. Between
1 June and 31 August you cannot run it at all — the Switch-off covers bore water
too — and in that window only a Waterwise Garden Irrigator can legally test for you.

## Before you open a trench

Lodge a free [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/) enquiry, then
hand-dig to expose anything you cross. Plans show approximate positions and they
never show what a previous owner buried: the shed feed, the old pool light cable,
the abandoned poly. Hitting a live consumer mains with a shovel is the way this
work kills people.

The cut-in into your water service is not yours. In WA the homeowner's own-work
list is
[five items long](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/plumbing-work-your-home)
and retic is not on it — see
[Irrigation 6](/curriculum/build/irrigation-mainline-valves-and-wiring/) for where
the licensed plumber's job ends and yours begins.

Two more physical hazards, both specific to this job:

- **Heat.** Your legal watering windows are before 9am and after 6pm, so you will
  be tempted to trench through the middle of a February day. WorkSafe WA's own
  figure is
  [250 ml of water every 15 to 20 minutes](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/working-safely-hot-conditions)
  in hot conditions, plus shade and extra breaks. Working alone in sand in
  40 degrees with nobody expecting you back is the real risk here.
- **Silica.** If a lateral has to cross an existing path, you are cutting pavers,
  limestone or concrete, and dry-cutting those releases respirable crystalline
  silica.
  [WA duties on managing silica dust are current and tightening](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/silica).
  Cut wet, outdoors, upwind, in a fitted P2 respirator.

## What a professional does at this stage

They build the entire system **open**, run it wet with no nozzles fitted, prove
it, and only then backfill. An amateur digs, lays, fits heads, backfills, turns
it on, and discovers the problem — and the problem is now under a lawn.

They also carry a **pressure gauge**. The amateur test is "does water come out of
it". The professional test is "how many kilopascals are there at the last head on
station 4", because that single number tells you whether the design survived
contact with the site.

And they write it down while the trench is open, because in three years nobody
remembers where anything is, and Perth sand looks identical everywhere.

The sequence is the real deliverable of this lesson:

1. Set out head positions and trench lines with marking paint
2. Trench, mainline first
3. Lay and join pipe; cap every open end at the end of each day
4. Let joints cure, then pressurise the mainline and walk it looking for leaks
5. **Flush** every lateral from its far end until the water runs clear
6. Fit heads at final grade
7. Set arcs, then radius
8. Measure pressure at the furthest head, station by station
9. Catch-cup the stations that matter
10. Set final run times on the controller
11. Photograph every trench open, with a tape measure in shot
12. Backfill, compact in layers, then record the as-built

## Trench depths

There is no WA regulation setting a minimum depth for private irrigation pipe on
your own block. What sets it is everything that will later be pushed into that
ground: mower blades, edgers, a garden fork, a lawn aerator, a fence post, the
next owner's shovel. Rain Bird's design manual notes that
[a residential sprinkler mainline is often about 300 mm below the surface](https://www.rainbird.com/sites/default/files/media/documents/2018-02/IrrigationDesignManual.pdf),
with 600 mm specified on commercial work — cover is protection, not code.

| Run | Working depth | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mainline (always under pressure) | 300 mm | A mainline failure runs until someone notices. Depth buys protection. |
| Laterals under lawn | 200–250 mm | Below aeration and edging, above the reach of most root systems |
| Laterals in garden beds | 150–200 mm | Deeper than a fork, shallower than a shrub's rootball |
| Under a path, driveway or slab | Sleeved in 100 mm conduit, laid before the base | Retrofitting a sleeve through finished paving costs several hundred dollars |
| Solenoid cable | In the mainline trench, beside the pipe | One trench, one photo, one record |

Three things go wrong in Perth trenches. **Limestone rubble** in the spoil, thrown
straight back on top of the pipe, will chew a hole in poly over years — bed and
cover the pipe with clean screened sand. **Fill collapse**: dry sand walls slump,
so cut narrow, keep spoil on one side and on a tarp, and never work in a trench
deeper than your waist without battering the sides back. And **thermal
movement**: lay poly slightly snaked in the trench rather than pulled tight,
because pipe laid taut at 6am is under tension by mid-afternoon.

## Joining pipe

Metric poly with barbed or compression fittings is the Perth domestic default.
Cut square, warm the pipe end in hot water rather than with a flame, and drive the
fitting **fully home** before the clamp goes on. A barb that is only half seated
will hold at 6am and let go at 2pm when the pipe is warm and soft.

If you are using PVC pressure pipe, the numbers are published and they are strict.
Vinidex's technical note is blunt:
[priming fluid must be used and not substituted](https://www.vinidex.com.au/technical-resources/technical-notes/dos-and-donts-of-solvent-cementing-pressure-pipes-vx-tn-10d/),
cut square with a mitre box, de-burr, coat the socket first, force the spigot
fully home, hold for **30 seconds**, do not move the joint for **5 minutes**, and
do not strain it for **24 hours** before pressure testing. It also says not to work
on hot, windy days, because solvent evaporation increases more than tenfold — which
in Perth means gluing in the morning or the evening and testing the next day, not
an hour later.

## Flush before a single head goes on

Everything that fell in the trench is now inside your pipe: sand, limestone dust,
PVC swarf, insects, a bit of the tarp. Fit it all up and turn it on, and that
material arrives at the smallest orifice in the system and stays there.

Flush the mainline first, at its last fitting, before the valves are connected.
Then, with valves in and **risers fitted but no nozzles or heads**, run each
station and let it purge from the furthest point. Holman's guide gives
[5 to 10 seconds through a bare riser, or 15 to 20 seconds with a flush cap](https://www.holmanindustries.com.au/how-to-flush-your-irrigation-system-and-replace-pop-up-nozzles/),
which is right for seasonal maintenance; on a new install, flush until the water
runs visibly clear, which after a limestone trench takes a minute or more per
lateral. Watch your two-minute-per-station testing limit and work in short bursts.

A blocked nozzle at handover is an irritation. Grit lodged in a solenoid seat is a
valve that weeps forever, and a dripline emitter blocked on day one never comes back.

## Setting heads: grade, then arc, then radius

Set pop-up bodies at **final finished level** — flush with the turf surface or a
few millimetres below, not proud of it — and fit them on flexible risers or swing
joints so a mower wheel breaks nothing more than the joint. Keep spray bodies far
enough back from a hard edge that the body is not clipped by an edger.

Then set the **arc**: most rotors have a fixed start edge and an adjustable stop,
so you set the fixed side against the boundary of the area and wind the arc back
until the far edge lands where you want it. Water landing on limestone paving
stains it, water on a timber fence rots it, and water on the neighbour's side is
water you paid for twice.

Set **radius last, and reluctantly**. The radius screw cuts into the stream and
reduces throw, but it also drops the precipitation rate of that head unevenly,
which shows up later as a dry arc in your catch-cup readings. Change the nozzle
instead. The BMP rule is to keep
[matched precipitation rate sprinklers within plus or minus 5 per cent in a zone](https://www.irrigation.org/IA/FileUploads/IA/Advocacy/Landscape-Irrigation-BMP.pdf),
which in practice means a quarter-circle head carries a quarter nozzle, not the
same nozzle as the full-circle head next to it.

## The commissioning audit

Three measurements per station, in this order.

**1. Pressure at the furthest head.** The Irrigation Association's audit
guidelines call for
[pressure tests at the sprinkler, under normal operating conditions, at the beginning and end of each zone](https://www.irrigation.org/IA/FileUploads/IA/Resources/CLIA-CGIA_AuditGuidelines.pdf).
Do it by fitting a pressure gauge on the riser at the first head and again at the
last, or with a pitot gauge held in the stream. Compare both readings to the
operating range in your nozzle's own chart. Misting and fog mean too much pressure;
short throw, big drops and a dry doughnut around the head mean too little. A large
drop between the first and last head means the lateral is undersized or the station
is carrying too many heads — that is a
[hydraulics](/curriculum/build/irrigation-hydraulics/) problem, not something to
tune out at the controller.

**2. Catch cups and uniformity.** The full method, the DU calculation and the run
time multiplier are in
[Irrigation 5](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/). Run it at
dawn, when Perth is calm — the sea breeze is usually still up at 6pm.

**3. Final run times.** Convert the measured precipitation rate into minutes,
apply the run time multiplier, and check the whole program still finishes before
9am. Then set it in the
[controller](/curriculum/build/irrigation-controllers-and-compliance/).

While you are there, check each station switches cleanly at the controller and
that the valve shuts fully, and take a meter reading before and after one station
so you have a flow figure for the file.

## Photograph before you backfill

This is the ten minutes that saves a day's excavation in five years.

The BMP's record-drawing list is the checklist: sprinkler locations, valves,
sleeving, sensors, mainline and lateral routing, wire routing, flush points and
major trees, each dimensioned **from permanent features** — the meter box, a
building corner, a fence post — not from a garden bed that will move.

Practically: photograph every trench open with a tape measure lying in the shot,
take two offsets to every valve box and every sleeve crossing, number your stations
on the photos, and mark the pipe route on the plan while you can still see it.
Laminate a controller map showing which station covers what and leave it at the
controller.

On a renovation you are also joining new pipe to old. Flush the old side too, and
be honest about it: aged PVC laterals sized for a different design will not deliver
your new station flows, and mixed head types on an inherited station are a
re-valve, not a repair.

## What happens to this next

You now have a working system, a measured pressure and uniformity figure per
station, real run times, and a record of where everything is.

Next comes the part the system exists to support — planting and turf — where the
watering exemption and the run times you just measured decide whether it lives.
Your as-built goes into [handover](/curriculum/handover/as-built-drawings/), and
this same audit, repeated each spring, becomes the
[annual retic audit](/curriculum/maintenance/watering-and-retic-audit/).
