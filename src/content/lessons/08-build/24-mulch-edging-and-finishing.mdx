Mulch and edging are about three per cent of the budget and roughly half of what anyone
sees. A well-designed garden with a soft, undefined edge reads as unfinished. A modest
garden with a hard, straight edge and one consistent mulch reads as professional.

The mistakes here are also cheap to make and expensive to unpick. Mulch washed across the
paving after the first winter storm means the levels were wrong, and levels are the one
thing a rake cannot fix. A bed built up against the house can bridge your termite barrier
and block the weep holes. An edge with nothing physical holding it will be gone in a
season, because kikuyu does not respect intentions.

You are renovating, so most of your edges already exist as habits rather than
construction — a spade line someone re-cut each spring until they stopped. Treat those as
unbuilt.

## What a professional does at this stage

Three things, in this order, and the order is most of the lesson.

1. **Sets the finished levels before ordering anything.** The mulch surface is a design
   level, the same as a paving level. It is decided against the adjacent hard surfaces,
   not by however much mulch turned up on the truck.
2. **Installs the edge before the mulch.** The edge is what makes the depth measurable and
   the line permanent. Mulch first, edge later, is how you get an edge sitting on loose
   material that moves within a month.
3. **Walks the perimeter of the house with a tape.** Wherever the works come within a metre
   of an external wall, they check what the finished level does to the slab edge, the weep
   holes and the termite inspection zone. Ten minutes, and the most expensive thing on this
   page to get wrong.

## Levels first, because mulch floats

A Perth winter storm can drop 20–30 mm in an hour onto ground that has been dry since
November. Woody mulch floats. If the bed surface is flush with the adjacent paving, the
mulch goes onto the paving, down the fall, and into the soakwell — where it stops being a
gardening problem and becomes a drainage problem.

| Interface | Finished mulch surface sits |
| --- | --- |
| Bed against paving or a path | 25–50 mm **below** the paving surface |
| Bed against lawn | Level with, or just under, the top of an edge that stands proud of both |
| Bed against a wall or fence | Below the damp-proof course, clear of any weep holes |
| Bed against a driveway or crossover | 50 mm below, with the fall running back into the bed |

Which means the **soil** surface must be 75–125 mm below the paving before you mulch. On a
renovation you get there by digging the bed down, not by adding soil. Importing soil to
raise a bed is how people bury a slab edge, cover a weep hole and redirect their stormwater
in a single afternoon.

Beds fall away from the house toward a defined discharge point, never into a saucer. A bed
that forms a low spot holds water in winter, drowns the plants, and is still dry in
February. That is a grading problem — go back to
[levels and grading](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/) and
[drainage and soakwells](/curriculum/build/drainage-and-soakwells/) before you spread
anything.

## The strip against the house

Read the durable notice in your meter box. The National Construction Code requires it, and
it tells you what termite management your house has and how it must be inspected.

Building and Energy is blunt: garden beds and plants should be kept away from direct
contact with outside walls, and **hard paving should be substituted**. Soil from garden
beds must not block drainage or ventilation openings in walls. Untreated timber sleepers
should not be used for garden beds or retaining walls, and posts belong in metal stirrups
rather than in the ground.

So: a mulch-free strip of paving or compacted crushed limestone against the wall, graded
away from it, slab edge visible. Bank mulch against your own render and you have bridged
the barrier the house was built with — and you will not see the mud tunnel until it is
inside the wall.

## Choosing the mulch

Water Corporation's specification is the one to follow: **chunky coarse mulch**, better for
water saving than soft fine mulches like straw and lucerne, applied at **5–10 cm**, worth
up to around 20% of your garden water.

Coarse matters more here than almost anywhere. Fine mulch on Perth sand mats into a felt
layer that turns hydrophobic in its own right, sheds rain sideways instead of letting it
through, and wicks soil moisture back up to evaporate. The garden looks mulched and is
drier than before.

| Mulch | Best for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coarse green-waste or tree-lopper chip | Bulk beds, back fence lines, under established trees | Cheapest by far; unknown species mix, variable size, weed seed, no dieback assurance |
| Graded woody mulch, chunky pine or jarrah bark | Front gardens and anywhere seen closely | Consistent size and colour, stays put; several times the price of chip |
| Pea straw, lucerne, shredded lupin | Vegetable beds and hungry exotics only | Feeds the soil fast, mats badly, needs constant topping up — wrong for ornamental beds |
| Gravel, pebble, crushed limestone | Succulent beds, the strip against the house, high-traffic corners | Permanent and non-flammable, adds no nutrient; leaf litter sits on top forever |

Two Perth sourcing rules. Ask for **dieback-free** material — the Dieback Working Group's
advice is to ask for dieback-free soil, gravel, potting mix, mulch and plants — which
matters most if you are planting banksias, grass trees or jarrah. And spread a fresh chip
load within a few days: a big pile of green chip composts internally, heats up and can
smoulder.

Free mulch is a real trade-off: a standby load through MulchNet costs nothing but arrives
whenever it arrives, $49.50 buys priority and $165 buys a guaranteed 5 m³. One cubic metre
covers about 10 m² at 100 mm, or 13 m² at 75 mm.

## Depth, and the two places mulch must never go

Aim for the top of Water Corporation's range, around 75–100 mm settled, on woody mulch.
Under 50 mm suppresses no weeds, which was half the point. Over 100 mm on small plants
starts burying crowns.

**Off the stems.** Water Corporation's verge guidance is explicit — keep mulch clear of the
base of all new plants and trees. Josh Byrne's Perth guide says the same: lay it "keeping
clear of the stem". Leave 50–100 mm of bare soil around a shrub stem and a wider ring
around a tree trunk. Mulch piled against bark holds moisture against living tissue, rots
the collar, and gives borers and termites somewhere dark and damp to work. The plant dies
eighteen months later and nobody connects it to the mulch.

**Off the drippers.** Dripline goes *under* the mulch, on the soil. Emitters sitting on top
wet the mulch, wick sideways and evaporate. Lay it out and pressure-test it before the
mulch goes down — see [irrigation emitters](/curriculum/build/irrigation-emitters/).

Mulch at the end of winter or start of spring, while the ground is still moist and before
the drying winds. If you are mulching new planting inside the 1 June – 31 August sprinkler
switch-off you cannot run sprinklers to settle it in; check Water Corporation's current
rules for what hand watering is allowed, and you can apply in advance for a new-garden
exemption.

## Edges that actually hold

An edge is a structural element. It has a top that is a set level and a bottom embedded in
something that will hold it.

| Edge | Where it works | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steel — galvanised, powdercoat, Corten | Curves and long clean lines | Needs depth in the ground and pinned joints; thin gauge ripples in the heat |
| Aluminium | Light-duty beds, tight radii | Deforms on mower impact |
| Concrete kerb or mowing strip | Lawn-to-bed edges you have to mow | Best long-term answer; needs a proper base, and cutting means silica dust |
| Brick or limestone on edge | Traditional Perth gardens, straight runs | Must be bedded and haunched in mortar or it walks |
| Poly or plastic strip | Nowhere you care about | Lifts out of sand within two summers |
| Timber | Beds well away from the house | Not untreated, and not against the house |

Three rules decide whether it lasts:

- **Set the top, not the bottom.** String line, pegs, checked with a level. The top of the
  edge is what the eye reads, and mulch sits down from it, so the line stays crisp as the
  mulch settles.
- **Embed most of it.** In deep sand the large majority of a steel edge belongs below
  finished soil level with only a small reveal proud. Sand gives almost no lateral support,
  so shallow edges lean.
- **Pin every joint.** Butt joints are the failure point. Overlap and pin them, and stake at
  the manufacturer's spacing rather than at "that feels about right".

Where paving meets anything else, edge restraint is not optional. CMHA's rules transfer
directly: restraint is required along the whole perimeter and wherever the pavement
material changes; at least 25 mm of vertical restraining surface must contact the side of
the paver; restraints must never be installed on top of the bedding sand; and spikes go
into the compacted base, never into soil. In Perth that base is compacted crushed limestone
— see [base preparation](/curriculum/build/base-preparation/) and
[paving](/curriculum/build/paving/).

Set out curves with a garden hose and check the line from inside the house before marking
it. A curve that looks generous on the ground usually looks timid from a window.

## Transitions: pick one rule and repeat it

The tell of an amateur job is that every junction was solved differently.

- **Bed to lawn** — physical edge proud of both, mulch down from it, turf level with its top.
- **Lawn to paving** — mowing strip or paving a few millimetres *below* finished turf level
  so a mower wheel can run on it. Turf sitting above paving means you strim that edge
  fortnightly forever. See [turf for Perth](/curriculum/build/turf-for-perth/).
- **Bed to paving** — mulch 25–50 mm down, restraint continuous, no soil touching the
  paving surface.
- **Level changes** — flush, or a proper step of 150–190 mm. Anything between about 30 and
  100 mm is a trip hazard that reads as a mistake, because it is one.

## Safety on this task

Cutting steel edging, concrete kerbing, pavers or limestone blocks generates respirable
crystalline silica. WA's workplace exposure standard is 0.05 mg/m³, and since 1 September
2024 the WHS regulations impose specific duties on anyone processing crystalline silica
substances at work. Those duties do not bind you on your own home; the disease does. Cut
wet or with on-tool extraction, work outdoors and downwind, and wear a fitted P2
respirator, not a paper nuisance mask.

Bulk mulch is a heat-and-back job done alone in the open on the sunniest part of the block.
Spread in the morning, not at 2pm in February.

If your block sits in a designated bushfire prone area — check the statewide map, the
current version of which took effect on 13 December 2025 — reconsider deep woody mulch
close to the house and use gravel or crushed limestone in that zone instead.

## What happens to this next

Once the mulch is down and the edges are in, the garden is built. What is left is the
difference between built and finished: sweeping, topping up joints, adjusting every
irrigation head now that the planting is in the way, aiming lights after dark, and shooting
the "after" photographs from the same positions as the "before" set.

That is the [final clean and detail](/curriculum/build/final-clean-and-detail/), the
cheapest quality improvement left on the job. After that, the garden's survival depends on
[the first twelve weeks](/curriculum/maintenance/the-first-twelve-weeks/).
