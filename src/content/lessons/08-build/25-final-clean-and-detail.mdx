The build is finished when the last plant goes in the ground. The job is finished
about four hours later.

Those four hours are the cheapest quality you will ever buy. They consume almost
no materials, and every one of them is visible: a swept edge, a joint that is
full, a sprinkler that no longer waters the fence, a light that shows you the tree
instead of shining in your eyes. People skip this because they are tired and the
garden already looks enormously better than it did. That is precisely the moment a
professional slows down. Book it as its own half day, not as the tail end of the
planting day.

## What a professional does on the last day

A landscaper's final clean is scheduled, priced and staffed like any other day on
the job, for two reasons.

The first is commercial. The client's opinion of the entire build forms in the
first ten minutes of the handover walk, and it forms from details. Nobody inspects
your base compaction. Everybody notices a half-empty paver joint, a hose coiled on
the new lawn, and a sprinkler misting the neighbour's car.

The second reason matters more to you, because you are the client. The final clean
is the first close-range look at your own work. Sweeping a paved area square metre
by square metre is how you find the two units that rock, the edge restraint that
has crept, and the dripline you nicked with a spade. Today, with tools still on
site, that is a ten-minute fix. In three weeks it is a day.

So keep a running snag list while you clean. It is the raw material for
[the defect walkthrough](/curriculum/handover/the-defect-walkthrough/).

## Sequence: wet work first, dry work last

Order is the whole trick here. Get it wrong and you do half the work twice.

1. **Cart the rubbish and strip the site.** Offcuts, bags, spare pavers, pallets,
   star pickets. Clean around obstacles and you will clean again.
2. **Run and adjust the irrigation** — the wettest job, so it goes before anything
   you want dry.
3. **Wash and sweep the hard surfaces**, working from the top of the site down, so
   you never wash dirt onto clean work.
4. **Let the paving dry, then top up the joints.** Sand will not run into a damp
   joint. It bridges, cakes, and drops out a fortnight later.
5. **Dress the mulch and the edges** — see
   [mulch, edging and finishing](/curriculum/build/mulch-edging-and-finishing/).
6. **Aim the lighting after dark.**
7. **Photograph the next morning**, in early light.

## Cleaning hard surfaces without wrecking them

**Dry sweeping is a dust hazard, not a chore.** Since 1 September 2024, WA's work
health and safety regulations treat any material containing at least 1% crystalline
silica as a crystalline silica substance — concrete, natural stone, bricks and
tiles are all named — and "processing" explicitly includes cleaning processes such
as sweeping. Those duties bind people working for fee or reward, not a homeowner on
their own block, but the dust does not check your employment status and silicosis
is irreversible. Hose the surface before you sweep, wear a P2 respirator, and do
not put a blower anywhere near cutting fines: a blower is a machine for lifting a
day's worth of silica to head height and then over the fence.

**Pressure washing: volume rinses, pressure damages.** Use a wide fan tip, keep it
moving, and keep it about 300 mm off the surface. A narrow or zero-degree tip on
concrete pavers strips the surface layer and blasts out the joint sand you are
about to spend the afternoon replacing.

**Efflorescence: leave it.** The white bloom on new concrete pavers and limestone
blocks is salt migrating out of the product. It is not a defect and it weathers off
over months. Acid cleaning a new job etches the surface and adds salt, so the
second bloom is often worse than the first. If you must treat it, wait until the
paving has been through a winter and use a buffered proprietary efflorescence
remover, not builder's acid.

**Where the dirty water goes.** Mortar slurry, limestone fines and cement washings
do not belong in the street gully — Perth's stormwater runs to sumps, wetlands and
the river, largely untreated. Squeegee it up, bag it, and take it with the rest of
the waste.

## Topping up the joints

Joints must be bone dry, and so must the sand. Sweep kiln-dried sand across the
paving with a soft broom in several directions, compact it — a plate compactor on
a rubber mat, or firm foot traffic over a small area — then sweep more in. Two or
three passes, then brush the excess off before it stains.

New paving keeps taking sand for months as it settles and the first storms wash the
joints down, so keep the leftover bag dry and do it again at twelve weeks.
Polymeric sand reduces that, but it must go down dry and hazes permanently if you
rush the clean-off — on a first job, plain sand plus a top-up is more forgiving.
Sealing is optional on most Perth jobs and needs at least 24 hours of dry surface
first. See [paving](/curriculum/build/paving/).

## Adjusting every head with the planting in place

You set your arcs during [installation](/curriculum/build/irrigation-install-and-audit/)
against bare soil. Plants change all of it: a shrub now blocks a spray, mulching
has buried three emitters and dragged a dripline sideways, and the shape you are
watering has moved.

**Check what the calendar allows before you plan this.** You may run each station
for no more than two minutes for testing, on your rostered days, before 9am or
after 6pm. Between 1 June and 31 August the Sprinkler Switch-off means you cannot
test your own system at all — only a Waterwise Garden Irrigator can. If your job
lands in winter and you have just planted, apply for a new lawn and garden
exemption on the day of installation; the clock runs from planting, not approval.

Then walk each zone while it runs and fix, in this order:

- **Blocked heads.** A plant in front of a spray is a permanent dry shadow. Move
  the head, raise it to a taller pop-up, or accept you will lose that plant.
- **Arc and radius.** Water on paving, fences, walls or the neighbour's side is
  waste, and on limestone and render it is staining. Wind the radius screw in
  rather than tolerating overspray.
- **Every drip emitter, by hand.** Pull the mulch back and confirm each one is
  discharging at the plant, not 200 mm away.
- **The filter**, which is full of the debris the install pushed through.
- **Final run times**, written down and carried into
  [scheduling and uniformity](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/).

## Aiming the lighting after dark

You cannot aim a garden light in daylight. Make a separate visit after dark with a
hex key, a rag and someone to stand where you tell them.

Aim from where people actually are: the seat, the back door, the kitchen window,
then from the street and from the neighbour's side of the fence. The rule is that
you should see the effect and not the source. If the lamp is visible from a seating
position, shield it, rotate it, drop it lower or point it further off-axis. Uplights
work best aimed across a trunk from slightly behind, so the canopy lights up and the
beam never crosses an eyeline. Keep the colour temperature warm and the output lower
than instinct suggests — a dark garden needs very little light, and too much of it
kills the effect.

Set the timer or photocell before you leave. And a reminder from
[garden lighting](/curriculum/build/garden-lighting/): any mains-voltage work,
including hard-wiring a transformer or adding an outdoor power point, must be done
by a licensed electrical contractor in WA.

## The "after" photographs

Pull up the "before" set you shot in
[your project workbook](/curriculum/trade-and-process/your-project-workbook/) and
repeat it exactly: same standing spot, same aiming point, same eye height, same
lens on your phone — never the 0.5x ultrawide. Small changes in position, height or
direction destroy the comparison, which is the whole value of the exercise. Shoot
early morning or late afternoon, never midday.

Take the boring photographs too: each valve box open with its station number
visible, the controller screen showing the programme, the transformer, and the
soakwell and mainline positions before mulch hides them forever. That set becomes
your [as-built record](/curriculum/handover/as-built-drawings/), and in five years
it will be worth more than any of the pretty ones.

Then take the tools off site. Not into the shed to deal with later. Off site.

The next phase stops admiring the garden and starts inspecting it, beginning with
the defect walkthrough — where that running snag list becomes a proper list with a
fix and a date against every line.
