You are renovating, so most of your garden beds already exist. That means they
already contain twenty years of nothing: leached sand, a crust of old mulch, and
a bit of chicken manure someone scattered in 2011. The plants growing in it are
alive because the retic runs, not because the soil works.

This is the last cheap moment to fix that. Once plants are in and mulch is down,
you are limited to top-dressing for the next decade, and top-dressing sand does
almost nothing — the good stuff sits in a 50 mm band on top while the roots stay
in dry, hydrophobic sand underneath.

Do it now, properly, and in the right order.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper treats this as three separate jobs that happen to occur on the same
day, not as one product you buy.

| Job | Material | Lifespan | What it actually does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Capital** | Clay | Permanent | Gives the sand a surface for water and nutrient to cling to. Buy once. |
| **Consumable** | Compost, manure, soil conditioner | 1–3 years | Structure, biology, cation exchange, moisture. Repeats forever. |
| **Bridge** | Wetting agent | 3–6 months | Lets water enter repellent sand at all, while the other two work. |

They also **zone the amendment**. Turf, a shrub bed, a native bed and a vegetable
bed get different depths and different recipes; one truckload of "garden mix"
spread evenly across the block overfeeds the natives, underfeeds the vegetables
and wastes money on the lawn.

And they get the sequence right. Soil goes in after every hard surface, drainage
line and irrigation trench is finished and tested, and before planting, turf and
mulch. Amend first and machinery and spoil will undo it.

## Step 1: match the depth to what is going in

Both the [Water Corporation](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/Help-and-advice/Waterwise/Garden/Improving-your-soil)
and [Green Life Soil Co](https://greenlifesoil.com.au/sustainable-gardening-tips/turn-sand-into-soil)
land on the same working depth: **300 mm**, roughly a spade's depth. That is the
default. Vary it deliberately.

| Area | Amendment depth | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lawn | 100–150 mm | Turf roots are shallow. Deeper is money spent below the root zone. |
| General shrub and perennial beds | 300 mm | The default. |
| Vegetable beds | 300–400 mm, richest mix | The one place you can genuinely overdo compost and get away with it. |
| WA native beds | 200–300 mm, lean mix | Clay yes, wetting agent yes, heavy compost and manure no. |
| Individual tree pits | Do **not** enrich the pit | Amend a wide, shallow area instead — see below. |

Work out volume before you ring a supplier. A 10 m² bed amended to 300 mm is 3 m³
of soil you are modifying; at 20% compost by volume that is 60 litres per square
metre, or 0.6 m³ for the bed. Yards sell by the cubic metre with a minimum
delivery, so total the whole block and order once — the numbers belong in your
[quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/).

The tree-pit rule catches people out. A rich, soft pit in surrounding sand
becomes a bathtub the roots refuse to leave. Amend a wide shallow circle instead,
two to three times the root ball diameter, and backfill the pit with what came
out of it.

## Step 2: clay, at a rate that does something

This is the part everyone skips, and it is the only part that is permanent.

The [Soil Quality Knowledge Base](https://www.soilquality.org.au/factsheets/water-repellency)
gives the target that makes this measurable: lift the clay content of the
surface soil to **at least 5%**. On broadacre sand that means at least 100 tonnes
per hectare of clay-rich subsoil — which is 10 kg per square metre. That number
tells you instantly whether a bagged product is doing anything.

| Clay | Rate | Why the rates differ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bentonite** (smectite) | 500 g – 1 kg/m² to start, up to 1–5% by weight | Swelling clay, enormous surface area per kilogram. A little goes a long way, and too much turns sand greasy and waterlogged. |
| **Kaolinite** (e.g. the Perth "clay" sold in bulk bags) | 5–15 kg/m² | Non-swelling, much lower surface area. You need an order of magnitude more of it. This is the range that lines up with the 10 kg/m² broadacre figure. |

Rates from [Green Life Soil Co](https://greenlifesoil.com.au/sustainable-gardening-tips/turn-sand-into-soil),
along with two operational details that matter more than the numbers. **Spread
bentonite onto dry soil** — wet it and it clumps to itself instead of dispersing
through the sand. And **in an established garden, start at the low end**; you
cannot take clay back out, so add, watch a season, add again.

Your soil type from [Perth soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) changes
the emphasis. Deep pale Bassendean sand is the worst case and needs the most of
everything. Shallow brown sand over limestone — Karrakatta — is already alkaline,
so keep lime-based products out of it and re-check pH against your
[soil testing](/curriculum/site-analysis/soil-testing/) baseline.

## Step 3: organic matter, and how to buy it

Green Life publishes the number that reframes this whole job: roughly **70% of
added organic matter decomposes in the first year, and 50% of what is left in
the second**. This is an annual task, not a one-off — though their estimate is
that you need progressively less over three to five years as humus builds.

When you buy in bulk, ask the yard four questions. Is it composted or raw? Raw
green waste and fresh manure rob nitrogen while they finish breaking down. Is it
made to AS 4454, the Australian Standard for composts, soil conditioners and
mulches — and can they show you the current product analysis rather than a
brochure? What is in it: green waste, food organics, manure, biosolids? And when
was the pile last turned? Then use your nose and your hand. Mature compost smells
like a forest floor and is cool in the middle. A steaming stockpile is still
cooking; sour or ammonia smells mean immature.

Native beds are the exception that catches Perth renovators. Josh Byrne's
[Guide to Waterwise Gardening](https://joshshouse.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/2_160711_Waterwise_Gardening.pdf)
puts it plainly: manure is nutrient-rich, should be used lightly, and "may also
harm some native plants". Many WA species — banksias, grevilleas, hakeas and
their relatives — evolved on some of the most phosphorus-poor soil on earth and
will decline in a bed you have generously enriched. Give natives clay, a light
compost, a wetting agent and coarse mulch. Save the manure for the vegetables.

## Step 4: incorporate, do not spread

Spreading 50 mm of black soil conditioner over hydrophobic sand and calling it
soil improvement is the most common expensive mistake in Perth gardens. Water
runs sideways off the layer, roots stay in it, and the first 38-degree week kills
everything.

The method:

1. Clear the bed and weed it.
2. Spread the **clay** evenly onto dry sand.
3. Spread the **compost** on top of the clay.
4. Turn it all through to your target depth. By hand: a fork, two passes at right
   angles to each other. By machine: a rotary hoe, two passes, or an excavator
   bucket rolling the material for large open areas.
5. Rake back to level and to your finished levels.

### Before you put anything spinning into the ground

- **Locate your services.** A rotary hoe at 300 mm finds retic laterals (usually
  100–150 mm down), irrigation cable and unmarked power to a shed. Mark what you
  installed in
  [irrigation install and audit](/curriculum/build/irrigation-install-and-audit/)
  and cross-check [locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/).
  Amend the beds before the final dripline goes down, or lift and re-peg it.
- **Do not hoe inside a retained tree's root zone.** You will cut the feeder
  roots keeping it alive. Hand-amend the outer edge only, and follow whatever the
  [tree assessment](/curriculum/site-analysis/tree-assessment/) said.
- **Rotary hoes kick.** They bite on roots and buried rubble and jump backwards.
  Firm grip, both hands, feet clear, nobody standing behind you.
- **Damp the material down before you move it, wear gloves, wear a mask.**
  Legionella lives in compost, mulch and bulk soil, and the soil-borne
  *Legionella longbeachae* is
  [the most common strain in WA](https://www.healthywa.wa.gov.au/Articles/J_M/Legionnaires-disease)
  — [HealthyWA](https://www.healthywa.wa.gov.au/Articles/J_M/Minimising-the-risk-of-a-Legionella-infection-at-home)
  sets out the precautions. Moving cubic metres by wheelbarrow alone in a Perth
  summer is its own hazard; this is autumn or spring work.

## Step 5: wetting agent, applied and watered in

Wetting agent goes on **after** incorporation and **before** planting, across the
whole amended area. Granular spreads more evenly on a large bed; liquid through a
hose applicator suits small or awkward areas.

Water Corporation's instruction is worth following literally: **water it in until
it foams.** The foam is the surfactant activating. Their timing is the start of
winter and again in early summer, then as the manufacturer directs.

Understand what you have bought. A wetting agent is a surfactant with a three to
six month working life. It does not build soil. It buys you the season in which
the clay and the compost start to matter.

## Step 6: wet it up and let it settle before you plant

Freshly turned amended sand is full of air and will drop. Plant into it
immediately and your root balls end up either proud and drying out, or buried
and rotting.

Soak the bed thoroughly, let it drain, soak it again, over two or three days.
Expect the surface to settle 20–40 mm; top up and re-level. A day after the last
soak, dig a test hole 200 mm deep. Damp all the way down means the amendment is
working. A dry band means you have not moved enough water through it, or the clay
did not mix properly — fix that now, not after planting.

## Timing, and the sprinkler switch-off

Water Corporation names autumn or spring as the window for soil improvement, and
that is not only about plant stress. Between **1 June and 31 August** the
Sprinkler Switch-off applies across Perth, Mandurah and parts of the South West
and Great Southern, to bore users as well as scheme users, with a **$100 fine**.
Outside that window you may water once before 9am or after 6pm on your rostered
days.

April–May or September therefore gives you a retic system you can legally use to
wet up and settle the beds. Mid-winter soil work means relying on rain and hand
watering — confirm with Water Corporation what hand watering is permitted before
you plan around it.

## What this changes downstream

The bed you have just built is what [planting](/curriculum/build/planting/) goes
into, and it changes spacing and establishment watering.
[Turf](/curriculum/build/turf-for-perth/) sits on the shallower version of it.
[Mulch, edging and finishing](/curriculum/build/mulch-edging-and-finishing/)
locks it in under 5–10 cm of coarse mulch, and
[fertilising Perth sand](/curriculum/maintenance/fertilising-perth-sand/) is the
annual repeat of the consumable half of this lesson.

Write down what you put where — clay type and rate, compost type and volume,
depth, date. In two years you will want to know, and nobody ever remembers.
