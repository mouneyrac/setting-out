Machinery is not a horsepower decision. It is an access decision measured in
millimetres, and a liability decision buried in a hire contract most people sign
without reading.

On a renovation the access is already built and you do not get to change it: a 900 mm
side gate, a meter box, a paved path you intend to keep, a tree whose roots you are not
allowed to chew up. When this goes wrong it is not subtle — the float arrives, the
machine will not fit, and you pay the delivery both ways for nothing.

## What a professional does at this stage

They walk the access route with a tape, write the narrowest point down in millimetres,
and book the **largest machine that fits with clearance**. Largest, because machine time
is the cheapest labour on a site and a machine one size too small turns a one-day job
into three.

They lodge the [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/) enquiry days before
the machine is booked, not the morning it arrives, because each asset owner returns its
plans in its own time.

And they book a machine they can already drive. Learning to operate on your own job, with
the day rate running, is the most expensive training in this trade.

## Measure the access before you look at machines

Walk the route from street to work area and record, in millimetres:

- **Narrowest gap** — gate posts, meter box to wall, tap to fence. One pinch point decides it.
- **Lowest overhead** — eaves, patio beam, pergola, low branch, the service wire over your verge.
- **Tightest turn** — corners the machine must swing through, not just pass along.
- **Steps** — a machine that cannot climb a 200 mm step stops at the step.
- **What it crosses** — paving you are keeping, lawn, a soakwell lid, the sewer junction, live retic.

Allow transport width plus about 100 mm each side, and get that width from the depot in
writing, quoting the model number. "1.7 tonne" is a weight, not a width, and two machines
of the same tonnage can differ by 300 mm depending on whether the undercarriage retracts.

Crossing paving or turf you are keeping is a plywood or track-mat job — plan it in
[site establishment](/curriculum/build/site-establishment-and-safety/). Rubber tracks are
gentler than steel; a wheeled skid steer scuffs every time it turns.

## The machines, and what each is honestly for

| Machine | Typical transport width | Best at | Worst at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Shovel and barrow** | ~600 mm | Working within a metre of services, roots and footings | Volume, and February |
| **Mini loader** (Dingo / Kanga class) | 750–900 mm | Standard side gates; shifting soil, sand and mulch | Deep digging, lifting, limestone |
| **Mini excavator, 1–1.7 t** | 1,000–1,300 mm | Soakwell holes, trenches, stumps, digging tight to a boundary | Moving spoil — it digs, it does not cart |
| **Excavator, 2.5–5 t** | 1,500–2,000 mm | Limestone, depth, big root balls, real volume | Fitting down the side of a Perth house |
| **Skid steer / posi-track ("bobcat")** | ~1,000 mm mini to ~1,900 mm | Spreading bulk, levelling, loading a truck | Precision; wheeled ones tear turf |
| **Dumper or tipper** | Varies | Getting spoil off site | Everything else |

Those widths are the shape of the market, not a guarantee — confirm each against the spec
sheet for the machine your depot is actually sending. Perth sand digs easily, so a small
machine goes further here than it would in Melbourne clay; but if your
[soil profile](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/) hits cemented limestone at 400 mm, a
1.7 t machine bounces off it. That is a bigger machine or a rock breaker, and a cost you
find in the test hole rather than on the day.

Hand digging stays on the list. It is the only sensible method within about a metre of a
known service or a retained tree's root zone, and it is how you pothole. The limit is heat.

## Dry hire versus wet hire

**Dry hire** is the machine only: you drive it and every consequence is yours. **Wet hire**
is machine plus operator, quoted by the hour with a minimum and usually a separate float
charge to get the machine there and back, with the operator's employer carrying the
insurance and the work health and safety duty for that operator's work.

The comparison people get wrong is time. On your first half day in a mini excavator you
will do perhaps a third of what an experienced operator does, and do it more roughly. A
"cheap" two-day dry hire regularly costs more than the half-day of wet hire that would
have finished the same task.

| | Dry hire | Wet hire |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You control | Timing, sequence, idle time | Little — you brief and supervise |
| You carry | Damage, downtime, services strikes, spoil | Less, but read the contract before assuming none |
| Suits | Simple bulk work, long durations, your second job | Tight access, one-day precision work, anything near services |
| Hidden cost | Your learning curve, and your errors | Minimum hire and float charges on a short job |

Wet hire wins when the job is short, precise, near something expensive, or when you would
otherwise be learning on it — [DIY versus subcontract](/curriculum/costing/diy-vs-subcontract/),
applied to a single day.

## What it costs beyond the day rate

Perth rates are set per depot and move constantly, so ring three — the yards around
Welshpool, Malaga, Bibra Lake, Canning Vale and O'Connor — and compare the **total**, not
the headline.

The trap is the word "day". At one national chain the day rate runs 24 hours from the start
of hire, a half day is four trading hours, a week is seven days, and extra days past a week
are charged at 20 per cent of the week rate, with the cheapest applicable rate applied
automatically
([Kennards Hire rates FAQs](https://www.kennards.com.au/help-centre/rates-and-payment-faqs)).
Then add delivery and pick-up, which is two float movements and often the biggest line on a
one-day hire; attachments, priced separately; fuel at a premium rate if returned empty; and
the bin, because [spoil is charged by volume](/curriculum/build/demolition-and-clearing/). If
you self-collect, a 1.7 t machine plus plant trailer is well over 2 tonnes — check the towing
capacity and tow ball download on your vehicle's compliance plate. Most people should pay for
delivery. All of it belongs in
[labour, hire and hidden costs](/curriculum/costing/labour-hire-and-hidden-costs/).

## Insurance, damage waiver, and what you are really liable for

A damage waiver is not insurance. It is a contractual cap with holes in it, and you are still
required to hold your own cover.

The pattern is consistent. The hirer is responsible for loss or theft outright, pays the full
cost of repairing damage they caused, and must hold adequate insurance for liabilities arising
from use of the equipment. The basic waiver caps repair exposure at an excess — at one national
chain, the lesser of the actual repair cost or 20 per cent of replacement cost — and it **falls
away entirely** if the operator is not properly licensed, the machine is rolled, it collides
with an overhead structure through insufficient clearance, it is overloaded, or it is used on
unsealed or non-public roads
([Kennards Hire contract conditions](https://www.kennards.com.au/hire-contract-conditions)).

So before you sign: ask your home and contents insurer, in writing, whether they cover
liability arising from your operation of hired plant on your own property, because many
policies do not; photograph the machine on all four sides with the hour meter visible before
it leaves the yard; and ask what the waiver excludes for underground services, because damage
to a cable or a main is usually yours regardless, and the bill is the utility's repair cost.

Add the crossover and verge. Tracking a machine over a kerb or footpath can leave you paying
for the repair, and Perth councils routinely hold bonds for exactly this — ask yours before
the machine arrives.

## Underground services: the one that ends projects

Lodge a free [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/) enquiry before you book
anything. Then treat the plans as approximate, because they are. The industry sequence is plan,
prepare, pothole, protect, proceed, and potholing means hand digging or soft-dig excavation to
physically expose the asset before the machine swings.

Water Corporation's own instruction is to pothole at 5 m intervals where you dig parallel to a
pipeline, and to backfill with clean bedding material for the first 300 mm above an exposed
asset. Electronic detection cannot find non-metallic pipe, which is most modern water service
and all poly retic.

BYDA plans also stop at the meter. Old power to a shed, the pool pump feed, unmarked poly, a
forgotten soakwell: those are yours to find, and
[locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/) is where you found them.

## The hazards that actually hurt people

- **Ground collapse.** Perth sand will not hold a vertical face and a cubic metre of it weighs
  well over a tonne. Never enter an unbattered excavation. Deeper than 1.5 m is high risk
  construction work with mandatory controls.
- **Overhead powerlines.** Booms, tipper trays, and the machine on the trailer. Western Power
  records 30 or more accidental contacts with its network every month — ring them for the
  approach distance rather than eyeballing it.
- **Being struck or crushed.** Nobody stands within slewing range. Most small-machine injuries
  are to the person on the ground, not the operator.
- **Rollover.** Tracks square to the slope, bucket low, never travel across a batter face,
  seatbelt on.
- **Silica dust.** Breakers and saws on concrete, limestone, brick and pavers generate
  [respirable crystalline silica](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/silica) — water suppression and
  proper respiratory protection, not a hardware-aisle dust mask.
- **Working alone in heat.** Machine work is sedentary and deceptively hot. Tell someone your
  finish time.

Your machine booking now has a date on it, and that date has to cover every task needing the
machine, because bringing one in twice is money you do not get back. Sequence it in
