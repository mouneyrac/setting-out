Everything in the first five irrigation lessons happened on paper. This one gets
buried. If the mainline runs where the alfresco slab is going, or the valves end
up under 90 square metres of limestone paving, you will not find out for two
years — and then it costs four figures to find out. A legal line also runs through
this lesson, so it goes first: part of this work is not yours to do, and it is the
part that protects the drinking water supply.

## Where the licensed trade starts and stops

Regulation 4 of the Plumbers Licensing and Plumbing Standards Regulations 2000
defines water supply plumbing work as supplying water **from a water supply system
to a point of discharge** — and for an irrigation service it defines that point of
discharge as including *the outlet of the backflow prevention device* required
under the plumbing standards. That draws the line precisely.

| Task | Who |
| --- | --- |
| Cutting into the service line downstream of the meter | Licensed plumbing contractor |
| Isolating valve and the backflow prevention device | Licensed plumbing contractor |
| Mainline, valve manifolds, solenoids, wiring, laterals, heads | You |
| A new 240 V weatherproof power point for the controller | Licensed electrical contractor |
| Pulling 24 V solenoid cable | You |

Unlicensed plumbing work carries a **$5,000 fine** under regulation 9(1), and the
list of plumbing work a WA homeowner may legally do on their own home is five
items long — shower head, tap repair, cistern washer, filter cartridge, plunger.
Retic cut-ins are not on it.

In Perth the trade calls this job a **retic cut-in**: the plumber exposes the
copper service downstream of your meter, cuts in a tee, and brings up a stub
fitted with an isolating ball valve and a dual check valve. Book it early. It
typically sits two to three weeks out in a plumber's diary, and everything
downstream of that stub waits on it.

## Backflow prevention is two obligations, not one

**Containment at the boundary.** Water Corporation's Backflow Prevention Policy
requires an approved containment device at the boundary of every water service
connection, backed by regulations 42 and 43 of the Water Services Regulations
2013. Water Corporation can order you to install one and can restrict or
disconnect your supply if you do not. Your risk rating — high, medium or low — is
assigned at building approval. High and medium properties get a **testable**
device: tested at commissioning, after any maintenance, and **every 12 months** by
a licensed plumbing contractor, with the report lodged within five working days.

**Protection of the irrigation service itself.** A containment device *does not*
remove the need for zone and individual protection under the plumbing standards,
and Water Corporation does not manage those — they sit with your plumber and the
Plumbing Code. Under NCC 2025 table B5D7 a Type C irrigation system is **medium
hazard** for zone protection, and a Type D system, injected with fertilisers,
herbicides, nematicides, insecticides or weedicides, is **high hazard**.

Read that last one twice. A fertiliser injector changes the hazard class of the
whole service, and high hazard means a testable device with an annual test forever.
Decide about fertigation now, and price both before you fall in love with the idea.

Two more that quietly bite in Perth:

- **Never cross-connect a bore to the scheme supply.** A bore pump running above
  main pressure pushes bore water backwards into the drinking water supply. That
  is backpressure backflow, exactly what the policy exists to stop. Bore and scheme
  retic stay on separate pipework.
- **Backflow devices eat pressure.** Water Corporation says so directly. The figure
  you measured in [Irrigation 2](/curriculum/build/irrigation-water-source/)
  predates the device. Re-measure at the stub once the plumber leaves and check
  your station flows still work.

## What a professional does at this stage

They walk the site with the concept plan and the paving setout in hand and route
the mainline **around** every future hard surface before a trench is opened. Not
because pipe under paving fails more often — it does not — but because the one time
it does, the repair is a demolition job.

Then they sleeve. Every crossing of a future path, driveway, slab or wall gets an
oversized conduit — 100 mm stormwater pipe is the usual choice — with a draw string
in it, both ends marked with a star picket, before the base goes in. A sleeve costs
about $15 now; retrofitting one through a finished driveway costs several hundred
and looks like it. They over-provide on everything that cannot be added later: a
spare sleeve, two spare cores, one spare station on the controller.

## Sizing the mainline

Size the mainline on **velocity**, not on whether water reaches the far end. The
Rain Bird manual's working limit is **1.5 metres per second**. Above that you get
friction loss you did not plan for, plus water hammer — the bang when a solenoid
shuts and a moving column of water stops in about 50 milliseconds. Water hammer is
what splits solvent joints and cracks valve bodies eighteen months after handover.

Indicative maximum flows at 1.5 m/s, using typical internal diameters:

| Nominal pipe | Approx. internal diameter | Max flow at 1.5 m/s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 19 mm poly | ~16 mm | ~18 L/min |
| 25 mm poly | ~21.5 mm | ~33 L/min |
| 32 mm poly | ~27.5 mm | ~53 L/min |
| 40 mm poly | ~34.5 mm | ~84 L/min |

Internal diameter changes with pressure class, so confirm against your supplier's
table. Size for your **largest station flow** from
[Irrigation 3](/curriculum/build/irrigation-hydrozoning-and-stations/), then go up
one size if the block is long. Oversized mainline is the cheapest insurance on the
job; undersized mainline is a fault you cannot tune out at the controller.

Metric **poly** with barbed or compression fittings is the Perth domestic default —
flexible, forgiving of sand movement, repairable wet. **PVC pressure pipe** to
AS/NZS 1477, solvent-welded, is stiffer and cheaper in larger sizes, but a bad
solvent joint is a slow leak you will never find. Either way buy pressure-rated
pipe: black poly sold for drainage is not the same product.

## Valve boxes

The Rain Bird manual states it flatly — valves installed below ground **must be
housed in valve boxes and not buried directly in the ground**. A buried solenoid
is a fault you will excavate a lawn to find.

Group two to four valves on a manifold in one rectangular box, then place it:

- **Not under future paving, decking, a slab or a raised bed**, and not in open
  lawn where the mower scalps the lid. Check the setout, not what is there now.
- Against a bed edge, beside a downpipe, in the side access — somewhere a person
  can kneel with a spanner in ten years.
- **Bedded on 100 mm of blue metal, never sand.** A box bedded on Perth sand fills
  within two summers and you will be scooping it out with a jar lid to reach a
  solenoid. Keep solenoid tops above the gravel line, and put a paver under each
  valve so the manifold does not sink.

Set the lid flush or 10 mm proud, number it with the stations inside, and coil a
300 mm service loop of cable in the box so a solenoid can be swapped without
stretching the wire.

## Solenoid wiring

Solenoids run at 24 V AC — below the 50 V AC threshold in the Electricity
(Licensing) Regulations 1991, so pulling solenoid cable is not licensed electrical
work in WA. The controller's 240 V supply is another matter: if there is no
weatherproof outdoor power point already, a licensed electrical contractor
installs it. Power and water in a garden is not where you save $250.

Each valve needs two conductors — its own station wire, and the **common**, shared
by every valve and returned to the controller. Use **multi-core direct-burial
irrigation cable**, not figure-8, not TPS. Holman's published size guide is 0.5 mm
to 50 m, 1.0 mm to 100 m, 1.5 mm to 200 m and 2.0 mm to 400 m; measure the cable
route, not the straight line.

Four rules separate a system that lasts from one that does not:

1. **Gel-filled or grease-filled direct-burial connectors only.** Holman's own line
   is that most field failures come from poor connections. Insulation tape is not
   a joint. Plain twist connectors are not a joint.
2. **Every joint lives inside a valve box** — never a splice loose in a trench.
3. **Pull at least two spare cores** and cap them in the furthest box. A broken
   core with no spare means trenching the whole run again.
4. **Run the cable in the mainline trench**, beside the pipe and through the same
   sleeves. Photograph every trench open, tape measure in shot, before backfilling.

Pick a colour convention — white for common is usual — and record which colour goes
to which station.

## Digging safely on a block someone else built

Lodge a free [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/) enquiry before
you open a trench. On a renovation what hurts is the unmarked consumer mains to a
shed, the gas service and the NBN lead-in, and plans show approximate positions
only — hand-dig to expose anything you cross.

Perth's other trenching hazard is limestone. Cutting limestone, concrete or pavers
dry releases respirable crystalline silica. Cut wet, outdoors and upwind, in a
properly fitted P2 respirator, with everyone else clear. Never dry-cut with an
angle grinder because it is quicker.
