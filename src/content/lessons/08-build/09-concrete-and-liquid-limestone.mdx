Concrete is the only thing you will build in this garden that cannot be adjusted
afterwards. Pavers lift. Decking boards unscrew. A slab poured 30 mm too low, or
falling towards the house instead of away from it, comes out with a jackhammer and a
skip bin.

That is the whole psychology of this lesson. Almost all the work happens before the
truck arrives, and the truck arrives whether you are ready or not. On a renovation it
is harder again, because you are pouring against things that already exist — a house
slab with its own floor level, an old path you must match into, a limestone wall.
Each is a fixed level you work to, not one you choose.

## Three hazards that are not theoretical

**Wet concrete burns you.** Not irritates — burns. Cement Australia's safety data
sheet for wet mix concrete carries the signal word *Danger* and the statement "causes
severe skin burns and eye damage", because wetted cement forms a caustic solution at
pH 11 or above. The cruel part is the delay: first, second or third degree burns may
appear **12 to 48 hours** after a one to six hour exposure, with no obvious pain at the
time. People are injured kneeling in a slab, or by a splash that runs into a gumboot
and stays there all afternoon. Gumboots, long sleeves, waterproof gloves, eye
protection; never kneel in it; and if it gets inside a boot or glove, stop and wash
immediately, not at smoko. Eye contact means 15 minutes of running water and medical
attention.

**Cutting and grinding it releases silica.** Saw-cutting control joints, trimming an
edge or honing a surface generates respirable crystalline silica, and the WA workplace
exposure standard is
[0.05 mg per cubic metre over eight hours](https://www.worksafe.wa.gov.au/whs-duties-businesses-who-work-crystalline-silica-substances).
Cut wet, always, and wear a fitted P2. The formal duties bind businesses rather than a
homeowner on their own block, but the dust does the same thing to your lungs either
way, and any contractor you engage must comply.

**It is heavy and it is hot.** Wet concrete runs a bit over two tonnes per cubic metre,
so a 20 square metre patio at 100 mm is around five tonnes crossing your garden. Trucks,
barrow runs and pumps all travel over soakwell lids, retic mainline and whatever else
you mapped in [earthworks and setting out](/curriculum/build/earthworks-and-setting-out/).
And pours start early because the alternative is working through the hottest part of a
February day.

## What a professional does at this stage

A concreter treats the pour as an inspection, not an activity. The day before, they
walk the job with a string line and a level and answer five questions. If any answer
is wrong, the truck moves to another day.

1. **Do the falls work** — off the slab, away from the house, to somewhere that can
   take the water?
2. **Is the formwork braced** hard enough to hold shape under wet concrete pushing
   sideways?
3. **Will the reinforcement stay where it is meant to sit?**
4. **Are there enough hands** to place and finish this in the time the weather allows?
5. **What is the curing plan**, and is the material for it already on site?

The amateur version orders concrete first and improvises the rest. That is how you get a
slab that ponds, mesh sitting on the sand doing nothing, and a crazed grey surface.

## Falls, formwork and base

Set falls first; everything follows them. WALGA's residential path guidance recommends
a crossfall of **2.0 to 2.5%** to shed water without ponding, 2.5% being the maximum
for accessibility — 20 to 25 mm per metre, and a sound default for garden flatwork.
Fall away from the house, never towards it, never onto a neighbour. Take the finished
levels straight off your
[levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/).

Formwork is typically 90 x 35 hardwood or form ply on stakes at roughly 600 mm centres,
with the top edge set exactly at finished level, because you screed off it. Check the
diagonals, check the string line, and check no stake sits proud where the screed must
run. Oil the forms before the mesh goes in. The City of Stirling spec requires forms to
stay on **at least eight hours**; longer helps curing.

Underneath, the base is the same job as
[base preparation](/curriculum/build/base-preparation/) — compacted subgrade, no soft
spots, no uncompacted service trench through the middle. Stirling's benchmark is a Perth
penetrometer reading of **seven blows per 300 mm**, calibrated to 95% maximum dry
density. Moisten the subgrade before the pour so dry Perth sand does not draw water out
of the bottom of the slab. Moist, not flooded.

## Reinforcement: the mistake nobody can see afterwards

Mesh laid on the ground is decoration. Steel only works in the part of the slab that is
in tension, and it must be surrounded by concrete or it rusts and splits the slab from
the inside. So mesh sits on **bar chairs**, tied so it cannot shift when a barrow wheel
or a boot lands on it, at the cover shown on your plan. Hooking mesh upwards during the
pour is not a substitute, and neither are synthetic fibres — CCAA is explicit that
fibres reduce early plastic shrinkage but do not replace mesh or bar, and that joint
spacing stays the same as for plain concrete.

## Ordering

Ring the batching plant with all of it at once. Concrete is ordered in 0.2 cubic metre
increments, so round up and add about 10%.

| Tell them | Typical domestic answer |
| --- | --- |
| What it is for | Path, patio, driveway, exposed aggregate |
| Strength grade | N20 or N25; CCAA sets 25 MPa minimum if coloured or decorative |
| Slump | Around 75 mm (normal class runs 20–120 mm) |
| Maximum aggregate | 20 mm, or 10 mm for thin or detailed work |
| Volume | Area x thickness, plus 10% |
| How it is placed | Chute, barrow or pump |
| First truck, and spacing between trucks | Early, and only as fast as you can place |

The most expensive thing you can do on pour day is ask the driver to add water to loosen
the mix. It costs strength and durability and raises drying shrinkage — an easier
morning in exchange for a cracked slab.

## The pour, and the finish

Place continuously, working to a live front; do not let one part stiffen while you
fetch the next barrow. Compact into the corners and against the forms, screed off,
bull-float, then **wait**. Stirling's spec puts it exactly right: final finishing does
not start until the bleed water has gone. Working bleed water back into the surface is
what produces a dusty, flaking, delaminated finish twelve months later.

| Finish | How it is made | Perth notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Broom** | Drag a broom across the stiffening surface; Stirling specifies about 2 mm of scarification | Cheapest, best slip resistance, the default for crossovers and side access |
| **Exposed aggregate** | Wash the paste off once the surface is firm but not dry, usually with a surface retarder | Very common here; aggregate choice sets the colour. Hides marks, harder underfoot |
| **Honed / polished** | Grind the cured surface to cut into the aggregate, then seal | Expensive, glare-prone in full sun, and grinding is a silica job — wet cut, fitted P2 |
| **Liquid limestone** | Crushed limestone blended with cement over galvanised mesh, joints cut, sealed | A WA speciality: pale and cooler underfoot, but porous, needs re-sealing every two to three years, and it is not MPa rated. Stirling requires no less than 100 mm for vehicle traffic |

One Perth installer publishes
[around $65 per square metre](https://www.fluidconcrete.com.au/what-to-know-before-choosing-liquid-limestone-for-your-outdoor-space/)
as a starting rate for liquid limestone. Treat that as a figure to argue from, not a
quote, and price your own job in
[quantity take-offs](/curriculum/costing/quantity-take-offs/).

## Joints, and curing in a Perth summer

Concrete cracks. Joints decide where. In an unreinforced slab, tool or saw control
joints at **20 to 25 times the slab thickness** — about 3 m maximum in a 100 mm slab —
cut a quarter to a third of the depth, dividing it into roughly square panels with
sides no worse than 1:1.5. Put a full-depth isolation joint anywhere the slab meets
the house, a wall or a step, so the two move independently.

Then curing, which is where Perth pours fail. AS 1379 caps concrete temperature at
delivery at 35 degrees; CCAA recommends precautions once air temperature passes 30,
and treats a surface evaporation rate above **1 kg per square metre per hour** as the
trigger for plastic shrinkage cracking. A hot, dry, windy Perth afternoon clears that
easily. Pour early, use sunshades or windbreaks, keep an evaporative retarder on site,
and start curing immediately after finishing — continuous fine mist, plastic sheeting
held down and checked, or a liquid membrane compound. Cure **at least three days, seven
if you want the strength and durability you paid for**. Hosing at breakfast and again
at dusk while it bakes in between is not curing.

## The legal bits

A **crossover** — the driveway between your boundary and the kerb — needs an application
to your local government before you build it, under the Local Government Act 1995 and
the Uniform Local Provisions Regulations. A slab inside your own boundary generally does
not need a building permit, and a registered building contractor is required only where
a permit *is* required and the value is $50,000 or more for a Class 10a building or
$20,000 or more for other work — see
[structures and building permits](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/).

Concrete slurry and wash water are strongly alkaline and must not enter a gutter,
stormwater pit or soakwell. Wash out into a bunded pit on your own land and dispose of
the hardened material as building waste.
