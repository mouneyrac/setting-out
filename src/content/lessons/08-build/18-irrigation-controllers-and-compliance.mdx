Every other part of this system is a physics problem. The controller is a legal
one. You can build a technically perfect retic system and still breach the law
twice a week because a box in the garage is set to Tuesday.

Water Corporation inspectors work seven days a week and issue
[$100 on-the-spot fines](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/check-your-watering-days),
and their published position is explicit: a malfunctioning controller is not a
defence. A flat backup battery or a power cut that reset the clock — the
infringement still lands on you. On a renovation the risk is higher, because the
program in the existing controller was written by someone else for a garden that
no longer exists.

## What a professional does at this stage

A retic contractor programs the controller last, working backwards from two fixed
numbers: **the roster days for that address**, and **9am**.

They do not start from what the plants want. That was settled in
[hydrozoning](/curriculum/build/irrigation-hydrozoning-and-stations/) and
[run times](/curriculum/build/irrigation-scheduling-and-uniformity/). At the
controller the only question is whether the whole program fits inside a legal
window on two days a week. If it does not, that is a design fault to fix now, not
something to fudge with an extra start time.

They also write it down — a photograph of the controller face, the station
schedule, and a roster card taped inside the door. That sheet goes into the
[maintenance manual](/curriculum/handover/the-maintenance-manual/) and is what
lets anyone rebuild the program after a blackout.

## The rules you are programming to

| Question | Perth and Mandurah |
| --- | --- |
| How many days | Two per week, fixed by the last digit of your street number |
| Which two | Look yours up — never assume from a neighbour |
| What time | Once on a rostered day, either **before 9am** or **after 6pm** |
| Season | 1 September to 31 May |
| Winter | No sprinklers 1 June to 31 August, scheme and bore alike |
| Bore water | Identical roster, identical hours |
| Hand watering | Any day, watering can or handheld hose with a nozzle |
| Testing | Two minutes per station, on rostered days, in the windows |
| Penalty | $100 on the spot; up to $500 for a new-garden breach |

Two traps. The roster follows the **street number**, not the unit or villa number
— on a multi-unit site use the higher street number. And rosters are not fixed
forever: from
[1 April 2026 the City of Karratha moved to a three day per week trial](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/waterwise-offers/west-pilbara-free-irrigation-controller-reset).
Perth and Peel remain on two days, but re-check yours each spring rather than
trusting a program written years ago.

## Making everything finish before 9am

A standard controller runs stations **sequentially**. Your program length is the
sum of every station's run time, not the longest one. Using Water Corporation's
[published run times](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/sprinklers-and-irrigation/how-long-should-i-run-my-sprinklers-for)
for a 10mm application — 13 to 16 minutes for spray, 40 to 60 for rotary, 30 to 40
for gear drive rotary and dripline — a modest six-station block adds up fast:

| Station | Type | Run time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Lawn, gear drive rotary | 35 min each = 70 |
| 3–5 | Beds, dripline | 35 min each = 105 |
| 6 | Verge, spray | 15 |
| **Total** | | **190 min (3 h 10 min)** |

Latest start = 9:00am minus 190 minutes minus a 15 minute buffer for valve delays
and a slow-closing solenoid. That is a **5:35am start**, and only if nothing is
added later. Set it at 5:00am and stop thinking about it.

Three failure modes:

- **Stacking.** Put lawn on program A and beds on program B, and if A is still
  running when B is due, most controllers queue B behind A instead of running both
  together. Your finish time silently moves past 9am. Give B a start after A's
  calculated finish, or use one program.
- **AM/PM.** The most common programming error on Perth blocks is 6:00 PM entered
  as 6:00 AM. WA has no daylight saving, so you never shift start times twice a
  year — but check the clock and the date, not just the schedule.
- **Cycle and soak.** Splitting a run into short cycles is right on sand and on
  slopes, but keep every cycle inside the same window. Sprinklers may be used once
  on a rostered day, and a program still soaking at 9:20am is a breach.

Prefer the morning window: early morning is the calmest part of a Perth day, so
distribution uniformity is at its best, and foliage dries as the sun comes up
rather than staying wet all night.

## The winter ban, and the exception a renovator needs

From **1 June to 31 August** no sprinklers run, scheme or bore. Do not delete the
program. Turn the controller to **OFF** — or set seasonal adjust to 0%, or a
90-day rain delay — so the schedule survives, then put a reminder in your phone
for 1 September. A controller left off through spring kills more Perth gardens
than one left on.

Renovations finish into new planting, and that is where the money is. Watering new
lawn or garden during the switch-off **without an approved exemption risks a fine
of up to $500**, and the breach is the property owner's responsibility, not the
turf supplier's. Apply for the
[watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
**on the day the lawn or garden goes in**, and program to its schedule: up to 42
days at 10mm a day between 1 October and 31 March, up to 35 days at 7.5mm a day
between 1 April and 30 September, tapering from several applications a day down to
every second day. Put the new area on its own program so the rest of the garden
stays on the ordinary roster.

Commissioning in winter is legal but constrained: you may test only on rostered
days, in the windows, for **no more than two minutes per station**, and during the
switch-off only a Waterwise Garden Irrigator may test for you, because endorsed
professionals are exempt from the schedules. Book one, and again when you re-test
in [install and audit](/curriculum/build/irrigation-install-and-audit/).

## Seasonal adjustment: the setting nobody uses

Almost every controller has a **seasonal adjust** percentage that scales every
station at once — set 80% and a 10 minute station runs 8. Water Corporation
publishes the schedule for Perth and the South West:

| Month | Seasonal adjust |
| --- | --- |
| January–February | 100% |
| March | 80% |
| April | 60% |
| May | Manual only |
| June–August | Sprinkler Switch-off |
| September | Manual only |
| October | 60% |
| November | 80% |
| December | 90% |

"Manual only" in May and September means the automatic program stays off and you
water by hand if something genuinely needs it. Those two months are where most of
the waste sits. Five diary reminders — 1 March, 1 April, 1 June, 1 September,
1 October — turn the table into a maintained system. Carry them into
[the Perth year](/curriculum/maintenance/the-perth-year/).

## Do smart controllers earn their money in Perth?

Sometimes. Understand what the roster does to the maths first.

Weather-based controllers save water overseas mainly by watering more often for
shorter periods. Here you are capped at two days, so a smart controller can only
**shorten or skip** a watering, never add one — which removes a large share of the
savings behind the
[US EPA's WaterSense figures](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/watersense-labeled-controllers).
What remains is narrower but real: it applies the seasonal curve daily instead of
relying on your memory, it skips after genuine rain while you are away, and you
drive it from your phone rather than from a garage wall you visit twice a year.

Buy one only if three things are true. **The Wi-Fi reaches the controller** —
check the signal at that exact spot before you buy. **It supports specific days of
the week**, not just intervals or odd/even watering; an interval schedule drifts
straight off a WA roster and odd/even is not our system at all. And **it lets you
set a hard exclusion window** so it can never pick a start time between 9am and
6pm on your behalf.

If any of those fail, a standard controller with a seasonal adjust dial and five
calendar reminders does the same job for less. There is no Australian smart
controller certification, so ignore the word on the box and check the three
features.

## Rain and soil moisture sensors

A rain sensor wires into two sensor terminals on almost any controller and
interrupts the common wire when it is wet. Cheap, and the only device that stops
your system while you are away.

The Perth reality: most of our rain falls in winter, when sprinklers are banned
anyway. A sensor earns its keep in April, May, September and October, and after
the occasional summer storm. Set the trip threshold **high**, not low. On deep
sand a 3mm shower wets the top 30mm and nothing more, and if the sensor cancels
your Wednesday your next legal watering is Saturday — under a two-day roster,
skipping a real watering costs you more than one small unnecessary one.

Mount it in open sky at gutter height, never under an eave, and check it annually:
the discs perish in WA UV and the vents block with dust, and a stuck-wet sensor
quietly stops the entire system.

Soil moisture sensors are more accurate and more work — buried in the root zone of
your driest station, cabled back, calibrated. Rarely worth it on a suburban block,
occasionally worth it on lawn over a bore where the water feels free.

Neither device makes you compliant. Neither knows what day it is.

## Power, batteries and where the licensed trade starts

Controllers run off a plug pack or a hard-wired transformer. Installing a new
fixed outdoor power point, or hard-wiring a transformer, is
[electrical work, and in WA it is illegal for an unlicensed person to do it](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/electrical-safety-home/having-electrical-work-done).
Use a licensed electrical contractor and keep the electrical safety certificate
they must give you within 28 days. Mains voltage beside an irrigation system is
the one place here where a shortcut kills someone;
[garden lighting](/curriculum/build/garden-lighting/) draws the same line.

Replace the backup battery every year. A power cut with a dead battery loses the
clock, and the unit restarts on a default program that knows nothing about your
roster. That is the $100 fine most people actually get.

## What happens to this next

You now have a written program: stations, run times, start times, roster days,
seasonal adjust schedule and a winter plan. The next lesson installs and
commissions the system against it — flushing, arcs, pressure at the furthest head,
and the catch-cup audit that says whether those run times deliver what you
designed. Expect it to change some of these numbers. That is what it is for.
