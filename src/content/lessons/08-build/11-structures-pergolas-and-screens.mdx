A garden structure is the only thing you will build that the wind actively tries
to remove. Everything else in this phase gets heavier and more stable as it is
finished. A pergola gets a roof, and the roof turns it into a wing.

That drives almost every decision below: post size, footing depth, beam span,
bracing, bolts, even where the structure is allowed to sit relative to your
boundary. Size a structure by asking "what will hold this up" and you will build
something that holds itself up perfectly well until the first serious front.

Say the legal part first. **Plumbing and gas work in WA must be done by licensed
tradespeople, and fixed electrical work requires a licensed electrical
contractor.** There is no homeowner exemption for running a new water line to a
garden sink, connecting a waste to the sewer, or wiring a power point on a post.
And the building permit has to be in your hand *before* you dig — that is settled
in [patios, pergolas, decks and sheds](/curriculum/approvals/structures-and-building-permits/),
and this lesson assumes you have done it.

## What a professional does at this stage

A landscaper builds a structure in a fixed order, because each step becomes
expensive or impossible once the next one is done.

1. **Confirm the permit and the stamped engineering are on site.** Not applied
   for. On site, with the footing detail visible.
2. **Locate services before any hole is dug.** See
   [locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/) and lodge a
   free [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/) enquiry.
3. **Set out and dig all footings in one go**, while the site is open and the
   machinery is still here.
4. **Stand and brace the frame**, temporarily but properly.
5. **Rough in every service** — conduit, water, gas sleeve, drainage — before the
   slab is poured, the paving is laid or a board of cladding goes on.
6. **Then** roof, then surface, then screens and fit-out.

Steps 2 and 5 are the ones amateurs skip, and they are the two that cost the most
to undo.

## Wind, not weight

Building and Energy audits plans in WA and keeps finding the wrong wind
classification on them. Their bulletin is blunt about why that matters: **net
design uplift can almost double between one wind classification and the next**,
and a structure built to a lower classification may be damaged in a design wind
event.

The classification is not a property of your suburb. It comes from four inputs,
and two of them catch Perth blocks constantly:

| Input | How it is set | The Perth trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wind region | Map in AS 4055 | Perth is not cyclonic, which is why people under-rate the rest |
| Terrain category | The **lowest-numbered** category within a 500 m radius | Within 500 m of the Swan, the ocean, a lake or canal wider than 200 m puts you in TC1; a golf course or open farmland puts you in TC2 |
| Topographic class | Position on a hill or escarpment | Anything on the Darling Scarp edge, or a built-up pad above a retaining wall |
| Shielding | Rows of housing on all four sides, judged five years forward | A new estate, a park, a school oval or a river frontage on one side means less shielding than you assumed |

AS 4055 only applies if the completed building stays within its geometric limits
— wall height under the eaves 6 m or less, roof height 8.5 m or less, width
across the ridge 16 m or less, pitch 35 degrees or less. Outside those, an
engineer must work from AS/NZS 1170.2, and the AS 1684 timber tables stop
applying too.

Practical version: get the wind classification in writing from whoever engineers
your structure, and tell them if you are near water, on a hill, or on the open
edge of a development. Do not accept "N2, same as everyone".

## Footings resist pull-out, not just load

Timber Queensland's pergola data sheet puts it in six words: footings are to be
**engineer designed for both bearing and uplift**. In Perth sand that is not a
formality — the roof pulls the post up while the wind pushes it sideways, and
loose sand resists neither until you go deep.

So the footing dimension on your drawing is not a suggestion. Reproduce it
exactly: depth, diameter or square size, concrete strength, and the embedment of
the stirrup or bolts. The most common site failure is digging shallower than the
drawing because the hole hit limestone at 400 mm or the sand kept collapsing. If
that happens, ring the engineer. Do not decide it yourself.

Two details worth knowing whatever the engineer specifies:

- On **galvanised stirrups**, leave at least 75 mm between the top of the
  concrete or paving and the bottom of the post. That gap is a termite inspection
  zone, and it stops the post end sitting in water.
- **Embedded posts** need 100 mm of coarse gravel at the base to drain, backfill
  sloped away, and H5-treated timber or in-ground durability class 1 hardwood.
  H3 is above ground only. Get it wrong and the post rots off at the ground line.

All fixings hot dipped galvanised or stainless. Not zinc plated.

## Spans: read the table, then place the posts

People decide the posts should be 3.6 m apart because it looks right, then buy
timber. The table decides. From the low wind speed tables (N1, N2, N3 and C1) for
a lightweight roof, seasoned treated pine F7, single span:

| Member | Roof load width 1500 mm | 3000 mm | 4500 mm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 90 x 45 beam | not suitable | not suitable | not suitable |
| 140 x 45 beam | 2800 mm | 1800 mm | not suitable |
| 190 x 45 beam | 3800 mm | 2700 mm | 2100 mm |
| 240 x 45 beam | 4900 mm | 3300 mm | 2700 mm |

Roof load width is roughly half the rafter span either side of the beam, so a
3.0 m deep patio with a beam down the middle sits near 3000 mm. Read across: a
190 x 45 beam gets you 2.7 m between posts, not 3.6 m. Overhangs are capped at
25% of the actual backspan.

Posts have their own table, and it is a shock the first time:

| Post (seasoned treated pine F7) | Roof area 5 m² | 10 m² | 20 m² |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 70 x 70 | 3500 mm high | 2500 mm | not suitable |
| 90 x 90 | 4800 mm | 4100 mm | 2900 mm |

A 70 x 70 post is not a patio post. And **bracing is not optional**: an attached
pergola needs double diagonal bracing under the rafters, or between a pair of
posts, or opposing knee braces bolted with 12 mm galvanised bolts. A freestanding
structure needs double diagonal bracing between posts in **both** directions. An
unbraced frame is a parallelogram waiting for a reason.

Steel patio kits come with the manufacturer's own engineering, which is fine —
just check it matches your wind classification, and that the coating system is
stated. Building and Energy has a standing bulletin about powder-coated patios
delaminating and rusting in WA, mostly near the coast or a river. Ask for the
galvanising thickness and whether the steel was primed before powder coating.

## Where the structure may sit — this changed on 1 May 2026

NCC 2025 took effect in WA on 1 May 2026 with new deemed-to-satisfy rules for
fire separation of roofed outdoor areas. Three options:

| Option | Separation | Conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fire-resisting external wall | — | Needed wherever distance is not achieved |
| Separation by distance | 900 mm to the boundary, 1.8 m to another building on the lot | Measured from the outermost **structural** projection — fascia face, or rafter ends if no fascia |
| Reduced distance | 500 mm to the boundary, 1 m to another building | Measured from the outermost projection including the gutter; at least two sides open; non-combustible construction; no vertical support to the dwelling; and a solid non-combustible fence or screen **at least 1500 mm high** along the affected side |

That last row is a screen requirement hiding inside a fire rule, and it will
change what your boundary screen is made of. It also sits on top of the R-Codes
setbacks, not instead of them — the two are measured differently and you must
satisfy both.

## Screens, slats and gates

A screen is a sail. A 1.8 m by 4 m solid screen on two posts collects real load
and the footings have to answer for it. Slat gaps are the cheapest way to shed
it: roughly a quarter to a third open still reads as private from a neighbour's
normal viewing angle, because they are looking obliquely, not square-on. Solid is
rarely necessary and always the most expensive structurally.

Three rules that save arguments:

- A screen **on** the dividing fence is not a fence. It is a structure, either
  agreed with your neighbour or set back on your own land — see
  [dividing fences](/curriculum/approvals/dividing-fences/).
- West-facing timber screens in Perth cup and check. Use narrower sections, seal
  all four faces including cut ends before fixing, and expect to re-oil.
- A gate's diagonal brace runs **from the bottom hinge corner up to the top latch
  corner** if it is timber in compression, and the opposite way if it is a steel
  rod in tension. Backwards is why gates droop. A gate in a pool barrier is a
  different problem — see
  [pools and pool fencing](/curriculum/approvals/pools-and-pool-fencing/).

## Outdoor kitchens: rough in before you clad

All of this goes in before the slab, paving or cabinetry. Retro-fitting one 50 mm
conduit under finished paving is a demolition job.

| Service | Rough-in | Who may connect it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power | Conduit from the switchboard, sized for the fridge as well as the outlets | Licensed electrical contractor; electrical safety certificate within 28 days |
| Water | Cold, and hot if you want it, in conduit not buried bare | Licensed plumber |
| Waste | Sink waste to sewer — a garden soakaway is not legal disposal | Licensed plumber |
| Gas | Sleeve for a natural gas run, or a ventilated compartment for LPG | Licensed gasfitter |
| Spare | One empty conduit for whatever you think of in three years | You, now, for about $30 |

Then the two gas rules that decide your design:

1. **1,200 mm minimum vertical clearance** between the cooking surface of a
   domestic barbecue and the ceiling, roof or overhead cover. Measure your
   pergola against that before you build it, and check the manufacturer's figure
   too — it is often larger.
2. **Blinds count as walls.** For gas compliance, any opening fitted with a blind
   or shade material, even a retractable one, is considered closed. Adding cafe
   blinds to a finished alfresco can turn a compliant outdoor barbecue into a
   carbon monoxide risk in an enclosed space. If blinds are even possible later,
   design the ventilation for the enclosed case now.

Then the benchtop. **Engineered stone has been banned in WA since 1 July 2024** —
manufacture, supply, processing and installation. Porcelain, sintered stone,
concrete, granite and stainless are all still available. Cutting any of them, or
cutting limestone or pavers for the surround, releases respirable crystalline
silica: cut wet, outdoors, upwind, with respiratory protection, as covered in
[site establishment and safety](/curriculum/build/site-establishment-and-safety/).
And if you are lifting long members near the front boundary, the service cable
overhead has a danger zone measured in metres — add the length of what you are
carrying to it, and ring Western Power before the lift, not after.
