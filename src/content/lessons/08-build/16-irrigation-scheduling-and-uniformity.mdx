Everything in this mini-course so far has been hardware. This lesson is where the
hardware becomes a number of minutes on a controller, and it is the step most
Perth retic installs skip entirely. A system with correct pipe sizing, matched
nozzles and sensible stations will still kill a garden if the run times were
guessed off the back of the controller box.

The unit that matters is the millimetre, not the minute. One millimetre of water
is one litre spread evenly over one square metre — the same unit the weather
bureau uses for rain. Water Corporation's target for a Perth garden is
[10 mm on each rostered watering day](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/waterwise/garden/how-much-water-does-my-garden-need),
twice a week in summer. Your controller has no millimetre button. Converting
between the two is the entire job of this lesson.

This is also the safest lesson in the mini-course. The hazards are legal and
financial rather than physical — testing outside your watering window is a $100
fine, and the trenching, pressure and wiring risks sit in
[lesson 6](/curriculum/build/irrigation-mainline-valves-and-wiring/). The one
physical caution is heat: your summer windows are before 9am and after 6pm, so
take the dawn slot, not the end of a 40-degree day.

## What a professional does at this stage

A retic installer doing the job properly does not read run times off a chart.
They measure the precipitation rate of each station they built, on the site they
built it, and set run times from that measurement.

The published numbers are bands, not values. Water Corporation lists spray heads
at 35 to 45 mm per hour, but a station whose heads ended up 3.5 metres apart
instead of the designed 4.5 applies water far faster than a correctly spaced one
using identical nozzles. Spacing, real operating pressure, arc settings and how
much of the zone is hard surface all move the rate. The only way to know it is
to catch it in a cup.

The second thing they measure is **uniformity** — whether the water lands evenly.
If the driest part of a zone gets half what the average gets, you must either
accept a dead corner or drown everything else to keep it alive.

Then they write both numbers down, station by station, so that in three years
someone can adjust the schedule without starting again. That record belongs in
the [as-built](/curriculum/build/irrigation-install-and-audit/).

## Precipitation rate, three ways

In increasing order of trust.

**1. The published band.** Fastest, least accurate. A sanity check, not an answer.

| Sprinkler type | Application rate | Run time for 10 mm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pop-up and fixed spray | 35–45 mm/h | 13–16 min |
| Micro spray | 35–45 mm/h | 13–16 min |
| Gear drive rotary | 10–20 mm/h | 30–40 min |
| Rotary | 10–15 mm/h | 40–60 min |
| Dripline | 15–20 mm/h | 30–40 min |

**2. Flow divided by area.** Because 1 mm equals 1 litre per square metre, the
metric version is trivial:

> **PR (mm/h) = station flow (L/h) ÷ irrigated area (m²)**

Eight spray heads totalling 30 L/min over a 45 m² lawn is 1,800 L/h over 45 m²:
40 mm/h, so 15 minutes for 10 mm. Do this for every station while the design is
still on paper. It takes a minute and it catches gross errors before you dig.

**3. Catch cups.** The only method that includes wind, pressure loss, worn
nozzles and the arc someone set badly. This is the one that counts.

## Running the catch-cup test

**Get the legal part right first.** A catch-cup test is a watering event, not a
test in the regulatory sense — the two-minute allowance is for fault-finding.
Run it as your once-per-day watering on a rostered day, before 9am or after 6pm.
Between 1 June and 31 August you cannot run it at all: that is the Sprinkler
Switch-off, it covers bore water, and the fine is $100. In that window a
Waterwise Garden Irrigator can legally test for you.

Then the method:

1. **Pick a still morning.** The Irrigation Association caps audit wind at 5 mph,
   about 8 km/h. In Perth that rules out most summer evenings, because the sea
   breeze is still up at 6pm. Dawn is the reliable slot.
2. **Use identical straight-sided containers.** Catch cups from a Waterwise
   Irrigation Design Shop are calibrated and cheap; twelve matching tuna tins
   work and cost nothing.
3. **Use enough of them.** The professional minimum is 24. On a suburban lawn
   station, 12 to 16 in an even grid is a workable compromise; below about eight
   the figure is not worth calculating. Keep them a metre from the nearest head
   and 300 to 600 mm in from the zone edges.
4. **Run 15 minutes**, then measure each cup in millimetres with a steel rule,
   recording readings in position order so you can map the dry patch.

Twelve cups, 15 minutes, readings in millimetres:
9, 7, 8, 10, 6, 8, 9, 5, 7, 8, 9, 6. Total 92, average **7.67 mm**.

> **PR = average catch (mm) × 60 ÷ run time (min)** = 7.67 × 60 ÷ 15 =
> **30.7 mm/h**

Not 40. This station is a third slower than the flow-and-area calculation
predicted, which is exactly why you measure.

## Distribution uniformity

Sort the readings, take the driest quarter — with 12 cups that is the lowest
three: 5, 6, 6, averaging 5.67 mm.

> **DU = average of the driest quarter ÷ average of all cups** =
> 5.67 ÷ 7.67 = **0.74**

Then apply a run time multiplier so the dry quarter still gets the target depth:

> **RTM = 1 ÷ (0.4 + 0.6 × DU)**

| DU | Multiplier | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.90 | 1.06 | Excellent. Drip and good rotor zones reach this. |
| 0.80 | 1.14 | Good for spray. A realistic suburban lawn target. |
| 0.70 | 1.22 | Acceptable. Worth chasing the worst head. |
| 0.60 | 1.32 | Poor. A third extra water, bought for one dry corner. |
| 0.50 | 1.43 | Bad. Find the cause before touching the controller. |
| Below 0.40 | — | Fix the sprinklers. Do not schedule around this. |

Low uniformity is almost always a physical fault, not a scheduling problem:
mixed head types on one station, heads not spaced head-to-head, pressure too low
at the end of the run, a worn or blocked nozzle, an arc throwing at a fence. Fix
those first. Every point of DU you recover is water you stop buying forever.

## Turning a depth into minutes

> **Run time (min) = target depth (mm) ÷ PR (mm/h) × 60 × RTM**

For the station above, targeting 10 mm: 10 ÷ 30.7 × 60 = 19.6 minutes, × 1.18 =
**23 minutes**.

That is well outside the published 13 to 16 minutes for spray, and it is
correct — this station is slow and slightly uneven. That number goes on the
controller and into the handover file.

Do this for every station, then check the total: all stations plus any soak gaps
must finish inside the window. If they do not, you have too many stations on one
start time, which sends you back to
[hydrozoning and stations](/curriculum/build/irrigation-hydrozoning-and-stations/).

Set the summer number once and let seasonal adjustment cover the rest of the
year: 100% in January and February, 80% in March, 60% in April, manual only in
May and September, off from June to August, then 60% in October, 80% in November
and 90% in December.

## Cycle-and-soak, and when Perth actually needs it

Cycle-and-soak means splitting a run time into shorter bursts with a soak
between, so water infiltrates instead of running off. On clay it is essential.
On deep Bassendean or Spearwood sand, infiltration sits so far above any
sprinkler's application rate that runoff is not normally the constraint — the
Perth failure is the opposite, a single long run pushing water past the root
zone and into the groundwater.

So on flat, deep sand, one uninterrupted run is usually right. Cycle-and-soak
earns its place in five specific situations:

- **Slopes and verges**, where water moves sideways before it moves down.
- **Water-repellent sand**, which sheds and channels the first few minutes of
  every watering. See [Perth soils](/curriculum/site-analysis/perth-soils/).
- **Compacted ground** — old driveways, paths, anywhere plant has run.
- **Clay-amended or imported loam topsoil**, which is what
  [turning Perth sand into soil](/curriculum/build/turning-perth-sand-into-soil/)
  creates.
- **Thatchy lawn**, where thatch intercepts water before the soil sees it.

Split the run time into two to four cycles with 20 to 40 minutes of soak between
starts — the 23-minute station above becomes three starts of 8 minutes. If water
still moves downhill, cut each subsequent cycle by about 20 per cent. Watch the
clock: soak time is dead time inside a window that closes at 9am.

## New lawn and new planting

If you are laying turf or planting in this renovation, the roster will not keep
it alive, and watering more often without an exemption is a breach. A
[watering exemption](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/help-and-advice/exemptions-and-breaches/watering-exemption/apply-for-a-watering-exemption)
gives up to 42 days from 1 October to 31 March, or 35 days from 1 April to
30 September, on a published schedule running from 3.5 mm three times a day down
to 10 mm every second day. Apply on the installation day — the clock runs from
then regardless of when you lodge it. Those are depths, and you now own the only
thing that converts them into minutes.

## What happens next

Per station you now have a measured precipitation rate, a distribution
uniformity, a run time multiplier and a summer run time. That is the schedule,
and it is also most of your
