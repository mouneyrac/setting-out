Everything else in this mini-course sits downstream of two numbers: how many litres per
minute arrive at your tap, and at what pressure. Get those wrong and no amount of good
equipment rescues the job. A rotor that turns slowly at the end of a long run, heads that
mist instead of throw, a dry crescent on one side of the lawn — those are hydraulic faults,
and you cannot tune a hydraulic fault out at the controller.

This is the only lesson in the series with real arithmetic in it. It is worth the time,
because the sums take about twenty minutes and they decide the pipe you buy — and pipe is
the one part of the system you cannot change later without digging up everything you built
on top of it.

If you are renovating, someone already sized this system by eye. Your
[garden audit](/curriculum/site-analysis/auditing-an-existing-garden/) almost certainly
found the evidence.

## What a professional does at this stage

A designer writes a **pressure budget** before drawing a single sprinkler.

It is a subtraction sum. Start with the working pressure measured at the tap while water is
actually flowing, subtract every loss between there and the furthest sprinkler on the worst
station, and check what is left against what that sprinkler needs. If the answer is positive
with margin, the design works. If it is negative, something has to change — fewer heads per
station, bigger pipe, a shorter run, or a different emitter.

Amateurs do this the other way around: they lay out the sprinklers they want, connect them,
and find out. On Perth sand that failure is quiet. Everything runs, nothing looks broken, and
the garden simply declines for two years on the dry side of each zone.

The second thing a professional does is **design for the worst case, not the good case**.
Rain Bird's manual is blunt about this: design for the pressure you will have on a hot
weekend when the whole street is irrigating, not the pressure you measured on a quiet
Tuesday afternoon. In Perth the worst case is doubly relevant, because
[the legal watering window](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/waterwise/watering-days/check-your-watering-days) —
once before 9am or after 6pm on your rostered days — falls squarely inside the network's
peak demand hours.

## Flow and pressure are not the same thing

They get used interchangeably in conversation and they are unrelated properties.

- **Flow** is quantity per unit time: litres per minute. It is what fills a bucket. It sets
  **how many sprinklers you can run at once**.
- **Pressure** is force per unit area: kilopascals. It is what makes a sprinkler throw its
  rated distance and atomise properly. It sets **how well each sprinkler works**.

You can have plenty of one and not enough of the other. A tap at 500 kPa through a
half-blocked 1965 galvanised service delivers beautiful pressure and useless flow. The two
are linked only through the pipe: **moving flow through pipe costs pressure**, and that cost
is what the rest of this lesson calculates.

Useful conversions, because Perth gauges and manufacturer charts disagree on units:
100 kPa = 1 bar = 10 metres of head ≈ 14.5 psi. And **one metre of height costs about
10 kPa**, up or down, regardless of how much water is moving.

## Static pressure, working pressure, and the gap between them

**Static pressure** is the reading with nothing flowing. It is easy to measure and it flatters
you.

**Working pressure** — Rain Bird calls it dynamic pressure — is the reading at a given point
in the system *while a given flow is passing that point*. It is lower, sometimes much lower,
and it is the only figure a design can be built on. The difference is consumed by the service
pipe, the meter, the backflow device, your own pipework and every fitting in it.

A typical Perth measurement: 480 kPa static at the tap, 380 kPa with 25 litres per minute
running. That 100 kPa has already gone before your mainline starts. The
[next lesson](/curriculum/build/irrigation-water-source/) is entirely about measuring these
two properly.

## Friction loss: the calculation

Friction loss depends on four things: the **flow**, the **internal diameter** of the pipe,
the **length**, and the pipe's internal **roughness**. For the smooth plastic pipe used in
domestic retic the roughness is effectively fixed, so three variables matter.

Two relationships are worth memorising, because they explain most bad retic:

- Friction loss rises with flow to roughly the **1.85 power**. Double the flow through a pipe
  and you multiply the loss by about 3.6.
- Friction loss falls with internal diameter to the **4.87 power**. Halve the internal
  diameter and you multiply the loss by about 29.

Diameter is the lever. Everything else is noise.

The table below gives the loss in kilopascals for **each 10 metres of pipe**, at the flows a
suburban station actually runs, using the verified internal diameters of the pipe sold here.
Shaded meaning: any figure marked † is above the 1.5 metre-per-second velocity limit and
should not be built.

| Flow | 19 mm rural poly (ID 19.0) | 25 mm rural poly (ID 25.3) | 32 mm rural poly (ID 31.6) | PVC DN25 PN12 (ID 29.8) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 15 L/min | 5.0 kPa | 1.2 kPa | 0.4 kPa | 0.6 kPa |
| 20 L/min | 8.6 kPa | 2.1 kPa | 0.7 kPa | 1.0 kPa |
| 25 L/min | 12.9 kPa | 3.2 kPa | 1.1 kPa | 1.4 kPa |
| 30 L/min | 18.1 kPa † | 4.5 kPa | 1.5 kPa | 2.0 kPa |
| 40 L/min | 30.9 kPa † | 7.7 kPa | 2.6 kPa | 3.5 kPa |

and the [Iplex PVC dimension guide](https://www.iplex.com.au/resources-and-tools/resources/pvc-pipe-size-dimension-guide/).
These figures reproduce Rain Bird's published charts and Vinidex's own WA pipe flow
calculator to within a few per cent. If your pipe is a different class or brand, look up its
internal diameter and redo it — do not assume.

### A worked pressure budget

One station: six spray heads, design flow 25 L/min, 32 metres of 25 mm rural poly from the
tap to the valve box, then 18 metres of 19 mm poly out to the last head, rising 1.2 metres to
a raised bed at the back.

| Line | Pressure |
| --- | --- |
| Working pressure at the tap at 25 L/min | **380 kPa** |
| 32 m of 25 mm poly mainline, at 3.2 kPa per 10 m | −10 kPa |
| Solenoid valve, read off the valve's own chart | −30 kPa |
| 18 m of 19 mm poly lateral, at 12.9 kPa per 10 m | −23 kPa |
| Fittings, estimated at 10% of all pipe losses | −3 kPa |
| Elevation rise of 1.2 m | −12 kPa |
| **Pressure at the last sprinkler** | **302 kPa** |

That is comfortable for spray heads and rotors alike, which is the answer you want. The 10%
fittings allowance is Rain Bird's own shortcut and it is close enough at garden scale.

Two refinements a professional would add. First, the lateral does not carry the full 25 L/min
along its whole length — flow drops at every head — so sizing each reach for the flow it
actually carries, working backwards from the last sprinkler, gives a smaller number. Treating
the whole lateral as full-flow is conservative, and conservative is free. Second, elevation
is a *net* figure: a 1.2 metre rise costs 12 kPa whether the pipe climbs it in one go or
undulates the whole way.

## The velocity limit is the real design rule

Rain Bird states the industry position directly: **1.5 metres per second is the accepted
maximum velocity**, because faster water causes damaging surge pressures and because friction
loss climbs steeply beyond it.

Surge — water hammer — is the bang when a solenoid closes and a moving column of water stops
in about fifty milliseconds. It is what splits solvent joints and cracks valve bodies a year
or two after handover, long after anyone connects the failure to the pipe size.

Converted into something you can use at the retic shop, here is the **maximum flow each pipe
size may carry**:

| Pipe | Internal diameter | Max flow at 1.5 m/s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 19 mm rural poly | 19.0 mm | 26 L/min |
| 25 mm metric poly, DN25 PN12.5 | 21.0 mm | 31 L/min |
| 25 mm rural poly | 25.3 mm | 45 L/min |
| 32 mm metric poly, DN32 PN12.5 | 27.0 mm | 52 L/min |
| PVC DN25 PN12 | 29.8 mm | 63 L/min |
| 32 mm rural poly | 31.6 mm | 71 L/min |
| PVC DN32 PN12 | 37.5 mm | 99 L/min |

Note what that table exposes. **Nominal size is not internal diameter, and two pipes both
called "25 mm" differ by 4 mm inside and 14 L/min in capacity.** Rural poly is sized by its
approximate bore; metric PE pipe to AS/NZS 4130 is sized by its *outside* diameter, so DN25
metric poly is a smaller pipe than 25 mm rural poly. Raising the pressure class shrinks it
further, because the wall gets thicker and the outside stays the same. Always look up the
number.

## Oversized pipe is the cheapest insurance on the job

Run the money. A 50 metre coil of Vinidex Rural Plus at Bunnings today is
[$81.90 in ¾ inch (19 mm)](https://www.bunnings.com.au/vinidex-rural-plus-x-50m-poly-pipe_p0135009)
and [$89.90 in 1 inch (25 mm)](https://www.bunnings.com.au/vinidex-rural-plus-1-x-50m-poly-pipe_p0135010).
Eight dollars.

For that eight dollars, on the 32 metre mainline in the worked example above, you go from
41 kPa of loss to 10 kPa, and from 1.47 m/s — right on the limit — to 0.83 m/s. Thirty-one
kilopascals is the difference between a rotor performing to its chart and a rotor that
throws two metres short into your neighbour's fence.

Now price the alternative. Undersized pipe is discovered after the trenches are backfilled,
the paving is down and the turf is laid. The fix is excavation, not adjustment.

The rule the trade works to: **size for your largest station flow, then go one size up if the
run is long, if the block rises, or if you might ever add a station.** Buy pressure-rated
pipe while you are at it — black poly sold for drainage is a different product and is not
rated for it.

## Where this becomes a safety and legal question

Two boundaries on this work in WA, and neither is negotiable.

**Plumbing.** [Building and Energy](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/plumbing-work-your-home)
states that all water supply, sanitary and drainage plumbing work must be carried out by a
licensed plumber or permit-holder. The exemptions for a homeowner are tiny — shower heads,
tap repairs, a cistern washer, a filter cartridge, plunging a blocked waste. Connecting your
retic to the scheme supply, and installing the
[backflow prevention containment device Water Corporation requires at the boundary of every water service connection](https://www.watercorporation.com.au/developing-and-building/building/backflow-prevention),
is a plumber's job. Retic sits in fertilised soil; the device is what keeps that out of the
main.

**Electrical.** Anything touching bore pump wiring or mains power is a licensed electrical
contractor's work. Water and 240 volts in wet sand is the one mistake on this job that kills
people.

And a physical hazard worth naming: a poly coil under pressure with a fitting that lets go is
a whip. Pressure-test with the trench open and stand clear of the ends, not over them.
