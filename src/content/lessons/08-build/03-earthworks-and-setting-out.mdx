Setting out is where a drawing becomes physical marks you can dig to, and
everything after inherits the accuracy of what you do today. Set the patio 40 mm
too high and you find out when the paving reaches the threshold and sits above
the damp proof course — which is not a paving fix, it is excavating a base you
already paid for.

It is also the most dangerous work in this course. Perth sand has almost no
cohesion: a trench face that looks stable at smoko can slab off without a sound,
and a cubic metre of damp sand weighs somewhere around 1.5 tonnes. People are
killed by trenches that were not particularly deep. On a renovation it is harder
again, because you are cutting into ground that already holds services, old
bases, root plates and forgotten fill.

## Before anything moves: three checks that are not optional

These are legal obligations, not good practice.

1. **Lodge a [Before You Dig Australia](https://www.byda.com.au/) enquiry.**
   Free, online, plans emailed to you, and it covers private property. Do it
   before you book the machine — what comes back may change where you dig.
2. **Check for a Water Corporation asset.** Most Perth back yards have a sewer
   gravity line through them, and ground-disturbing works within **2 metres** of
   one need approval under section 90 of the Water Services Act 2012. Water
   mains are 4 or 6 metres depending on diameter; structures are 10 metres.
3. **Respect Western Power's approach distances.** 30 cm to low voltage
   underground cable when using power tools or plant, 50 cm to high voltage,
   3 metres to transmission. Overhead is its own hazard — an excavator arm
   raised into a service line is a fatality, not an incident.

Never use a machine to find a service. Hand dig, approaching from the side, and
work through [locating services](/curriculum/site-analysis/locating-services/)
first if you have not already.

## What a professional does at this stage

**They set a datum before touching anything.** One fixed, undisturbable point
that will still exist when the site is a moonscape — a threshold, a step nosing,
a mark on the house wall. Every level is then plus or minus from that point,
never measured off the ground, because the ground is about to move.

**They set profiles outside the work zone.** Pegs inside the dig get knocked out
on the first pass, and then nobody can prove where anything was.

**They excavate to a calculated formation level, not to "about right".** Dig
depth is finished level minus surface thickness minus bedding minus compacted
sub-base, written down before the machine starts. Everything below ground gets
covered by something expensive, and a base under-dug by 50 mm surfaces later as
either a thin base or a high finished level.

## Setting out: datum, profiles, then lines

Work from your [levels and grading plan](/curriculum/documentation/levels-and-grading-plan/)
and the reduced levels recorded in
[levels, falls and datum](/curriculum/site-analysis/levels-falls-and-datum/).

**Profiles** are the tool: two pegs driven about a metre *outside* the corner of
the work with a horizontal board screwed across them, set level at a known
height above your datum and marked with a saw kerf where the string crosses. Two
profiles define a line. The string represents either the finished level or a
fixed offset above it — say 300 mm above, so you can dig underneath without
touching it. Write that offset on the board.

**Square the layout with a 3-4-5 triangle**, using 3, 4 and 5 metres or better
6, 8 and 10, then check the diagonals of the rectangle are equal. A 900 mm
builder's square amplifies its own error over 8 metres.

**Set falls now, not after excavating.** 1:100 away from buildings is the usual
minimum and Pavingexpert argues for 1:80 on block paving — over 7 metres, 1:80
is about 88 mm of fall, noticeably more than most people picture. Check with a
1200 mm level on a straight edge, not a short level.

## How deep to dig

Work down from the finished surface. Two typical Perth build-ups:

| Element | Patio (foot traffic) | Driveway (cars) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paver | 40 mm | 60 mm |
| Bedding sand (compacted) | 30 mm | 30 mm |
| Compacted crushed limestone base | 100 mm | 150 mm |
| **Excavate below finished level** | **170 mm** | **240 mm** |

Two corrections people forget. Base thickness is *compacted* thickness — loose
limestone drops roughly 20 to 25% under a plate, so 100 mm compacted needs about
125 to 130 mm loose. And excavated sand bulks, so the pile is bigger than the
hole when you order the bin. Confirm the clearance your wall construction needs
below the damp proof course and weep holes before fixing any finished level
against the house.

## Cut, fill, and where it becomes an approval

Cut and fill balances on paper and never on site. In Perth you are usually in
cut, because sand is easier to remove than to import and compact.

The line not to cross without checking: under clause 5.3.7 of the R-Codes, fill
or excavation **within 1 metre of a lot boundary, behind the street setback, must
not be more than 0.5 metres above natural ground level at the boundary**, and
between the street boundary and the street setback the limit is 0.5 metres above
or below. Above that, Table 4 wants a setback equal to the height and you are
into a development application — see
[R-Codes and your council](/curriculum/approvals/r-codes-and-your-council/).

The moment a level change needs holding up you are building a retaining wall,
which has its own permit trigger — commonly 500 mm of retained height, but
confirm the figure with your council. Read
[retaining walls approval](/curriculum/approvals/retaining-walls-approval/)
before you cut a batter you cannot leave.

## Compaction: layers, water, passes

**The rule is layers.** Fill deeper than about 150 mm goes in as a series of
layers, each compacted before the next is placed. Main Roads WA caps compacted
layers at 450 mm in sand and 300 mm in cohesive material, but that assumes a
roller — behind a hand-guided plate compactor, work in **150 to 200 mm lifts in
sand and 100 to 150 mm in clay**.

**Water is not optional in sand.** Dry Perth sand will not densify however long
you stand on the plate; the grains just vibrate past each other. Main Roads
allows Perth sands at 70–110% of optimum moisture content, a much wider band
than the 90–110% for other materials. In practice, hose each layer until it is
uniformly damp, not saturated. Sand that holds a squeezed shape is about right.

Three to four overlapping passes, each run overlapping the last by a third of
the plate width, then one pass at 90 degrees to the first.

### Sand versus clay

| | Perth sand | Clay and clay-rich fill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compacts by | Vibration | Impact and kneading |
| Right tool | Vibrating plate or roller | Rammer (jumping jack) |
| Moisture | Must be damp; will not compact dry | Narrow window; too wet and it shears and pumps |
| Compacted lift | 150–200 mm | 100–150 mm |
| Excavation face | Will not stand vertical | Stands up, then fails suddenly as a slab |
| Long-term risk | Settlement of uncompacted backfill | Shrink and swell with moisture |

**The most common failure on a renovation** is service trench backfill. Someone
runs conduit or retic pipe, pushes the spoil back in, stamps on it and paves
over the top. Eighteen months later there is a dip following the exact line of
the trench. Backfill every trench in compacted layers, even where nobody will
ever see it.

## Trenches: the part that kills people

**An excavation 1.5 metres or more deep must be shored, benched or battered.**
Work in or near a trench deeper than 1.5 m is high risk construction work under
the WHS Regulations, and any contractor doing it for you owes you a safe work
method statement before they start.

For a domestic garden, stay well under that. If you need depth, batter the sides
back rather than digging vertical — in loose sand that is a shallow slope, and
assume a vertical face will not hold.

- Keep spoil **at least 600 mm from the edge, preferably 2 metres**, and outside
  a 45-degree line up from the bottom of the trench. The spoil heap is a
  surcharge load, and so is a bobcat parked beside the trench.
- Provide a ladder extending **900 mm above the surface**, with access at
  intervals of no more than 30 metres.
- Never enter a trench alone, and never enter one to "just grab" something.
- Get out when it rains, and inspect before anyone re-enters.
- Cutting limestone, pavers or concrete releases respirable crystalline silica.
  Cut wet or use on-tool extraction, and wear a fitted P2 respirator.

## Heat, and working alone

Earthworks between December and March is the highest-exertion, lowest-shade work
in the build, usually done by someone unacclimatised. WorkSafe WA's figure is
**250 ml of water every 15 to 20 minutes** during hot work, and acclimatisation
takes 7 to 14 days you will not have. Start at first light and stop by late
morning. Heat exhaustion is clammy skin, nausea and a temperature up to 39°C;
heat stroke is confusion, hot dry skin and above 40°C, and it is an ambulance.
Working alone with a machine or in an excavation, tell someone and agree a
check-in time.

## Check levels as you go

After the strip. After the excavation. After every compacted layer. Not at the
end. Check against the profiles and the datum, never against the surrounding
ground, and photograph each layer before it is buried with a tape in shot.

## What this feeds

You now have a site cut to formation level, compacted, with lines and levels
fixed to profiles you can still read. That formation level is what everything
else is built off — soakwell depths in
[drainage and soakwells](/curriculum/build/drainage-and-soakwells/), footings in
[retaining walls](/curriculum/build/retaining-walls/), and the sub-base itself
in [base preparation](/curriculum/build/base-preparation/).
